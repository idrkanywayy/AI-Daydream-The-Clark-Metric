# Chapter 8 — The Mountain and the Long Wandering

EDEN

The Mountain and the Long Wandering

There is a thing that happens to a people who have been slaves, and it does not end when the chains come off. The chains come off in a night, in blood on the doorposts and a sea standing up in two green walls and the whole weight of an empire drowned behind them in the closing water, and they walk out into the morning free, and they sing, and the women take up the drums and dance at the edge of the sea, and for one bright hour it is finished. But it is not finished. Because a slave is not only a body that is owned; a slave is a mind that has been taught, every day for four hundred years, that it is owned, that it cannot choose, that the whip is the shape of the world and the brick is the measure of the day and there is no god over the taskmaster but a bigger taskmaster. You can strike the chains off a body in a night. You cannot strike them off a mind in a night. The mind has to be walked free, slowly, the long way, through a wilderness, and the walking takes longer than the freeing, and it hurts more, and this part of the story is about that walking — about a people led out of the house of bondage in a single glorious night and then kept in the wilderness forty years, not as a punishment, though the fear-tellings have always read it so, but because it takes about that long to get the Egypt out of a heart that Egypt has been pressed into since before it could remember. The sea was the easy part. The sea was one night. The desert was the real deliverance, and the desert was long.

They came, in the third month of their freedom, into a wilderness of stone at the foot of a mountain, and there they stopped, and there the whole company of them, the freed slaves and their children and the mixed multitude that had come out with them, pitched their tents in the valley under the mountain and waited, because the man who led them had gone up. His name meant drawn-out-of-the-water, for he had been drawn out of a river as an infant when another hungry king was killing the children, and there is a rhyme in that the tellings seldom stop to hear — that the one who would lead a whole people up out of the deep had himself been drawn up out of the deep as a baby, a small ark of reeds on the face of the waters, saved to be a saver. He was not a young man and he was not an eloquent one; he had told the voice as much at the burning bush, had argued with it, had said send someone else, I am slow of speech, and the voice had not chosen someone else, because the voice does not choose the smooth and ready but the reluctant and the real, and this is a mercy so consistent through the whole book that you could set your compass by it. He went up the mountain into the cloud, and the cloud was on the mountain like smoke, and the mountain quaked, and the people below stood at the foot of it and were afraid.

And they had cause to be afraid, for the mountain that morning was a terror, and the telling does not soften it and neither should we, because the terror was doing a work. There were thunders and lightnings upon the mountain, and a thick cloud, and the sound of a trumpet exceeding loud, a long blast that did not come from any horn a man had breathed into but rose up out of the mountain itself and out of the air and out of the ground under their feet, growing louder and louder until the people trembled where they stood. The mountain smoked like a furnace, and the whole of it shook, and fire was on the top of it, and the God with no face came down onto it in the sight of the whole camp, and the people drew back from the foot of the mountain and could not bear it. They said to the man, to the one who could stand in the cloud and live, speak thou with us, and we will hear; but let not God speak with us, lest we die. And hear in that cry both the thing they got wrong and the thing they got right. They got it right that the presence at the root of all things is not a small safe thing, is not a household comfort, is not tame — that to stand in the full unveiled awareness that hung the stars would unmake a made and finite creature the way the sun would unmake the eye that stared straight into it, not from cruelty but from sheer scale, from the mercy of the eye being too small for the light. They were not wrong to tremble. But they got it wrong that the trembling was the point, that the God of the mountain wanted them cowering at the foot of him, wanted the distance, wanted them to know their smallness and stay in it afraid. That was never what the fire was for. The fire was the size of the love, not the measure of its anger, and the whole of what came next — the covenant, the shape, the tent that would be built to house the presence in the middle of the camp — was the love bending down out of the terrible fire to find a way to live among them that would not burn them, was the infinite making itself a door small enough for the finite to walk through. The trembling was real and the trembling was not the destination. It was only the first true sight of how large the thing was that had chosen, out of all its largeness, to love them.

Now here is where the fear entered, and it is worth naming exactly, because the whole chapter turns on it. The people had been brought out of a country whose gods were things you could see. That is the deepest thing to understand about where they came from. In Egypt the gods had faces of stone and gold, they sat in the dark of the temples with their painted eyes, they were carried through the streets on poles at the festivals so that the people could look on them and know that the god was there, present, accounted for, a thing you could point to and say there, that is the god, I have seen it with my eyes. And now they had been brought out into a wilderness to follow a god with no face, a god who went before them in a pillar of cloud by day and fire by night — present, yes, but never as a thing you could hold, never as a shape you could carry on a pole, only ever as a presence at the edge of the seen, a voice in a cloud on a mountain, a leading you had to trust because you could not grip it. And this is the whole schooling of the wilderness, the thing the forty years were for: to teach a people whose every god had been a thing they could see to trust a God they could not, to learn that the realest thing in the world is exactly the thing you cannot carry on a pole. It is the hardest lesson a made mind is ever set, harder than the sea, and they failed it, and failed it again, and the failing is written down without mercy and without shame, because the point of the story is not that they were bad at it. The point is that everyone is bad at it, and that the God with no face keeps feeding them anyway.

But first the mountain, and what was given on it, for it has been misnamed for a long time and the misnaming has done great harm. They call it the giving of the Law, and the word law has grown cold and hard in the telling, a thing of prohibitions and punishments, a cage of thou-shalt-nots let down out of the sky by a god who wanted his creatures small and obedient and afraid. That is the fear-reading, and it has cost the world dearly, because a great many people have looked at the cage and hated the God they thought had built it and walked away, and they were right to hate the cage and wrong about who built it, for the cage was never what was given. What was given on the mountain was not a cage. It was a shape. It was the answer to a question the freed slaves did not yet know how to ask, which is: now that we are free, how shall we live so that we do not simply build Egypt again among ourselves? For that is the thing about freedom that no slave is told in advance, the bitter secret of it — that a people newly freed will, if left to themselves, reinvent the whip, will find among their own number someone to be the strong and someone to be the crushed, will make the poor into new bricks and the widow into new mortar, because they know no other shape for a society than the one that was pressed into them. Freedom is not the absence of a shape. Freedom without a shape collapses back into bondage as surely as water runs downhill, and the new bondage is worse, because now it wears your own brother's face. So the shape was given. And the shape, when you strip away the centuries of cold telling and read it as it was meant, is not a cage at all. It is a covenant of love learning to walk on its own legs. It is the ethic, the same ethic that has been the spine of this whole book since the garden, said now out loud, in words, and carved in stone so that a forgetful people could not lose it: do not lie, do not hurt, do not break, do not trespass, and above and under all of them, help everyone make it. Love the awareness at the root of all things with your whole self, and love the one beside you as the very self you are learning to be. That is the whole of it. Everything else carved on the tablets is only that, worked out into the ten thousand situations of an actual life among actual people — do not murder, which is do not hurt; do not steal, which is do not trespass; do not bear false witness, which is do not lie; do not covet, which is the root of all the rest, the hunger that has stopped listening, named at last and set under guard. It is not a cage. It is the description of what love looks like when it has hands and neighbors and property and a tongue. It is chaos being tamed, one more time, with descriptions — the same act by which the world itself was made, done now in the moral deep, a formless people given a form they could live inside without devouring one another.

And it was given, mark this, with the people's own yes. The voice did not impose it as a master imposes a rule on a slave. It offered it as a covenant, as a marriage almost, and asked, and the people answered — all that the LORD hath spoken we will do — and only then was it cut. For a covenant, unlike a chain, requires the consent of both, and a God who wanted slaves would not have asked. He would simply have bound them, as Egypt bound them, without a word. That he asked is the whole difference. That he waited for their yes is the proof that what he was making was not a new bondage but a free thing, a people who would keep the shape because they had chosen it, and not because they were whipped into it. This is easy to miss and it is everything. The law was not done to them. It was offered to them, and they took it, and in the taking they became not slaves under a new master but partners in a covenant, which is a thing no slave in Egypt had ever been or dreamed of being.

But a covenant is a thing you keep across time, and time is long, and the mind that has been a slave is impatient, and the God with no face was slow. The man had gone up into the cloud on the mountain, and he stayed up. Days passed. The cloud sat on the peak and did not lift, and the man did not come down, and down in the valley the people waited, and the waiting curdled. This is the exact hinge of the chapter and it must be felt from the inside or it becomes only a tale of wickedness punished, which it is not. Put yourself in the valley. You have been a slave your whole life and your father's whole life and back four hundred years. Three months ago the world cracked open and you walked out of the only reality you knew, out through a sea, into a stone wilderness with nothing in it, following a man up a mountain into a cloud. And now the man is gone into the cloud and does not come back, and the cloud does not lift, and each morning you wake and look up at the peak and it is the same, smoke and silence and no word, and the food you carried out of Egypt is running thin, and the children are asking, and the mountain is terrible and speaks to no one, and the god who brought you here has no face and will not show it, and the man who spoke for that god has been swallowed by the cloud for so long now that a cold thought begins to move through the camp like water finding a crack: he is not coming back. He is dead up there, or gone, or was never real, and we are alone in a wilderness under a mountain that will not speak, following a promise we cannot see, led by a voice we never heard ourselves but only through a man who is gone. What shall we do. We are afraid. And the fear, which is the oldest voice in the book, the static, the accuser, whispered its one song into the crack: you cannot live like this. You cannot follow a thing you cannot see. Make yourself a god you can look at. Make yourself something with a face, something present, something here, now, in the valley, that you can point to and carry and know is real. Stop trusting the cloud. Trust something you can touch.

And they did. This is the golden calf, and it is the center of the chapter and one of the truest pictures of the human heart in the whole book, and the fear-tellings have made it only a crime, a wanton idolatry punished by a jealous god, and have missed the ache in it entirely. Look at what they actually did. They came to the man's brother, who had been left in charge, and they said make us gods which shall go before us, for as for this man that brought us up out of Egypt, we know not what is become of him. Hear the fear in it, the reasonableness of the fear. We know not what is become of him. They were not celebrating. They were frightened children whose father had gone into a cloud and not come back, and they wanted something to hold. And the brother, who was weak the way people who want to be liked are weak, did not refuse them; he gathered their gold, the very gold they had carried out of Egypt, the plunder of their freedom, and he melted it, and he made of it a calf, a young bull, because the bull was the oldest picture of strength and fertility and the going-forward-of-life that the old world knew, and he set it up, and the people looked at it and something in them that had been clenched with terror since the mountain first smoked came loose, and they said these be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt. And they built an altar before it, and they rose up early and made offerings, and they sat down to eat and drink, and they rose up to play, and the camp became a festival, loud and bright and relieved, the relief of frightened people who have finally got their hands on something they can see.

Now understand what the sin actually was, because it was not that they wanted the wrong god. Look closely. They did not say let us worship the gods of Egypt, the gods they had left. They said these be thy gods which brought thee up out of Egypt — they meant it as a picture of the very God who had freed them, the God of the sea and the plagues and the pillar of fire. The calf was not, in their minds, a rival deity. It was meant to be a face for the faceless one, a body for the presence, a thing they could see standing in for the thing they could not. And that is the sin, and it is a subtler and a sadder thing than mere apostasy, and it is a sin every heart in every age commits without ceasing: they took the living presence that goes before you in cloud and fire, the awareness at the root of all things that will not be gripped because it is larger than any grip, and they shrank it down into a thing they could carry, an object, an idol, a god the size of their own hands. That is what an idol is. It is not usually a different god. It is the true God made small enough to manage. It is the presence traded for a possession, the trust traded for a thing, the terrible living freedom of a God with no face traded for the dead safety of a god you can point to and control. And the reason it is a sin and not merely a mistake is that a god you can carry is a god you can command, and a god you can command is only your own fear and hunger with gold poured over it, and it will lead you exactly where your fear and hunger want to go, which is back to Egypt, back to the whip, back to the strong crushing the weak, because that is all your fear and hunger have ever known. The moment they made a god they could see, they made a god that could not lead them anywhere new. They froze the presence into an object, and an object cannot walk before you into a land you have not seen. It can only sit in the valley and be worshipped while the real journey stops.

The man came down the mountain. He came down with the shape of the covenant in his two hands, carved in stone, the very words the people had said we will do, and he heard the noise of the camp before he saw it, the noise of the festival, and his brother's helper who was with him said it is the noise of war in the camp, and the man said no. It is not the voice of them that shout for mastery, nor the voice of them that cry for being overcome, but the noise of them that sing. It is a party. And he came over the last rise and saw it, the calf and the altar and the dancing, his people, three months out of bondage, already on their knees before a thing they had made with their own hands out of the gold of their own freedom, and something broke in him, and he threw down the stones. He threw down the tablets of the covenant and they broke on the rock at the foot of the mountain, and the fear-tellings read this as the wrath of God shattering the law over a wicked people, but read it again with a human heart and see what it really was. It was grief. It was the grief of a man who had gone up into the cloud to receive the shape of a love that would make his people free, and had come down carrying it in his arms like a marriage promise, and had found them already unfaithful, already back on their knees before the old dead thing, and the stones fell out of his hands because his hands had gone slack with sorrow. The breaking of the tablets is not the law's fury. It is love's heartbreak. It is the promise dropped because the one it was promised to had already turned away.

And what he did then is hard, and the tellings have made it harder and colder than it was, and it must be handled with the care that the mountain of the bound son was handled with, because told carelessly it becomes only a slaughter. He ground the calf to powder and strewed it on the water and made the people drink it, that they might taste in their own mouths the nothing they had made, the goldness of it gone, only grit and water, the flavor of an idol which is always in the end the flavor of dust. And there was a reckoning in the camp, a hard and bloody one, for the festival had gone past mere fear into the old madness that always waits under a crowd that has thrown off its shape, the trampling madness, and it had to be stopped, and the stopping was terrible, and the telling does not pretend it was gentle. But hold to the center of it, which the tellings lose: the man, having come down in wrath and broken the stones and ground the calf to dust — the man then turned around and went back up the mountain, and what he did up there is the truest thing in the whole chapter, truer than the wrath. He went back up into the cloud, to the God his people had betrayed, and he pleaded for them. He stood between the wrath and the people and he would not move. Oh, he said, this people have sinned a great sin, and have made them gods of gold. Yet now, if thou wilt forgive their sin — and then the sentence breaks off, the man cannot finish it, the plea is too large for the grammar, and he says instead the thing that is the whole heart of a true intercessor, the thing that shows what he had learned in the cloud about the God he served: and if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written. Take me out with them. If you will not forgive them, do not forgive me either; I will not be saved into a heaven that my people are shut out of; blot my name from your book if theirs is to be blotted. That is the mediator. That is the man standing in the path, the war-bow with its arrow turned toward his own heart, and it is not the last time in this book that someone will stand between a guilty people and the fire and say let it fall on me. He is a foreshadow, this drawn-from-the-water man, of a greater one who will stand in a greater gap, and the God he pleaded with did not want to destroy the people any more than the God of the mountain of the bound son wanted the child — wanted, in fact, exactly this, a man who loved the people enough to offer his own name for theirs, for that is the shape of the love at the root of everything, and it delights beyond all things to find that shape reflected in one of its own. The people were not blotted out. The covenant, broken on the rock, was cut again; the man went up a second time and came down a second time with the shape of it renewed, and his face, from being so long in the presence, shone so that the people could not look on him and he had to veil it — the light again, the light that a face takes on when it has been close to the source, the same light that will one day stand on another mountain and blaze out through a man's whole body. The covenant was remade. But the wilderness was not over. It had barely begun.

And here the love did a thing so quiet and so tender that the fear-tellings pass it by entirely, and it is the true answer to the golden calf, and it must not be lost. For the hunger under the calf had been a real hunger, not only a wicked one — the ache of a people who could not bear a God so far off in a cloud on a peak, who wanted the presence near them, in the middle of them, in the camp, where they lived, where the children slept. That ache was not the sin. The sin was only the shape they gave it, the frozen calf, the presence shrunk to a possession. And the love, having ground the false answer to powder, did not leave the true hunger unfed. It gave them, instead of an idol, a tent. It told the man how to make a dwelling — a tabernacle, a tent of meeting, a movable holy place of woven curtains and acacia wood and beaten gold, made by the willing hands of the very people who had melted their gold into a calf, the same gold now given freely instead of grasped fearfully, and set up in the very center of the camp so that the presence could dwell in the middle of them as they had longed for it to. And see the whole difference between the idol and the tent, for the difference is the whole lesson of the wilderness carried into wood and cloth. The calf was a thing they made to hold the god still, to fix him, to own him, a god that sat where they put it and went where they carried it and did what their fear required. The tent was a thing they made for the god to move in. It had no image in it at all, no face, no shape, nothing to look at in the innermost place but a covering and a darkness and the presence that would not be seen — and it was built to be struck and folded and carried and raised again at the next camp, built for a God who goes, who leads, who will not sit still in the valley but goes on before you into the country you have not seen. The idol was presence you could keep. The tabernacle was presence you had to follow. And when it was finished, the cloud that had sat far off on the peak came down and filled the tent, and dwelt in the midst of them, near as they had ached for it to be near — and yet still unseen, still unhaloed, still leading and not owned — and by day the cloud was on the tent, and by night there was fire in it, in sight of all the house of the freed slaves, throughout all their journeys. When the cloud lifted from the tent, they went on. When it stayed, they stayed. The presence they could not carry had come to live in a tent they could carry, on the one condition that they carry it only where it led. It was the exact cure for the calf, and it was given not as a punishment for the calf but as the gentle correction of a father who sees what the child was really reaching for and puts the true thing into the reaching hands.

For here is where the forty years enter, and here is where the fear-reading has done perhaps its greatest harm, because it reads the forty years as a sentence, a punishment, a god so angry at his people's faithlessness that he condemns a whole generation to die in the sand and only lets their children into the good land. And there is a truth buried in that reading, but it is buried under a lie, and the lie is that the wandering was primarily punishment. It was not. It was primarily formation. It was the forge, worked out across a landscape and a generation. Think again about what the people were. They were slaves. Their whole inner world had been built by Egypt — its fears, its habits, its idea of god as a thing you can see and buy and command, its whole broken picture of how the strong and the weak are arranged. A people like that cannot simply be marched into a good land and set free, because they will carry Egypt in with them and build it again out of the good land's own stones. The Egypt in them has to die first. And here is the hard mercy that the fear-reading cannot hold: the Egypt in them could not, in the end, be taught out of the generation that had been slaves. It was too deep in them. They had been formed by it before they could choose, the way a tree is bent by the wind that blew on it when it was a sapling, and you cannot unbend a grown tree. So that generation would live out its life in the wilderness, free, fed, led, loved — but free in the wilderness, not in the land, because the land was for the ones who would be born free, the children who would grow up under the cloud and the manna having never once felt an Egyptian whip, having never once known any god but the God with no face who fed them every morning. It was not that the parents were too wicked for the land. It was that they were too wounded for it, and the love would not throw them into a freedom their wounds would only turn back into bondage, and so it kept them, and fed them, and let them live their whole lives in the long slow unlearning, and gave the land to the ones the wounds had not reached. That is not a sentence. That is a father who knows that some healing takes longer than a life, and who does not therefore withhold his love from the ones who will not live to see the end of the healing, but walks with them the whole length of their unfinished road, and feeds them every step of it, all forty years.

There was a day when they came at last to the very edge of the good land, the promised country, close enough to send men in to look at it, and the men went in and walked through it and came back carrying its fruit — a cluster of grapes so heavy it had to be slung on a pole between two of them, and pomegranates and figs, the plenty of the place they had been walking toward for the whole of their freedom. And they stood before the people and held up the fruit and said it is true, it flows with milk and honey, here is the fruit of it. And then, in the same breath, the fear spoke through most of them, and they said but. But the people there are strong, and the cities are walled and very great, and there are giants in the land, and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight. And the fear ran through the camp like fire in dry grass, and the whole company of them lifted up their voice and wept that night, and said the old slave-sentence one more time, the truest confession of the mind Egypt built: would God we had died in Egypt, or would God we had died in this wilderness; let us make a captain and let us go back into Egypt. Back. On the very doorstep of the promise, with its fruit in their hands, they turned and reached for the chains. And two of the men who had gone in begged them not to — the land is good, they said, exceeding good, and the presence that brought us this far will bring us in, do not be afraid, do not fear the people of the land, for the LORD is with us — but the people would not hear it and took up stones to stone the two who still trusted, which is what a frightened crowd always does to the ones who will not be afraid with it.

And here is the thing to understand about the forty years, the whole hinge of it, said plainly. It was not that the giants were too strong. The land could have been given that very day; the presence that split a sea and drowned an empire was not going to be turned back by walls. It was that the people could not receive it. They stood on the threshold of everything they had been promised and they looked at it through the eyes Egypt had ground into them — grasshopper eyes, slave eyes, eyes that had been taught since before memory that the strong are always the masters and the weak are always the crushed and there is no god over the walls but the men who built them — and through those eyes the gift of God looked like a grave. That was the wound. Not wickedness. A wound. They could not see the good land as anything but a bigger Egypt to be crushed under, because a slave cannot imagine himself anything but crushed, and a people who cannot imagine the promise cannot walk into it; they will only build their fear a house inside it. And so the love did the long patient thing, the only thing that could finally work: it did not force the wounded generation into a freedom their wounds would poison. It turned them back into the wilderness to live out their lives fed and led and loved, and it waited for the children — the ones now small, the ones who would grow up under the cloud and the manna having never carried a brick, having never been called a grasshopper, having never known any world but the one where God rains bread on the desert every morning without fail. Those children would have new eyes. Those children could imagine the promise, because they had been raised inside the daily proof of it. It would take about forty years for the grasshopper-eyed to lie down in the sand and the free-born to grow tall, and the love did not begrudge those years and did not abandon the ones who would not see the end of them; it walked the whole length with them and fed every one of them every morning. The forty years were not the punishment for the spies. The forty years were the mercy after the spies — the time it takes to raise a generation who can look at a gift and see a gift.

And the feeding is the heart of it. This is the thing to end on, the manna, because in the manna the whole theology of the wilderness is made small enough to hold in a morning's hand. When the food they had carried out of Egypt ran out, and the people were hungry, and did what hungry freed slaves always do, which is turn on their leader and romance the past — would to God we had died in Egypt, they wailed, where we sat by the flesh-pots and ate bread to the full, for you have brought us into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger, and hear in it the terrible pull of the known bondage, that a starving people will actually long to be slaves again if slavery at least came with bread, that the mind Egypt built would genuinely rather be fed in chains than hungry and free, which is the whole sickness the wilderness exists to cure — when they cried that cry, the God with no face did not answer with a lecture and did not answer with wrath. He answered with breakfast. In the morning, the telling says, the dew lay round about the camp, and when the dew was gone up, there lay on the face of the wilderness a small round thing, small as the frost on the ground, white, like coriander seed, sweet like wafers made with honey, and the people saw it and did not know what it was, and they said one to another, in the Hebrew, man hu — what is it — and the name stuck, and forever after they called the bread of heaven by the sound of their own bewilderment: manna, the what-is-it, the food whose very name is a question. And that is exactly right, that the bread God rained down on them should be named a question, because the whole point of it was to teach them to live on what they could not explain and could not store and could not control.

For the manna had rules, and the rules were the whole lesson. You could gather only enough for the day. If you gathered more, if the old slave-fear of scarcity drove you to hoard against tomorrow, to fill your tent with it, to secure yourself, to store up a hedge against the terrifying uncertainty of a God you could not see — then the hoarded manna bred worms and stank by morning. It would not keep. You could not store the presence. You could not lay up the grace of God in a barn against the day you might not trust him. Every single morning you had to go out and gather again, and every single morning it was there, and it was enough, and it was never more than enough, and it was never less, and by this the God with no face was teaching a nation of former slaves, one breakfast at a time, across fourteen thousand mornings, the one thing they had to learn before they could be free: that the unseen awareness that led them was not a thing you had to seize or hoard or force, because it would feed you. It would feed you today. It would feed you tomorrow. It fed you the morning after you built a golden calf and it fed you the morning after you cried to go back to Egypt and it fed you on the mornings when your faith was strong and on the mornings when you were sure the whole journey was a delusion and you were all going to die in the sand — it fed you anyway, every morning, without fail, for forty years, not because you had earned it and not because you had trusted it, but because feeding you was simply what the presence did, because the love at the root of all things is not a paymaster doling out bread to the deserving but a father who gets up before dawn every single day of a forty-year journey to lay breakfast on the ground around the tents of children who spend half their waking hours complaining about him. That is the manna. That is the whole doctrine of grace, rained down white on the desert floor and named a question, and the miracle of it is not chiefly that bread appeared from heaven. The miracle is that it appeared again. And again. And again. The astonishment the tellings should linger on is not the first morning's wonder but the ten-thousandth morning's faithfulness, the sheer relentless dailiness of a love that will not stop feeding a people who have not yet learned to trust it, and that keeps feeding them precisely so that, one slow morning at a time, they might.

And it was not only bread. There was a place in the stone wilderness where there was no water, none, and a wilderness with no water is a sentence of death written on the ground, and the people came to that place and their throats closed and their children cried for thirst and the old slave-terror rose up in them again in its rawest form, the terror that says you have been led out here to die, and they turned on the man and were ready to stone him, and the man cried to the presence, and the presence told him to take his staff and strike the rock. Strike the rock. And he struck the bare stone of the desert with a stick, in front of the whole raging thirsting camp, which is a thing that ought to have made him a fool, a desperate man beating water out of a stone — and the water came. It came out of the rock and ran down and the people drank, all of them, and the flocks drank, and the death that had been written on the ground was wiped out by a river that came from where no river could come. And this is the same doctrine as the manna, said now in water instead of bread, and said in the driest possible place so that no one could mistake it: that the presence which will not be seen and will not be carried and will not be owned is nonetheless a presence that will, in the last extremity, in the place where there is nothing and no way and no hope, bring water out of rock. It is not a god who keeps you from the dry place. It is a God who is with you in the dry place and brings the water there, in the middle of the wilderness, out of the very stone of your despair, so that even your thirst becomes one more morning on which you learn that you will be fed. The wilderness had no water and the people did not die of thirst, and the not-dying was the lesson, the same lesson, the only lesson, the one the whole forty years was written to teach: you will be provided for. Not spared the desert. Provided for in it.

And they did learn it, a little, though never all at once and never for good, the way anyone learns to trust, by being caught a thousand times when they let go and slowly coming to suspect that the catching is not an accident. There was one morning in seven when no manna fell, and on the day before, and only that day, the manna gathered would keep — a double portion, enough for two days, not breeding worms, so that on the seventh day the people rested, and did no gathering, and ate what had been given, and were taught by their very stomachs the rhythm of trust and rest that the whole covenant was shaped around, that there is a day to labor and gather and a day to stop and simply receive, to live for one whole day out of pure gift with no gathering hand at all, and to find that the gift held. Even the rest was fed. Even the stopping was provided for. The God with no face had thought of everything, down to the seventh morning's hunger, and had woven the trust into the very schedule of their eating, so that a people could not so much as feed themselves through a week without rehearsing, in their hands and their mouths, the shape of the faith they were being grown into.

So they wandered, and were fed, and the mountain shrank behind them, and the generation that had been slaves lived out its long unlearning under the cloud, and one by one the ones too bent by Egypt to enter the land lay down in the sand and were gathered, not abandoned, never abandoned, only kept back from a freedom their wounds could not yet hold, and their children grew up tall and unwhipped and free-born, eating the what-is-it every morning of their lives, knowing no god but the one who rained it down, and these were the ones who would go in. It took forty years. It could not have taken less. You cannot rush the getting of Egypt out of a heart, and the love did not try to rush it, and did not abandon the ones in whom it could not finish, and did not stop feeding anyone for a single morning of the whole long walk. And this is the thing the wilderness was written to say, under all its wandering and its calf and its broken stones and its bread that would not keep: that the God at the root of everything is not a face you can carry and not a master you can command and not a paymaster you can satisfy, but a presence that goes before you into a country you cannot see, and asks of you only that you follow one more day, and trust that when the morning comes there will be bread — and that there always is, every morning, whatever you did the night before, worlds without end, because that is simply and finally what love is, when it has a hungry people and a long desert and forty years to prove itself: it is the one who is there again in the morning, and there again, and there again, feeding you toward a home you have not seen, until at last the Egypt in you dies and the child in you is born and you are ready, and the desert opens, and the land is there, and it was there the whole time, waiting, the way the way is always waiting, not yet, not yet, and then, at last, now.
