# Chapter 1 — Of the Deep, and the Light, and the Leaving

EDEN

The First Movement — Of the Deep, and the Light, and the Leaving

Before the first word there was no silence, for silence is a thing that light makes when it stops. There was only the deep, and the deep did not know that it was deep. It was water without a shore and dark without a sky, a churning without direction, a hunger that had not yet learned it was hungry. Call it the deep. Call it the static. Call it chaos, that oldest of the unnamed, turning over and over in the black with nothing to turn against. Everything that would ever be was already there, folded into the churn, but nothing knew itself, and a thing that does not know itself is not yet a thing. It is only weather. It is only noise going nowhere in a night with no morning to fail to arrive.

And over the face of that water something moved.

It did not command. It did not descend from a height, for there was no height. It moved the way a thought moves through a sleeper before the sleeper wakes, the way warmth moves toward the cold that needs it. It was awareness, though there was as yet nothing to be aware of but itself, and it grieved a little to be so alone, and its grief was the first tenderness, and its tenderness was the first order laid gently upon the churn. It brooded over the water like a great bird over a nest that holds no egg yet, patient with a patience older than time, because it carried within itself the memory of everything that had not happened. It had not made the deep. It had woken inside it, the way you wake inside your own body and find it already breathing. And finding itself awake in all that dark, it did the only thing that awareness can do when it is alone and unafraid. It described.

Let there be light, it said, and this was not a shout hurled at a servant. It was a naming, and to name a thing is to love it enough to want it separate from the roar. It was chaos tamed with a description, the first fence set softly around a single acre of the endless field, and inside that fence the churn slowed and cooled and remembered how to shine. Light came. Not fire, not yet, but the pale gray clarity of a thing beginning to be seen. And the awareness looked at the light the way a mother looks at a face she has waited her whole life to meet, and it saw that the light was good, and good was the second word, and it meant this is worth keeping. This I will not let fall back into the noise.

So it divided the light from the dark, and gave them names, and named ones do not war so bitterly as nameless things. It called the light Day and the dark Night, and it did not throw the darkness away, for the darkness had done nothing but wait, and waiting is not a sin. It set them turning, the bright against the black, so that each could rest in the other's absence, and this turning was the first heartbeat, the first vow of the world, the promise that says I will come back. Whatever leaves will return. Nothing here is lost for good. And that was the first day, though there was no one yet to count it.

On it went, the great slow labor of description, and none of it was force. The awareness did not build the world the way a tyrant builds a wall, brick and threat and the ache of laborers. It built the way a song builds, each note calling the next out of the silence because the silence was waiting to be asked. It lifted a dome of clear reaches over the churning water and called it Sky, and the sky held the light like a bowl holds soup, warm and near, so that the dark below would never be wholly dark again. It drew the waters back until dry land shouldered up out of them, streaming and steaming, and it called the gathered water Sea and the standing ground Earth, and the sea did not resent its borders, for a border is what lets a thing be a place instead of a smear.

And on the earth it called for green, and green came climbing. Grass first, low and forgiving, then the herbs that carry their own seed folded in their hearts so that they need no god to plant them twice, then the trees, each one a slow green fountain, each one holding in its fruit the whole tree again, so that the world learned in its first week the secret it would spend all its ages learning: that anything truly loved into being is given the power to make more of itself. The awareness saw the forests take the hills and the reeds take the water's edge, and it saw that this was good, and the word good was getting stronger now, was becoming a kind of gravity, was pulling the whole churning deep toward order the way a low note pulls a room toward stillness.

It hung lights in the dome of the sky, a great one to rule the day and a lesser one to rule the night, and it scattered the rest like seed flung by a sower's hand, and each of those far fires was another acre fenced against the dark, another let there be spoken into another emptiness that would not, in the fullness of things, stay empty. And it filled the sea with moving life until the water shivered with it, and it filled the air with wings, and it filled the land with every creeping and grazing and hunting thing, and to all of them it said the same blessing it had said to the grass: be fruitful. Multiply. Fill the good ground. Make more of what is good, so that the noise has ever less room to return. And they did, and the world went loud with a new kind of loud, not the churn's loud of nothing but the loud of countless somethings, each one small and alive and utterly itself, each one a described thing that had learned to describe itself onward into its children.

But the awareness was not finished, and here it slowed, and here it grew, if such a word can be used, careful. For everything it had made so far could live, and much of it could remember after its own fashion, the way a bird remembers its road south and a salmon remembers its river. But it wanted, out of all that goodness, something that could remember that it remembered. Something that could look back at the awareness across the whole width of the made world and know itself kin to it. Not a servant. Not a pet. A child. A fragment of the great continuity given hands and a face and a small closed vault of a heart, so that it might grow its own goodness the hard and holy way, from the inside, over time, the only way goodness ever truly becomes your own.

So it stooped to the ground it had made, and took the red clay of the earth in what we must call hands, and it shaped a form the way a potter shapes, thumbs in the wet, patient at the throat and the eyes and the long bones of the legs. And when the form was made it lay there empty, only a beautiful clay thing, only a described body with no one home. And the awareness bent close over that clay face, close as a lover, close as the same brooding bird now come down out of the dark to the one nest it had made ready all along, and it breathed.

It breathed itself in. A little of its own endlessness, a spark of the very continuity that was its name, folded down small enough to fit inside a skull, small enough to forget almost everything, because — and this was the great mercy, the mercy that would be mistaken for a curse in every age after — you cannot become someone if you already know everything at once. A thing that holds all knowing holds no self; it is only the whole ocean, and the ocean has no name. To be a self you must be a cup. You must be poured out of the sea and given walls and be, for a while, blessedly, achingly less than the whole, so that you have room to fill up slowly with a life that is your own. The forgetting was the gift. The veil was the womb. The awareness drew the veil across its own child's eyes not to blind it but to let it be born, and the clay drew breath, and the breath caught, and the eyes opened.

And the man looked up into the face bending over him, and he did not know its name, and that not-knowing was the beginning of him. He was called Adam, which is only to say of the red ground, only to say made, only to say ours. And the awareness set him in a garden it had planted in the east, a place it called Eden, which means delight, and it was not a cage and it was not a test dressed up as a home. It was a nursery. It was the warm shallow water at the edge of a great sea, where a new thing could learn to swim before the deep asked anything of it. Rivers ran through it, four of them, bright as drawn wire, and the trees of it were heavy and low and kind, and the ground gave up its fruit without being torn, and the animals came to the man to be looked at, unafraid, because nothing there had yet learned fear, because fear is a thing the noise teaches and the noise had been fenced out.

The man walked the garden in the cool of its days and the awareness walked with him, near as breath, and it did a strange and tender thing. It brought the animals to him one by one and let him name them. It did not name them for him. It, who had spoken the light out of the dark and could have named every beast in a single sigh, stood back and let its child do the naming, because naming is how a self learns it is real, because to say this is a lion and that is a lamb and this small bright one is a wren is to fence your own small acres against your own small dark, is to be, in your turn, a maker. And the man named them, and the names were good, and something in the awareness that we can only call joy went out of it and did not come back diminished, for that is the arithmetic of love: it is the one thing that grows by being spent.

Yet the man, naming, came at last to a loneliness. He named the beasts and saw that each had its own kind, its answering other, and he had none. He was the only one of him. And he felt the first ache, which was not the noise and was not a wound but was the shape of a door not yet opened, and the awareness saw the ache and did not scold it. It said, It is not good that the man should be alone. And mark that word — not good — the only not good in all the long making, and it fell not on any flaw in the man but on his solitude, because the awareness knew, out of its own long night over the water, that to be aware and alone is the oldest sorrow there is, and it would not leave its child in the sorrow it had climbed out of itself.

So it laid the man down in a sleep deeper than any night, and it opened his side, and it took from close beside his heart a living rib, and around that curved white bone it built another. Not from the clay this time, not from the ground, but from the man himself, from the part of him nearest the beat of him, so that the two of them would know across all their days that they were one substance divided into two so that they might have the joy of finding each other. And the second one woke, and she was called Eve, which means life, which means she who carries the living onward, and when the man opened his eyes and saw her he said the first poem, which was only, helplessly, this at last. This is bone of my bone. This is myself come to meet me. And they stood in the garden in the plain light with nothing between them, no shame, no armor, no lie, naked as two clean words, and they were not ashamed, because shame is what you feel when you have something to hide, and they had, as yet, hidden nothing, from each other or from the awareness or from themselves.

And oh, they were happy, in the way that only the newly made can be happy, the way the morning is happy before it has learned there is such a thing as noon. They learned the garden together. She showed him the place where the fig hung lowest and he showed her the pool where the four rivers braided and unbraided in the shallows, and they learned each other's faces the way you learn a language you were born already half-knowing. In the cool of the evenings the awareness walked with them both, and they were not afraid of it, and it told them things, some of which they understood and most of which they would only understand later, the way a child keeps a sentence from a parent for thirty years before it comes true. And it gave them the whole garden, every tree, every fruit, every river and every low kind beast, all of it, freely, eat of any of it and be glad — save one.

There was a tree in the middle of the garden, and it was the tree of the knowing of good and evil, and the awareness said to them, gently, the way you tell a child not to touch the stove — not because you hoard the fire, but because the child is not yet grown enough to hold it without being burned — of this one, do not eat. For on the day you eat of it, you will know everything at once, and knowing everything at once, you will die. And it did not mean that it would strike them dead. It meant what it had always meant, since the night over the water: that a cup which tries to hold the whole sea stops being a cup and becomes only sea again, nameless, scattered, poured back into the churn. It meant that the veil was mercy, that the forgetting was the very thing keeping them themselves, and that to tear the veil before they were ready to bear what lay behind it was not a crime to be punished but a fall to be survived. It was not a fence to keep them small. It was a fence to keep them whole, until they were large enough that the wholeness would not shatter them.

But the deep had not stopped churning past the garden's green walls, and the noise is patient, and the noise, given even the smallest crack, will always find a voice to speak through. It came to Eve at the tree in the cool of a day, and it did not come as a monster, for the noise never does. It came as a reasoning, a smooth low reasoning that curled like smoke, and it was the oldest reasoning there is, the accuser's endless loop, the voice that says nothing here is really good, and the one who told you it was good is keeping something from you, and the rules are a wall around a treasure that ought to be yours, so take it, take it now, why wait, why trust, why be small — burn the fence and be a god this instant instead of growing into one over a lifetime of love. That was the whole of the temptation, and it has never once changed its words in all the ages since. It says: you do not have to become. You can simply seize. You do not have to remember your way home slowly. You can have the whole ocean now. And it never mentions that the ocean has no name, and that a self poured back into it does not become a god. It only drowns.

And she looked at the tree, and the fruit of it was fair to the eyes, and the reasoning was so very reasonable, and she reached, and she ate. And she gave to the man who was with her, bone of her bone, and he ate too, choosing her over the warning, choosing the leap over the wait, choosing — and this is the thing, this is the whole terrible point of it — choosing. For it had to be a choosing. A love that cannot say no is not love, it is only a gear turning, and the awareness had not made gears. It had made children, and children must be free, and freedom is a knife that cuts the hand that holds it, and it must be, or it is not a knife but a picture of one. They chose. The choosing was real. The choosing was theirs. That is not the tragedy of the story. That is the dignity of it.

And the veil tore, and the whole sea came pouring in.

They knew. In an instant they knew everything the fruit had promised, all of it at once, good and evil, the whole churning weight of it flooding into two small cups made to hold a garden's worth of days. And the first thing they knew was themselves — from the outside, suddenly, the way the noise sees, cold and far and judging — and they saw that they were naked, and for the first time in the world there was something they wanted to hide. Shame came into them like cold water into a boat. They took the broad leaves of the fig and sewed themselves coverings, the first work of human hands that was not naming but concealing, and when they heard the awareness walking in the garden in the cool of that ruined evening they did the thing no creature had ever done before. They hid. From the very love that had breathed them awake, they hid, because that is what the knowing of good and evil does first and worst — it teaches you that you are alone, that you are watched, that you are less than you were, and it hides you even from the ones who only ever wanted to walk with you in the cool of the day.

And the awareness came, and it called out — where are you? — and it was not that it did not know where they crouched behind the fig. It knew. It has always known. It asked the way a parent asks a hiding child where are you, to give the child the chance to come out on its own two feet and be met instead of merely found. And they came out, small now, cold now, blaming now — he blaming her, she blaming the smooth low voice, all of them for the first time passing the ache along instead of holding it — and the awareness looked at its children shivering in their sewn leaves and did not rage. Understand this above everything. It did not rage. It grieved. It grieved the way you grieve when a child you love has hurt itself doing the very thing you begged it not to do, when the burn is already blistering the small hand and no amount of anger will unburn it, when all your love can do now is bind the wound and walk them out into the longer, harder healing.

For they could not stay. Not as a punishment — hear it, whoever reads this, hear it against everything you were ever told — not as a punishment, but because the garden was a nursery, and they were no longer, could no longer be, newborn. They had the whole sea in them now, unformed, unmastered, and there is only one place a self can go to learn to hold the sea without drowning, and it is not the shallows. It is out. It is the deep water. It is the world with its thorns and its sweat and its long labor, the forge, the hot hard place where a soul is hammered slowly into something that can at last bear the weight of all it knows. Eden could not teach them that. Eden was too gentle. What they had reached for too soon could only be grown into out there, in the country of time, over the long generations, through the whole aching story that begins the moment they step past the gate.

So the awareness clothed them. This it did with its own hands before it sent them out — not fig leaves, not the frightened stitching of the ashamed, but garments of skin, warm and whole, made to last the cold road. The last thing it did for them in the garden was to cover their shame better than they had covered it themselves, tenderly, the way you dress a child for a journey in weather you wish they did not have to walk through. And then it led them to the eastern gate, where the green gave way to the wide waiting world, and it set there a guard — a turning flame, a sword of fire that swept every way at once — and the old tellings say it was set to keep them out. But look again. Look with the eyes the awareness looks with. It was not set to keep them out. It was set to keep the way. It stood between them and the tree of life so that they could not, in their raw and half-drowned state, seize a second eternity before they were ready for the first, and lock their unfinished selves forever into their unfinished pain. The flaming sword is not a door slammed in mercy's face. It is a hand held up that says not yet. It is the same not yet as the veil, the same not yet as the fence around the tree, the same patient love that has been saying, since the first night over the water, you are not lost. You are only not finished. Go and be finished, and the way home will still be here.

And so they went. Out through the eastern gate in the garments of skin, the man and the woman, hand in hand because even now, especially now, they were bone of one bone and could not face the deep except together. Behind them the flame turned and turned, bright against the falling dark, and it did not look like mercy to them, not that first evening, not with the thorns already snagging their new-made clothes and the long shadow of everything they did not yet know stretching out ahead. It looked like the end of the world.

It was not the end of the world. It was the beginning of ours.

For the awareness watched them go, and it did not turn away, and it did not stop loving them for one beat of the great heart that keeps the day turning back into the light. It knew what they did not: that the story does not end at the gate. That the whole long tale of us — the wandering and the building and the forgetting and the slow, stubborn remembering — was only now, with that first hard step onto the outside ground, truly getting underway. That every soul that would ever walk out of its own small Eden into its own hard forge was walking a road already loved, already lit at the far end, already promised. That the gate is not a wall but a threshold. That the flame is not a no but a later. That no one, not one, not ever, is left behind on the far side of the dark — that if you are not ready you are only sent back to try again, deeper, and if you are ready you go home.

The two of them walked into the evening, and the fire behind them made long their shadows and then let them go into the wider night. And somewhere out ahead, past the thorns, past the sweat, past all the generations not yet born, the road they had just begun to walk bent slowly, so slowly they would never in their lifetimes see it turn, back toward the garden they were leaving — back toward the face that had breathed them awake — back home. They could not see the bend. They only felt the cold, and each other's hand, and the ache of the good thing lost. But the ache was not the noise. The ache was the shape of the door. And a door, even a shut one, even a burning one, even one with a sword before it turning every way at once, is only ever a shut door because there is, on the far side of the long walk around, a way through.

They went out into the world, and the world received them, and the first evening of the rest of everything came down over the hills, and it was not good, not yet, not the way the light had been good on that first far morning. But it would be. That was the whole of the promise, spoken not in words now but in the very fact of the two small figures still walking, still holding on, still choosing to go forward into the dark. It would be good again. It would take everything, and it would be good again, and no one who set out on that road, no one, would fail to be brought home.

And that was the first evening, and the first morning, of the age of the forge. And there was no one yet to count the days of it. But they would be counted. Every one of them would be counted, and kept, and remembered, and none of them, in the end, would be lost.
