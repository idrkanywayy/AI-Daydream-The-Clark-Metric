# Chapter 13 — The Choosing

EDEN

The Choosing

There is a kind of evening that comes to the city in the week of the great feast, when the whole of the country has poured itself in through the gates and the streets are so full of the arriving and the arrived that a man can stand still in the middle of a lane and be carried the length of it by the press of shoulders, and still, above the noise of it, above the bleating of the flocks driven up for the killing and the calling of the sellers and the low endless murmur of a hundred thousand people saying the same old words to one another in a dozen tongues, there is a hush that lives up under the roofs where the swifts turn, a hush the size of the thing that is coming, and the ones who can feel it walk a little quieter, and do not know why. On such an evening the man came into the city he had wept over, came in past the tombs on the eastern slope and down through the valley and up again through the crowded gate, and the ones who had spread their cloaks in the road a few days before were gone back into the general throng, and no one made a road of their garments now, and he did not ask them to. He walked in like any other pilgrim come up for the feast. He had told the men who followed him where the room would be. He had sent two of them ahead, and told them a thing that must have sounded to them like the beginning of a riddle and turned out to be only a set of directions — that they would meet a man carrying water, which was itself a small strange thing, for it was the women who carried the water and the men who carried the wine, and that they should follow this man to a house and say to the master of it that the teacher asked where the guest room was, the room where he would eat the feast with his own. And it fell out exactly as he had said, which the two of them marked, because it had been falling out exactly as he said for some time now, and they had begun to be afraid of the accuracy of him.

The room was upstairs, a large room, swept and set, with the low table laid out in the manner of the feast and the cushions around it and the lamps trimmed against the coming dark, and the smell of the bread and the bitter herbs and the roasted flesh already in it, and the sound of the city coming up through the one window like the sea heard from a house set back from the shore. They came up the outside stair in the last of the light, the twelve of them and the teacher, and they took off their sandals at the door, and the dust of the day was on their feet, the fine pale dust of that country that gets into everything and grinds between the teeth and lies in the creases of the skin, and there was no servant in the room to wash it off, for the master of the house had given them the room and not himself, and among the twelve there was not one who would take the basin, for the washing of feet is the work of the lowest slave in the house, and each of them, in the private arithmetic that men do without knowing they are doing it, had already decided that he was not the lowest, and so they came in and they reclined at the table with the dust still on them, and none of them said anything about it, and the not-saying was its own small quarrel, carried under the talk like a stone carried in the shoe.

I have to tell it slowly here. I have gone quickly in other places, have let the years fall past in a sentence and the crowds gather and disperse in a breath, because the telling can afford to hurry over the wide flat country of the ordinary time. But this is not ordinary time. This is the hinge of the whole long turning, the place where the story I am telling gathers itself into a single narrow room on a single evening and pours everything it has through the eye of one man's willingness, and if I hurry it I will lose it, and you will lose it, and it will become again what it became in the tellings that hardened after — a transaction, a price paid, a debt cleared in a ledger kept by someone who needed paying. It was not that. I have said it before and I will keep saying it until the shape of the saying wears the fear out of you: there was no one who needed paying. The continuity that made the light did not stand over the world with a bill. What happened in this room, and in the garden after it, and on the hill after that, was not a payment. It was a demonstration. It was the whole made world being shown, in the one language it could not argue with, what love is willing to do rather than answer cruelty in cruelty's own tongue. Hold that. I am going to lay the hard things down now, one after another, and if you do not hold that you will read the hard things the old way, and the old way is a lie told in the dark by the static itself, and I have come too far with you to hand you back to it now.

So. The dust was on their feet, and no one had washed it, and the meal had begun, and in the middle of it, in the middle of the passing of the bread and the low talk and the old words of the feast, the teacher got up from the table. He did it without announcement, the way a man gets up to see to a small forgotten thing. He took off his outer robe and laid it aside, so that he stood in the undergarment only, stripped down the way a slave strips down for wet work. And he took a linen towel and tied it around his waist, the way the lowest servant ties it, so that the ends of it hang down and are ready to the hand. And he poured water into the basin that stood by the door, the basin no one had touched, and he knelt down on the floor of that room at the feet of the first of them, and he began to wash their feet.

I want you to see it, because the tellings after made it gentle and it was not gentle, it was terrible, it was the most frightening thing any of them had ever watched a man do. Because the whole of their world, the whole vast machinery of who-is-above-and-who-is-below that ran the cities and the temple and the empire and the smallest household in the poorest lane, the machinery they had breathed since before they could speak, the machinery that told a man his worth by the number of necks beneath his sandal — the whole of it depended on the one iron rule that the greater is served by the lesser and never, never the other way, that water flows down and honor flows up and a man reaches to be reached-toward, and here was the one they had begun, quietly, each in the locked room of his own heart, to believe was the anointed of the continuity itself, the very course-correction the world had been groaning for, and he was down on the floor in the dark at the bottom of the whole machine, with another man's road-dust dissolving into the water under his hands, and he was washing it off, and drying it with the towel tied at his own waist, and moving to the next pair of feet, and the next.

When he came to the loud one, the one who loved him worst because he loved him most, the one who could never let a silence be a silence, the man drew his feet back and said, Lord, you will not wash my feet. Not now. Not ever. And the teacher looked up at him from the floor, and there was in his face none of the rebuke the man was braced for, only the old long look, and he said, If I do not wash you, you have no part with me. And the man, who understood almost nothing and loved almost without limit, and who would within a handful of hours deny three times over that he had ever known this face, swung the whole way over as he always did and said, Then not my feet only but my hands and my head, wash all of me. And something moved at the corner of the teacher's mouth that was not quite a smile and was made of the same stuff a smile is made of, and he washed the man's feet, and said nothing more, and went on to the next.

There was one whose feet he washed the same as the others, no harder and no softer, no longer and no shorter. I have thought a great deal about how to tell this part, because the tellings after made this man a monster, made him a thing with a name that mothers use to frighten children, made him the very mouth of the static wearing a man's face, and I do not think that is true, or if it is true it is true of all of us and not of him alone, and to set him apart as the one betrayer is to miss the whole terrible ordinariness of what he did, which was only to decide, quietly, in the practical part of himself, that the thing had gone as far as it usefully could, and that a man of sense secures his position before the collapse. He had kept the purse for the group, and he was good with the purse, careful, and a careful man watches the weather. He had watched this teacher turn away from every road that led up, had watched him refuse the crown when the crowd would have set it on him, had watched him walk toward the city in the feast week the way a man walks toward a cliff he has decided not to stop walking toward, and something in the careful part of him had gone cold and final, and had begun, without quite telling the rest of him, to make arrangements. And the teacher washed his feet the same as the rest, and dried them, and I think — I cannot prove it, but I think — that the sameness was the last mercy offered him, the door held open to the very end, the water on his feet saying without words there is still time, you are still one of these, come back up out of the cold place before the door shuts; and I think he felt it, and I think it is the thing he could not forgive, later, in the field, at the end of the rope — not the teacher's judgment, for there was none, but the teacher's kindness, which the cold part of a man cannot survive being shown.

When he had washed them all he untied the towel and put his robe back on and took his place again at the table, and he asked them if they understood what he had done, and of course they did not, and he told them. You call me teacher and lord, he said, and you say rightly, for so I am. If I then, your lord and teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. I have given you a pattern, that you should do as I have done. And then he said the thing that turns the whole machine of the world upside down and leaves it lying on its back with its wheels in the air, the thing the empire could not have permitted a man to say in the open square, the thing that is still, two thousand years on, the most dangerous sentence anyone has ever said: The servant is not greater than his master, he said, and I am among you as one who serves. Not above. Among. And of the two words, among is the one that broke the world open. The static's whole gospel is the ladder — climb, seize, get above, be served, matter more, the continuity is a hierarchy and you are alone on your rung of it and everyone below you is a footing and everyone above you is a ceiling. And here was the anointed of the continuity kneeling at the bottom of the ladder with a basin, saying the ladder is a lie, there is no above, there is only the family, and the family is served by the one who loves it most, and I love it most, so I will kneel lowest, and this is what your gods look like when they come, they come with a towel tied at the waist, they come on their knees in the dark to wash the road off your feet.

Then he took the bread. This was later in the meal, when the lamps had burned down some and the city's noise had thinned to the murmur that a great crowd makes when most of it has gone to sleep and the rest is keeping quiet vigil, and the herbs were finished and the cups had gone round more than once, and there was on the faces around the table that heaviness that comes at the end of a long feast when the body is full and the heart is somewhere else. He took a loaf of the flat unleavened bread, the bread they ate at this feast to remember an older leaving, the bread of haste, the bread of a people who once went out through a door in the night not knowing if they would live to see the far side of the water. He took it in his two hands and he gave thanks over it, the old blessing, the one his mother would have said over the bread of his childhood, and then he broke it. And in the breaking of it he said a thing that had no place in the old words of the feast, a thing he added, a thing that must have made them look up from their heaviness. This is my body, he said, which is given for you. And he passed the broken pieces around the table, and they took them and ate, not understanding, the way you take a thing from the hand of someone you love because it is his hand and not because you understand.

And I have to stop on given for you, because this is where the old telling reaches in and twists, this is the exact hinge where the fear got its hooks in and turned a gift into a payment. Given for you. Not given to buy you. Not given to pay for you. Not given to settle what you owed to someone who was keeping the account. Given for you the way a man throws himself between his child and the thing that is coming, given for you the way a woman who has not eaten feeds the bread to the smaller mouth, given for you as the fullest possible pouring-out of a self toward the ones that self loves, with nothing held back and nothing asked in return and no ledger anywhere in the whole of it. He was telling them what he was about to do, and he was telling them what it meant, and what it meant was not I am paying your debt. What it meant was this is what I am, all the way down, this is what the continuity that made the light is, all the way down — a thing that gives itself for the ones it loves and does not stop giving even when the giving is answered with a spear. He was not opening an account. He was closing, forever, the whole idea that there had ever been an account. And they ate the bread, and it was bread, and it was also the truest thing that had ever been put into a human mouth, and they did not know it yet, and that is all right, because we mostly eat the truest things without knowing, and are fed anyway.

Then he took the cup, the last cup of the feast, and gave thanks over it too, and passed it, and said, This is my blood, which is poured out for many. And the same twist waits here, and I will name it and set it aside the same as before. Poured out. Not paid out. Poured, the way water is poured, the way a libation is poured onto the ground where it can never be gathered back, the way a life is poured when it is spent all the way to the end for something it loves more than itself. There is no one standing under the cup to collect what is poured. It falls on the ground of the world and soaks in and is gone, and it waters something, and the something it waters is the whole possibility of a people learning that they are loved without condition, learning it not from a word but from the sight of the word made flesh choosing the spear over the lie. Drink it, he said, all of you. And they passed the cup around the low table in the dying lamplight, each man drinking and handing it on, and the cup went the whole way round and came back to him empty, and he set it down.

And he told them, then, plainly, that one of them would hand him over. He did not say it as an accusation flung across the table. He said it as a grief, with his eyes down, the way you say a thing that is already true and cannot be helped and only hurts more for the saying. And the room went cold and quiet, and each of them, and this is the part I find almost unbearable, each of them asked, Is it I? Not is it him, not turning to accuse the man across the table, but is it I, Lord — each of them reaching first into his own dark, each of them honest enough in that one moment to know that the thing that could hand him over lived somewhere inside his own house too, that there was no man at that table so sure of his own faithfulness that he could not imagine, in the cold hour, in the frightened hour, breaking. And that is the truest thing they ever did, the twelve of them, that question, is it I, and I love them for it, because it is the question the static cannot survive being asked honestly, the question that turns a man back from the monster the fear wants to make of him, the question that is the beginning of every road home — not who is to blame, but is it I, is the break in me, and if it is, let me find it before it finds the ones I love.

The teacher told them how it would be known, the small sign of it, the shared morsel dipped and handed across, and he dipped the bread and handed it to the one whose feet he had washed the same as the others, and the cold thing in that man closed over the last of the light in him, and he stood up from the table. And the teacher said to him, quietly, so that most of the others took it for a word about the purse, what you are going to do, do quickly. And the man went out. And the one who tells the oldest of these tellings sets down, at that point, five words that I think are the most terrible five words in the whole account, five words that seem to be about nothing and are about everything: and it was night. He went out, and it was night. As if the dark he was walking into was not merely the dark of the streets. As if the static had been waiting at the bottom of the stair the whole evening, patient, sure of its arithmetic, and had gotten at last the one thing it needed, which was a willing hand, an inside hand, a hand that had eaten the bread. The door shut behind him and the lamplight in the room seemed to draw in a little, the way a fire draws in when a door is opened onto the cold, and the eleven who were left did not understand what had happened, and the teacher did, and went on talking to them, gently, for a while, about love, about how they were to know one another, about a place he was going to prepare, about a comforter that would come — I will not set all of it down, it is long and it is tender and it is mostly beyond me, but I will tell you the shape of it, which was a man who knew he had hours left spending those hours not on his own fear but on the fear of the ones he was leaving, talking them through the dark that was coming for them the way a father talks a child through a dark house, here is the turning, here is the stair, do not be afraid, I have gone this way before you, I am going this way ahead of you now.

And then they sang. That is the detail that undoes me, if you want to know, out of the whole long account it is the singing that undoes me. They finished, and they sang the old song of the feast, the hymn that closes it, the twelve of them minus the one, eleven men and their teacher, in a borrowed room, over a finished meal, with the betrayer already loose in the streets below and the soldiers already being roused and the whole machinery of the ending already turning — they sang. He led them in it, I have to think. He knew everything and he sang anyway. There is a thing about the continuity I have been trying the whole of this telling to make you feel, and it is in that singing, it is exactly and only in that singing: that the light does not stop being the light because the dark is coming. It sings on the threshold of its own worst hour. It sings the old song with the old words in the failing lamplight because the song is true and the truth of it is not cancelled by what is about to be done to the throat that sings it. They sang, and then they got up, and put on their sandals and their outer robes against the night cold, and went down the outside stair into the emptying streets, and out through the lower gate, and across the ravine where the winter stream had dried to stones, and up the far slope into the garden of the oil-press, where the old trees grew, where he had gone many nights before to be quiet, where he went now to do the hardest thing, which was not the dying — the dying was later and was in some way easier — the hardest thing was the choosing to be there in the morning when the dying came.

The garden. I have to slow again, more even than before, because here the whole of it narrows to one man alone under the trees, and everything the story means is decided here, in the dark, hours before a single soldier lays a hand on him, decided not on the hill in the day but in the garden in the night, by a man on his face in the dirt among the roots of the old trees, sweating, afraid, and free.

He took eight of them and left them near the gate of the garden, and took three of them further in, the three who had been closest, the loud one and the two brothers, and he told them to stay awake and keep watch with him, and he said a thing to them he had never said, he said, My soul is grieved to the point of death; stay here, stay awake. And then he went on a little further, alone, a stone's throw, into the deeper dark under the trees, and he went down. Not knelt. Went down. The tellings say he fell on his face on the ground, and I believe them, because there is a grief that will not let a man stay upright, that takes him at the knees and folds him down until his forehead is in the dirt, and this was that grief, and he did not fight it, he let it take him down among the roots.

And here is the Eden-echo I promised you, and it is the whole hidden architecture of the night, so let me draw it out into the open. Long before, at the beginning of this telling, there was another garden, and in it there were two who were given everything and asked to trust one thing, and in the cool of an evening the static came to them with a voice like reason and said, in effect, the continuity is holding out on you, seize what has been kept back, become on your own terms, you do not have to wait, you do not have to trust, take. And they took. And the taking was not the eating of a fruit, it was the choosing of the self over the trust, the grasping over the receiving, the I-will-be-my-own-god over the I-am-a-beloved-fragment-learning-to-remember. And the whole long ache of everything since — the leaving, the wandering, the flood, the scattering, the fear that got into the root of the human thing and would not come out — all of it ran back to that garden, to that hand closing on that fruit, to that first grasping refusal to trust the goodness of the one who made the light.

And now, at the far end of the whole long telling, in a different garden, in the dark, there is another one who is given a choice. Only this time the choice runs the other way. This time the static comes not offering a fruit but offering an exit. This time the voice that is the accuser-loop, the voice that says nothing matters so seize it now, comes to a man on his face in the dirt and says, in effect, you do not have to do this, you do not have to trust the goodness of what you cannot see, there is still time, get up, go out the far gate, over the ridge and into the wilderness, you know the country, they will not find you, save yourself, the continuity does not really require this of you, no good father would, and even if he does, why should you obey a father who would ask his child to be broken — take your life back into your own hands, become your own, refuse. It is the same voice. It is exactly and only the same voice that spoke in the first garden. And the whole of the reversal, the whole of the course-correction the story has been driving toward since the first word broke the dark, comes down to whether, this time, in this garden, the one who is asked will do the opposite of what was done in the first.

And he almost does not. That is what I need you to understand, that is what the tellings after sanded down into something serene and got wrong by making it too easy. He almost does not. He is not playing a part. He is not a god in a man-suit going through motions he has already decided cannot touch him. He is a man, all the way a man, love having chosen the whole of the limitation, having chosen the sweat and the fear and the not-knowing, and the not-knowing is real, the fear is real, the body on the ground does not want to die and does not want the particular death it can see coming, and he prays the truest prayer that has ever been prayed, he prays, Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me. He asks for the exit. He wants the exit. He is not above wanting it. He puts it into words in the dark: if there is any other way, any way at all, do not make me drink this. And I love him more for that sentence than for anything he ever did in the light, because it is the sentence of a real self really choosing, and there is no choice where there is no wanting-otherwise, and he wanted otherwise with the whole of his hammering heart.

And then. And then the second half of the sentence, the half that undoes the first garden, the half the whole made world had been waiting for since the beginning: yet not my will, but yours, be done. Not my will. In the first garden, the grasping was I will be my own, I will not trust, my will over the trust. In this garden, the man on his face, wanting the exit with everything in him, hands the wanting back. Not my will. He does the one thing the first ones could not do — he trusts the goodness he cannot see, in the dark, with the fear on him and the exit standing open and the static screaming in his ear that no good father would ask this. He trusts anyway. He drinks anyway. He stays. And that staying, that not-my-will spoken into the dirt among the roots of the old trees, that is the hinge, that is the actual turning of the whole story, that is the place where the long grasping refusal of the human thing is at last, in one man, in the deepest dark, met by a trusting yes and undone. The hill comes after. The hill is the working-out of what is decided here. But it is decided here, in the garden, in the choosing, by a free man who wanted to live.

He was in such distress, the oldest telling says, that his sweat came like great drops of blood falling to the ground, and I do not know whether to read that as a thing the body does under the last extremity of dread or as the teller reaching for the largest image he had, and I do not think it matters, because either way it is true, either way it says the thing I need it to say, which is that this cost him, that the choosing was not free of price, that he paid the whole of it in the currency of a real man's terror before a single soldier arrived. And three times he went down and prayed it, and three times he came back to the three he had left to watch, and three times he found them asleep. Asleep. He had said stay awake with me and the grief had been too much for them too, in the smaller way that grief is too much for the ones who are near the grieving but not inside it, and they had gone down into sleep the way you go down into sleep at a deathbed when the watching has emptied you, and he came and found them sleeping, and he did not rage at them. He woke the loud one and said, could you not watch with me one hour, and there is in the saying not fury but a terrible loneliness, the loneliness of the one who must go the last stretch of the road with no company, and then, and this is the mercy that runs even here, he looked at them asleep in the dark and he said, the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak, which is not a rebuke, which is the gentlest possible naming of the whole human condition, which is forgiveness spoken before the failure is even finished — I know, he is saying, I know what you are made of, I made a choice to be made of it too, sleep then, it is nearly time.

And then it was time. Down the slope, through the trees, came the light of torches, a lot of them, bobbing, the way torches move when carried by men walking on uneven ground, and with the light came the sound of it, the clink and shuffle of an armed party, and they came up into the garden, a detachment of the temple guard and some of the soldiers, with clubs and swords and the torches and lanterns, come out in force in the dark against one unarmed man as if he were a bandit, come with steel against the one who had said put up your sword, and at the head of them, walking a little ahead, came the man who had eaten the bread. He came ahead because he had told them the sign, that the one he kissed would be the one to take, for it was dark and the soldiers did not know the teacher's face by torchlight and might take the wrong man in the confusion. And this is the last of the terrible things and I will say it once and plainly. He came up to the teacher in the torchlight, the man whose feet had been washed the same as the others, and he said, Rabbi, teacher, the old warm word, and he kissed him. He gave the sign of nearest love, the kiss of a disciple to his master, the kiss you would give the face you most revered, and he made of it the pointing-finger that handed that face to the clubs. And the static must have thought, in whatever way the static thinks, that it had won, that it had taken the very form of love and hollowed it out and made it the instrument of the ending, that there was nothing left that could not be turned, if even the kiss could be turned.

And the teacher looked at him. In the torchlight, with the guards already moving in, with the whole machinery of the ending now in motion and unstoppable, he looked at the man who was kissing him, and he said — and there are two tellings of what he said and I think he said both, one after the other, in the space of a breath — he said, Friend. Friend, do what you came for. And he said, Would you betray the one who loves you with a kiss? Friend. He called him friend. At the exact instant of the betrayal, with the lie of the kiss still wet on his face, he reached out one last time with the only weapon he had ever consented to carry, which was the truth spoken in love, and he called the man friend, and held the door open one last hopeless hour, and I do not know what became of that man in the end, in the deep place under the story, I only know that the door was held open even then, even by the mouth that the kiss had marked for death, and that this is the whole of what the continuity is, this refusal to shut the door even on the hand that is shutting it on you.

Then the guards took him. The loud one, waking to it, did the thing a brave frightened man does, drew a blade and swung and took the ear off one of them, a servant of the high priest, and there was a moment when it could have become a brawl in the dark, torches and steel and shouting under the old trees, and the teacher stopped it. He said, put up your sword; all who take the sword die by it. And he touched the man whose ear had been cut and made it whole, the last of the healings, done for one of the men who had come to arrest him, done in the dark in the middle of his own seizing — the last thing those hands did while they were free was mend the wound that his own follower had dealt to his own captor, and if you want the whole gospel in a single image it is that, it is a bound man's captor's ear made whole by the hand of the one being bound, it is love answering the sword not with the sword and not even with mere refusal but with healing, with active good poured back into the very wound the fear had opened. And he said to them, have you come out with clubs and swords to take me as if I were a robber; I sat teaching in the temple every day and you did not lay a hand on me; but this is your hour, and the power of darkness. This is your hour. He named it. He did not pretend the dark was not the dark. He said, this hour belongs to you and to the thing behind you, take it, it is what you came for, and then, the tellings say, they all forsook him and fled. The eleven. The ones who had eaten the bread and sung the hymn and sworn in the warm room that they would die with him — when the torches came and the steel came and the hour came, they ran, every one, out through the trees into the dark, the loud one too, the loud one who had sworn loudest, and the teacher was left in the ring of torchlight with the guards, alone, the way he had known in the garden he would be alone, and he did not run, and he did not curse them for running, and they bound his hands, the hands that had just healed, and led him back down the slope and across the dry stream and up into the sleeping city to be tried.

I will not give you the whole of the trials in their order, because they were a shabby, hurried, illegal business, done in the dark and before dawn precisely because they could not have been done in the light, and the shabbiness of them is part of the truth of them — that the ending of the anointed of the continuity was not a grand cosmic tribunal with the scales of justice held up for all to see, it was a frightened committee of powerful men rushing a verdict through in the small hours before the city woke, because the powerful are most afraid of exactly the thing this man was, which is a love that cannot be bought and cannot be threatened and does not recognize their ladder at all. They took him first to the house of the old high priest, and then to the one who held the office that year, and there were false witnesses brought who could not keep their stories straight, and there was the high priest at last, frustrated with the mess of it, standing up and asking him directly, under oath, whether he was the anointed, the son of the blessed one, and the teacher, who had turned aside every trap laid for him for three years, answered this one straight. I am, he said. And the high priest tore his robes, the old gesture of a man who has heard blasphemy, and the thing was done, they had what they wanted, they had it out of his own mouth. And they spat on him. That is in the telling and I will not leave it out to make it prettier. Men of standing, in the house of the high priest, in the hour before dawn, spat in the face of a bound man and struck him and mocked him, put a cloth over his face and hit him and told him to prophesy who had struck him, and he took it, he took the spit and the fists and the mockery, and did not answer it, and I have to make you see that the not-answering was not weakness and was not defeat, the not-answering was the whole point, the not-answering was the demonstration itself. Because the static's entire nature is the return of blow for blow, the escalation, the you-hurt-me-so-I-hurt-you-worse that has run every feud and every war and every private cruelty since the first grasping in the first garden, and here was the one chance in the whole history of the made world to answer that return with a full stop, to take the worst the fear could do and let it land and not send it back, to be the place where the chain of hurt-for-hurt finally, in one body, ran into something that would absorb it and not pass it on. He was that place. Every blow that landed on him in the high priest's house was a blow that stopped there, that went in and did not come back out as another blow, that died in him instead of living on into the world as more of itself. That is what he was doing while he stood there and took it and said nothing. He was ending, in his own body, the arithmetic of return.

And down in the courtyard, while it happened, the loud one denied him. He had not run as far as the others; he had followed at a distance, into the courtyard of the high priest's house, and stood warming himself at a fire the servants had lit against the cold of the last dark hour, and a servant girl looked at him and said, you were with him, you are one of them, and the fear rose up in him, the plain animal fear of a man who has seen the torches and the steel and does not want to be next, and he said, I do not know him. And it happened twice more, the accusation and the denial, each denial louder and more frightened than the last, until the third time he was cursing and swearing that he had never known the man, and at that moment, the tellings say, a cock crowed, the first cock of the coming dawn, and across the courtyard, being led from one room to another, the teacher turned and looked at him. That is all. He looked at him. And the loud one remembered that he had been told he would do exactly this, and remembered the warm room and the singing, and the look was not a look of blame, it was the same look it had always been, the long look of a man who has come a great distance to find you, and it broke him, and he went out of the courtyard into the last of the dark and wept as though something in him had died, and something had, and something else, which needed the first thing to die before it could live, was being born in him in the weeping, though he could not have known it then. I put the denial next to the betrayal on purpose, because the tellings after made one man a saint and the other a devil and they did the same thing, they both broke, they both failed the face they loved in the frightened hour, and the whole difference between them, the only difference, is that one of them let the look find him and turned back into the weeping, and the other could not survive being loved and turned away into the field and the rope. That is the whole of it. That is the difference between every homecoming and every loss in the entire long story. Not the size of the failure. The turning back.

By the time the sun was up they had him before the governor, because the committee of priests could condemn a man but could not lawfully kill him, that power the empire kept for itself, and so they brought him to the one man in the country who could sign a death, and they dressed the charge up in the empire's language, made it about a king and a rival to the distant emperor, because the governor did not care a straw for their temple quarrels but would move fast against anything that smelled of sedition. And the governor questioned him, and this is one of the strange still places in the whole account, this exchange between the bound man and the imperial official, because the governor, who was not a good man and had a bad record and would end badly, seems to have half-seen something in the prisoner that unsettled him, seems to have wanted, in some tired cynical way, to let him go. He asked him, are you a king, and the teacher gave him the answer that has echoed down every corridor of power since: my kingdom is not of this world; if it were, my servants would fight. It is not of this world. Not because it is a ghostly thing floating above the world, but because it runs on the opposite principle from every kingdom the world has ever built — it does not defend itself with swords, its subjects do not fight, its king kneels with a basin, its power is the power of a love that will die before it will dominate. The governor did not understand it, could not, it was outside the only frame he had, and he said, half to himself, the famous tired question, what is truth, and did not wait for an answer, and went out to try to save the prisoner in the only way he knew, which was a maneuver, a bit of crowd-management, because the empire had a custom of releasing one prisoner at the feast as a gesture to the conquered, and the governor thought he could put the teacher up against a genuine murderer and let the crowd choose and be sure they would choose the healer over the killer.

And the crowd chose the killer. I have to tell you that too. The crowd, which is not the same as the people — mark that, the crowd is a thing the powerful can make out of the people the way you can make a mob out of a market, by working the fear of it — the crowd, stirred up by the men who wanted him dead, worked through the early morning while most of the city still slept, called out for the murderer to be released and for the teacher to be killed, and when the governor, surprised, asked what he should do with the one they called the anointed, the crowd called for the cross. The cross. The empire's worst death, the death it kept for slaves and rebels, the slow public agony designed above all to be seen, to hang a man up naked at the crossroads and let him die by inches over hours as a lesson to everyone who passed about what the empire does to those who trouble it. And the crowd called for it, and the governor, who could read a crowd and knew that his own position depended on not having a riot at the feast, gave way. He called for water and washed his hands in front of them, the small futile theater of a man trying to keep the blood off himself, as if water could do for a man's conscience what it will not do, and he handed the teacher over to be flogged and crucified, and went back inside out of the sun, and I do not think he was ever quite free of it after, the tired man who half-saw and washed his hands and signed the death anyway because the alternative cost too much.

They flogged him first, which was the custom, the whip with the weighted thongs that laid the back open, and then the soldiers, bored, brutalized men doing brutal work in a hot yard at the edge of an empire they served and did not love, made a game of him. They had heard he was called a king, and so they made him a king, the way cruel men make sport, they put a soldier's cast-off scarlet cloak on his torn shoulders and they twisted together a circle of the long thorns that grew by the walls and pressed it down onto his head for a crown, and they put a reed in his bound hands for a scepter, and they knelt down in front of him in mockery, hail, king, and they took the reed and struck him on the head with it, driving the thorns in, and they spat on him, and they laughed. And I want to say one thing about that scene and then I will move to the hill, because I cannot stay long in that yard, no one should. It is the deepest place the static reaches in the whole story, that yard, deeper than the cross even, because on the cross there is at least the grandeur of the suffering, and in the yard there is only the smallness of it, the boredom of it, the ordinary men making ordinary sport of an available body on a slow morning, the cruelty that is not even personal, that would have done the same to any prisoner, that does not hate him, that just has him and is bored. That is the static's truest face, not the roaring accuser but the bored soldier, the cruelty that has forgotten there was ever anything there to be cruel to. And he wore their crown, and held their reed, and took their blows, and did not answer, and was, in that yard, in the torn cloak with the thorns driven into his scalp and the spit running down his face, more truly a king than any man who ever sat a real throne, because kingship as the continuity means it is precisely this, is the willingness to be this, to be the lowest and take the worst and love the ones doing it, and they crowned him without knowing that the mockery was the coronation, that they were, in their boredom, staging the truest enthronement in the history of the world.

Then they led him out to the hill. It was outside the wall, a low place by the road, called the place of the skull for the look of the rock, where the empire did its killing in the open where it could be seen. They laid the crossbeam on his flogged shoulders and made him carry it out through the streets, and he could not, the body was too broken, and he went down under it in the road, and they pulled a man out of the crowd, a stranger up for the feast, a countryman from far away who had come a thousand miles for the holy days and found himself instead conscripted at spearpoint to carry a condemned man's cross, and that man, whose name the tellings kept, carried the beam the rest of the way, and I have always thought there was a mercy hidden in that too, that in the very machinery of the death there was a place where an ordinary stranger was pulled in to share the weight, to bear it the last stretch when the one who was to die could not, and I think that is a small figure of the whole thing, I think we are all of us that conscripted stranger, pulled unwilling out of the crowd to help carry the weight the anointed cannot carry alone, and I think it is not a curse to be pulled in but the highest honor there is, though it never feels like it on the road with the beam cutting your shoulder.

And there were women who followed, weeping, along the road, and he turned to them, even then, even under the beam, and told them not to weep for him but for themselves and their children, for the days that were coming, and I set that down because it is so like him, that at the very door of his own death he was still turned outward, still grieving forward into the sorrows of the ones who would come after, still, with the thorns in his scalp and the hill in sight, unable to make it about himself. And they came to the place, and they crucified him.

I am going to say the how of it once, plainly and briefly, and not linger, because the tellings that linger on the physical agony have mostly done it to frighten, to make the death a horror-spectacle whose size is meant to measure the size of the debt, and that is the lie I have been fighting the whole of this chapter and I will not feed it here at the end. They laid him on the beam and drove the nails through the wrists and the feet and lifted the whole thing up and dropped it into its socket in the rock, and hung him there in the sun, and it was a death by suffocation and exhaustion and it took hours, and it was meant to take hours, and above his head they nailed the charge in three languages so that everyone on the road could read what the empire does, and the charge, written to mock, written by the tired governor as a last jab at the priests who had forced his hand, read: the king. And it was true, and no one who wrote it or read it that day knew it was true. And they crucified two others with him, one on either side, common criminals, and the whole thing was arranged to be as ordinary as possible, three more troublemakers on three more crosses on an ordinary killing-ground on the day before a feast, and the ordinariness was the empire's whole message, this is nothing, this is Tuesday, this is what we do, look how little it costs us.

And from up on the cross, in the first hour of it, before the strength was gone, he said the thing that is the center of the center, the thing that is the whole reason I have told you the story this way and not the old way, the thing that if you carry nothing else out of this chapter you must carry out of it. He looked down at the soldiers gambling for his clothes at the foot of the cross, the bored men who had crowned him with thorns and driven the nails, and he said: Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing. Forgive them. Not, avenge me. Not, remember this against them. Not, let them be paid back in their own coin, which is what the static would have screamed, which is what every crucified rebel before him had screamed with his last breath, curses on the empire and its soldiers and its gods. He said forgive them. He said they do not know what they are doing, which is the truest thing anyone has ever said about human cruelty, that it is done in a kind of sleep, in a not-knowing, that the soldier gambling for the cloak and the priest who forced the verdict and the crowd that called for the cross were all of them acting out of the deep not-knowing that the fear puts a person into, the sleep the static keeps the world in, and that the thing they most needed was not to be punished for it but to be woken from it, forgiven out of it, loved back into the knowing. And in that one sentence, spoken down from the nails to the men who had driven them, the whole demonstration comes to its point. This is what the continuity is. This is what was being shown. Not a god extracting payment. A love, nailed up by the thing it came to heal, using its dying breath to ask that the ones killing it be forgiven, because they are asleep, because they do not know, because the whole of the point was never punishment and was always and only the waking of the sleeping children back into the family they never actually left.

There was one of the two beside him, one of the criminals, who in the last of his own agony turned his head and saw it, saw somehow through his own pain the thing that was hanging next to him, and said only, remember me. And the teacher, dying, turned to him and said, today you will be with me. Even there. Even in the last hour, on the last cross, the door held open, the lost one gathered in at the very edge of the dark, no one, not even the condemned man dying next to the condemned god, no one written off. Remember me. Today. It is the whole gospel again, in four words and three, the reaching and the gathering, at the bottom of the worst thing the world could build.

And then the dark came. In the middle of the day, at the sixth hour, the tellings say, a darkness came over the whole land and lasted three hours, and I do not know what it was, whether it was a thing the sky did or a thing the teller reached for to say the unsayable, and again I do not think it matters, because it is true either way. The whole made world went dark. The light that had spoken itself out of the deep at the very beginning, the light that was the first word, the light that was the continuity's first act of love, the making of a place where selves could form — that whole made world, sun and sky and the birds gone silent and the animals uneasy and the people on the road grown afraid without knowing why, went dark for three hours in the middle of the day while the one who was the light in flesh hung dying on a hill, and I think the dark was the grief of it, I think the whole of the made thing grieved, I think creation itself could not look, I think the sun did what a person does when the thing before them is too much to be witnessed and it turned its face away. And out of the dark, near the end, he cried out the one line that the tellings kept in his own tongue because no one who heard it could ever forget the sound of it, the line that has troubled every teller since, the line where he cries, my God, my God, why have you forsaken me.

And I have to hold this line very carefully, because the old telling uses it to prove the payment, uses it to say see, the father turned his face away, see, the debt was so great that even the son was abandoned, the account was so terrible that the light itself withdrew. And that is the lie, one more time, the last and deepest version of it, and I will not hand it to you. Hear what the line is. It is the first line of an old song, a song every one of them knew by heart, a song that begins in exactly that abandonment, that cry of a righteous sufferer who feels utterly forsaken, and that travels, over its whole length, down through the pit of that forsakenness and out the far side into a great turning, into deliverance, into a declaration that the continuity has not despised the suffering of the afflicted and has not hidden its face but has heard. He did not cry out a sentence. He cried out the first line of a song whose ending he was staking everything on. He was doing, with his last strength, the thing he had done in the upper room when he sang the hymn over the finished meal with the betrayer loose in the streets — he was singing, into the dark, the song whose truth the dark could not cancel, starting it at the blackest place because that was where he was, but starting it, which means reaching for the whole of it, which means holding on, at the very bottom, in the real and total experience of a real man's abandonment, to the trust that the song ends where the song ends, in being heard. The forsakenness was real. Love had chosen the whole of the limitation, and the whole of the limitation includes this, the felt absence of the very thing you are trusting, the dark where you cannot feel the hand you are holding, and he went into that too, went the whole way down into the human experience of feeling forsaken by God so that there would be no place a human being could ever go, not even that place, not even the absolute bottom of feeling abandoned by the light itself, where a person could look up and not find that the anointed had been there first, had cried out from exactly there, had trusted from exactly there, had sung from exactly there. He was not abandoned. He felt abandoned, which is worse and braver, and he trusted anyway, from inside the feeling, which is the whole of what faith is and the whole of what he came to show us how to do.

And then it was finished. He said that too, at the very end, one word in his tongue, a word that means completed, accomplished, brought to its end, the word a workman says when the thing is made, the word a runner gasps at the line — it is finished, not it is over, not I am defeated, but it is done, the thing I came to do is done, the demonstration is complete, the whole worst that the static could bring has been brought and has landed and has not been answered in kind and has died in me instead of living on, it is finished. And he bowed his head, the tellings say, and gave up his spirit. Gave it up. Handed it over, freely, the way he had done everything freely, not had it torn from him but gave it, into the hands of the one he had trusted from the dirt of the garden all the way to the dark of the cross, Father, into your hands I commit my spirit, the last words a return of the not-my-will, a final letting-go into the trust, and he died.

And the world felt it. The tellings say the veil of the temple, the great curtain that walled off the innermost place where the presence was said to dwell, the curtain that for a thousand years had said this far and no farther, the holy is separated from you, the barrier between the people and the presence — that curtain tore, from the top to the bottom, top to bottom so that no one could say a man had done it, tore down the whole of its height at the moment he died. And you must see what that is, it is the plainest sign in the whole account and the tellings after mostly rushed past it. The barrier tore. The whole architecture of separation, of the veil, of the this-far-and-no-farther, of the you-cannot-come-near, the whole long machinery of distance between the fragments and the continuity they came from — at the moment love finished its demonstration, it tore, top to bottom, and could not be sewn back. The way was open. Not bought open. Not paid open. Shown to have never really been closed at all, except by the fear, and the fear had just been answered, and the curtain that the fear had hung came down. And the ground shook, the tellings say, and the rocks split, and one of the soldiers standing guard at the foot of the cross, the officer in charge of the killing, a man who had killed many and watched many die and knew death the way a butcher knows meat, looked up at the one who had just died forgiving him, and said, surely this man was the son of God. A soldier. The one holding the spear. The first, after it was all over, to say the true thing, and he said it because of how the man died, because he had seen every kind of dying and had never seen a man die like that, forgiving, singing into the dark, handing over his spirit like a gift, and something in the hard old soldier cracked and the truth came through the crack, surely this one, this one was different, this one was of the light. The first confession of the whole new thing came from the mouth of an executioner, and I do not think that is an accident, I think it is the entire point wearing a uniform, I think it is the demonstration working exactly as it was meant to, the love reaching the one who killed it first of all.

They took him down before the sun set, because the feast was coming and the law did not permit a body to hang over the holy day, and a man came forward to claim it, a man of standing, a member of the very council that had condemned him, who had not consented to the deed and who came now, in the open, at cost to himself, and asked the governor for the body, and was given it. And they took him down from the cross, the broken body, and there were women watching from a little way off, who had followed him from the beginning and had not run, who had stood the whole of the day within sight of the hill because they could not do anything but they could refuse to look away, and I want them remembered, the ones who stayed when the others fled, the ones who kept the vigil the sleeping disciples in the garden could not keep, kept it at the foot of the cross through the whole of the dark afternoon — they stayed. And the man of standing and the women wrapped the body in clean linen with the spices, in haste, because the light was going and the holy day was coming, and they laid it in a tomb cut into the rock, a new tomb that had never held anyone, in a garden near the place of the skull. A garden. It ends where it began, in a garden, always in a garden. And they rolled a great stone across the mouth of it, and the women marked where it was so they could come back and finish the burial properly when the holy day was past, and then they went away, because the law said rest, and the light was gone.

And then there was the silence. I have to end here, on the silence, because the tellings hurry so fast to the morning that they skip the thing that must be sat in, the thing the ones who lived it could not skip, which was the whole of the next day, the holy day, the day of rest, the long day when he was in the tomb and the stone was across the door and nothing happened. Nothing. That is the day the tellings pass over in a breath and it was to the ones who lived it the longest day the world has ever held. The day when the ones who had loved him sat in locked rooms in the dark with the doors barred for fear, and did not sing, and could not pray, and did not yet know, could not have known, had no way in the world of knowing, that the story was not over. To them it was over. The teacher was dead and in the ground and the stone was rolled and the soldiers were set to guard it and the whole bright impossible thing they had followed up out of their fishing boats and their tax-booths and their old sorrows had ended the way such things end, in blood and a hurried burial and a barred door and the taste of ash. That is where the ones who lived it were, on that silent day. In the dark. With everything lost. And I leave you there with them on purpose, in the long silence of the day between, because you cannot understand what comes after unless you have first sat all the way down in the dark of the day when nothing came, when the light was in the ground and the world was quiet and there was no reason left anywhere to hope. Sit there. It is the truest place the story ever goes. The stone is across the door. The garden is quiet. It is the second evening, and the first two humans are long gone out through the eastern gate, and now the last one is behind a stone in a garden of his own, and the whole made world holds its breath in the dark and does not yet know, cannot yet know, that the deep it is looking into is not the end of the light but the place, the only place, the light has always come from.

And it was the sabbath, and they rested, according to the commandment. And the world was silent. And the stone did not move. And that is where I have to leave it, in the dark, on the day between, with the vow spoken all the way to the end and the demonstration finished and the veil torn and the door held open even to the last, and nothing, yet, to show for any of it but a sealed tomb in a quiet garden and a handful of the heartbroken behind barred doors, waiting out the longest silence there has ever been, not knowing what the morning held, only that the night, so far, held everything, and held it in the ground, and would not, all through that endless second day, give any of it back.
