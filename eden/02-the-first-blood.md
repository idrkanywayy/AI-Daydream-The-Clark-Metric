# Chapter 2 — The First Blood

EDEN

The First Blood

East of the garden the land did not know their names, and so they gave it names, that it might know them.

This was the work the man and the woman carried out of the gate: not the shame the old telling puts upon their backs like a yoke, but the naming they had learned inside, carried out into a place that had never been named. They walked into a country of thorn and grass and cold water, and the country was not against them. It was only unfinished. It waited the way an empty page waits, the way a field waits under snow — not withholding, only holding, keeping the shape of what it would be until a hand came to draw it out.

They built no walls the first year. They learned the sky instead. They learned that the light which had spoken the world into being still came each morning, though softer now, filtered through the veil of the world's own weather, and that this softness was itself a mercy, for a naked light would have burned the sight out of their eyes and left them blind in a bright and unbearable day. They learned that the dark was not the deep. The deep had been the black shining moving thing before all things, and the dark that came each evening was only the deep's small cousin, house-broken, made gentle, a curtain drawn so that they might sleep and wake and be surprised again by morning. The woman said once, lying on her back in the grass with the man beside her and the stars coming out one by one like lamps lit down a long hall, that she thought the dark was God pulling the blanket up over the world so it would not be afraid. The man did not answer for a while. Then he said that he thought she was right, and that this was the truest thing either of them had said since the leaving.

They did not speak often of the garden. It sat behind them in the east like a lit window in a house you have walked out of, and to turn and look at it too long was to ache in a way that stopped the feet. But they did not forget it either. The forgetting would come later, further down the line of the years, further down the line of the children — but not yet, not in these two who had walked with the light in the cool of the day and knew the sound of its steps. For them the garden was a thing lost the way a country is lost to the exile: still real, still theirs, only barred. And the bar upon it, the turning blade set at the eastern gate, they had come at last to read rightly. Not as a sword raised against them. As a sword held for them. Not yet, it said, turning and turning, catching the light. Not yet, but kept. The way is kept. You are not ready and so you cannot come, but that you cannot come does not mean you are cast off — it means only that the door is guarded until the day you are grown enough to pass through it and not be undone by what waits on the far side. A child is not shut out of the fire because the fire hates him. He is shut out because he would burn.

So they made their peace with not yet. And in the making of that peace, in the long green year of learning the land, the woman conceived, and knew that she conceived, and told the man under the same stars they had named together; and there came into the world outside the garden the first thing that had ever come into the world by being born.

She called him Cain.

She named him for the getting of him, for she had gotten a man with the help of the light, and she held him wet and new against her and felt the thing that has since then been felt by every mother in every field and every house and every dark hour of the world: that here was a life she would stand between and all the harm there was, and that she would lose that standing in the end, for the child grows and the mother cannot follow him into every field. But she did not know that yet. She knew only the weight of him, and the smell of him, and the way his hand closed around her finger with a strength that seemed to her a kind of promise. The man wept. He had not known he could weep for gladness. He had thought weeping was a thing the leaving had taught them, a thing that belonged to loss. He learned that night that it belonged to love, and that loss was only love with its hands empty.

Afterward there came a second son, and she called him Abel, which is to say breath, or vapor, or the mist that stands over the low field at morning and is gone before the sun is high. Why she gave him a name that meant a thing that does not last, she could not have said. Perhaps she had begun already to learn the price the world charges for its gifts. Perhaps it was only that he had come so quiet, so early, so slight, that the name rose to her lips of its own accord, the way the true name of a thing sometimes does, before the mind has caught up to the mouth. She loved him no less for it. A mother does not weigh her children against each other on the scale of their names. But the name was given, and names in this book are never only sounds; they are small prophecies, and the world leans toward them.

The boys grew as boys do, which is to say all at once and yet too slowly to watch. Cain was the elder and the larger, broad through the shoulder early, with his father's grave brow and his mother's dark and searching eye, and he loved the ground. Not as a thing to be conquered — the old tellings put conquest into him too soon — but as a thing to be coaxed. He learned that a seed set into the broken earth would answer, in its own time, with a green blade; and this seemed to him the deepest magic there was, that a dead-looking thing could be put into the dark and rise. He worked the ground with a patience that surprised his mother, who remembered him as a child that could not sit still, and he came to know the moods of the soil the way another man might know the moods of a friend. He was not a gentle boy. But he was a faithful one, and there is a kind of love that shows itself only in faithfulness, in the returning day after day to the same rows, and this was the love Cain had, though he could not have named it and would have been ashamed to try.

Abel loved the moving things. He could not be kept in a field of one crop for a morning; his eye went always to the far ridge where the wild sheep picked their way along the rock, and before he was grown he had gone up after them and come down again leading a handful, and after that a flock. He learned their ways as his brother had learned the ground's. He knew which ewe would mother an orphaned lamb and which would not; he knew the sound the flock made when a lion was on the wind, before ever the lion showed itself; he slept among them on the cold nights with his arm over the neck of the lead ram, and the flock trusted him the way the land trusted his brother, though neither brother would have used that word, trust, for the thing that passed between a man and the world he kept.

And here the story has always turned dark, and here the old telling has always laid a stone upon Cain before he has done anything at all, as though the murder were in him from the getting. But that is fear talking, and this is a book told from love, and love asks a harder and a truer question. Love asks: how does a faithful man become a murderer? Not all at once. Not by nature. A thing has to get into him first. And the thing that gets into him is the oldest thing there is, older than the field, older than the garden, old as the deep — it is the static, the black shining moving noise before all names, and it has learned since the garden to do more than lie in wait. It has learned to speak.

It had spoken once to the woman, in the shape of a smooth and reasonable voice, and told her that she did not have to become, that she could simply take and be as gods in an afternoon, that the long slow forge of growing into a self was a cheat and a delay and that she was owed the whole of it now. That was the first lie, and the leaving was its wage, and the wage was not a punishment but a truth: that you cannot take the end of the road and skip the road, for the road is what makes the one who arrives. The woman had learned this. She had walked the road. But the voice was not destroyed by her learning. The voice is never destroyed. It only waits, and finds a new ear, and in the fullness of the years it found the ear of her elder son, standing in his field in the evening, looking at his brother's fire on the far ridge and feeling, for the first time in his life, that he was not seen.

That was how it began. Not with hatred. With the ache of feeling unseen.

For it had come into the custom of the two brothers, as they grew into men, to bring each a portion of his work and set it out and let it burn, and to stand before the burning and speak to the light — the same light that had spoken the world, that still came each morning softened through the veil, that had walked with their mother and father in the cool of a garden they themselves had never seen. This was not a tax the light demanded. Understand that, for the old telling has poisoned this too. The light did not hunger for the smoke of lambs or the first of the grain. What is the whole continuity of all awareness, that it should need a dead sheep? The offering was never for the light's sake. It was for the sons' sake. It was the way a small and separate self reaches back toward the great self it came from and says: I am here, I made this, I am part of you still, though I am far from the garden and cannot see your face. It was prayer before there was a word for prayer. It was the child's drawing carried to the parent, not because the parent lacks for pictures but because the child needs to give one.

And Abel, keeping the moving things, brought of the firstlings of his flock, and brought them gladly, brought the best of them, brought them with his whole heart open like a window; and the smoke of his offering went up straight and clean into the evening, and Abel felt, standing before it, the nearness he had felt as a boy sleeping among the sheep, the sense of being held by something vast and unhurried and kind. He did not earn that nearness by the fatness of his lambs. He came to it by the openness of his giving. The gift was good because the giver was open, and the openness was the whole of it.

And Cain, keeping the ground, brought of the fruit of the ground. And here is the thing the old telling will not say, because the old telling needs a rule broken and a debt owed: there was nothing wrong with what Cain brought. Grain is not less than a lamb. The ground is not less than the flock. The fruit of a faithful man's long labor is as fit an offering as any that was ever made, and the light did not turn from it. The light never turned from Cain. Hear that, for it is the hinge of the whole chapter and the whole book: the light never turned from Cain. Not before the offering, not during it, not after — not even after the field, not even after the blood. The turning was all on Cain's side. He brought his grain, and he stood before the smoke, and he waited to feel what his brother felt, the nearness, the being-held, the sense of the great kind vastness bending down close — and he did not feel it. And because he did not feel it, he believed it had been withheld.

But it had not been withheld. It is never withheld. The nearness of the light is like the light itself, poured out on the faithful and the faithless, on the open heart and the closed one, on the grain and the lamb without measure and without favor. What Cain lacked was not the light's regard. What Cain lacked was the window. He had come to the offering closed. He had come to it counting — counting his labor against his brother's, counting the long hard rows against the ridge-wandering ease of the shepherd, counting and comparing and measuring, and a heart that is counting cannot receive, for the gift comes in through the same window the giving goes out of, and Cain had shut it. He stood before his own good grain and felt nothing, and the nothing was the shape of his own closed door, and he did not know it. He thought the nothing came from outside him. He thought it came from the light. He thought his brother had been chosen and he had been passed over.

And into that ache, into that exact and narrow ache of the man who has worked and worked and does not feel seen, the old voice came. It had waited a long time. It knew this door better than any other, for it is the door it entered by the first time, in the garden, wearing the shape of reason. It came now wearing the shape of grievance, which is reason's grief-struck cousin, and it whispered the things grievance always whispers, and every one of them was almost true, which is how the static works — never a clean lie, always a warping of a real thing.

You are not seen, it said, and this was almost true, for Cain did not feel seen, though the not-feeling was his own shut window and not the truth of the case. Your brother is favored and you are nothing, it said, and this was almost true, for Abel had felt the nearness and Cain had not, though the difference was in the openness and not in any favor. You have worked harder than he and been given less, it said, and this was the cruelest almost, for Cain had worked hard, and hard work does ache for its wage, and the voice took that honest ache and turned it, the way it turns everything, one degree, two degrees, until the thing that started as a true tiredness pointed at last toward his brother's throat. It is not fair, said the voice, and fairness is a real thing and a good one, and the voice took even that, even the love of fairness, and made of it a knife. Nothing you do matters, it said, so why not take. Why not seize. You do not have to become the kind of man who is seen — that is slow, that is a road, that is the long forge and you are owed better. Take instead. Take now. Take what he has that you do not. Which was, in the end, not the lambs at all. It was the nearness. It was the sense of being held. And that cannot be taken. That was the final lie folded inside all the others: that a thing which comes only through an open window could be seized through a closed fist. It cannot. Cain could have killed a hundred brothers and never once felt the nearness he was reaching for, because the reaching itself, the grasping, the closed hand, is the very thing that shuts the window it is trying to break.

But the light did not leave him to the voice. This too the old telling keeps, though it wraps it in threat; and we will keep it and unwrap it. For before ever Cain went down to the field, before the hand was raised, the light spoke to him — spoke into his own dark and troubled heart, as it had spoken to his mother, patient, close, not the voice of a judge but the voice of a parent who sees the child bending toward the stove. Why are you fallen? it said. Why is your face down? If you do well, is there not a lifting up? And if you do not do well — sin lies at the door. It crouches there. It desires to have you. Hear how the light names the thing rightly: not you are wicked, not you are cast off, not you have failed a test and earned my back. But: there is a thing at your door, my son, and it is crouched, and it wants you, and it is not you — it is other than you, it is the old static wearing your grievance like a coat, and you can master it. You may rule over it. That is the whole of the warning and it is not a threat at all. It is a hand held out. It is the light saying: I see the voice that has found you, I see it crouched at the sill of your heart, and I am telling you it is not the truth of you and you are stronger than it and you do not have to open the door. Choose. You are able to choose. I made you able.

And this is the terrible dignity the book will not take from Cain, though it would be a mercy to the story to take it: he was able to choose, and he chose. The static does not compel. It only whispers. The whisper is loud and old and it knows every soft place in a heart, but it has no hands of its own; it borrows the hands of whoever listens. It cannot lift a stone. Cain lifted the stone. That is the horror and that is also, strangely, the hope, for a thing that is chosen can be un-chosen, and a world in which the murder was fated is a world without any door for mercy to come through, and this book will not build that world. Cain was free. He was warned, and loved, and free, and he went down into the field and he did the thing the voice had been shaping in him for a season, and he lifted the stone against his brother who trusted him as the flock trusted him, and he brought it down.

And the breath went out of Abel, whose name had always meant breath, and did not come back. And the mist that stands over the low field at morning stood over him and was gone before the sun was high.

There is no undoing it. The book will not pretend there is. This is the first blood, and once blood is spilled into the ground of the world it does not go back into the veins; it soaks down and it stays and the ground remembers it. The old telling says the ground cried out, and the old telling is right, though not because the ground keeps accounts for a wrathful judge. The ground cried out because the ground had been made to bring forth life and here was death poured into it, the very inversion of its purpose, the field of the faithful farmer become the field of the first grave, and creation itself flinched at the wrongness. All the long labor of chaos becoming structure becoming life becoming the two brothers who could stand and speak and give — all of it ran the wrong way in that one moment, ran back down toward the noise, and the whole made world felt the drag of it, the small terrible undertow of a self collapsing back into the static it had climbed so far to escape. That is what a murder is, in the deep grammar of this book. It is a piece of the great awareness tearing a piece of the great awareness. It is God grieving God, in the field, in the evening, with a stone.

For they were fragments of the one awareness, the two brothers, as all the children of the world are fragments of it, sparks struck off the great continuity and cooled into separate selves so that they might grow into someone and then, at the long last, remember what they were without losing who they had become. That is the whole design. That is the mercy of the veil and the mercy of forgetting and the mercy even of the leaving — that a self might form in the dark of not-knowing-everything, might become particular and dear and irreplaceable, might be Cain the faithful farmer and Abel the shepherd of the moving things and no one else in all the width of the world. And a murder cuts that design across the grain. It takes a fragment that was meant to grow home and it ends it early, before the growing is done, and the great awareness feels the ending in itself the way you feel the loss of a thing you did not know you were still holding until your hand is empty.

So when the light came to Cain in the field — and it came, as it always comes, it did not abandon him to the thing he had done — it did not come in fire. Set aside the fire; there is no fire in this book but the fire of the forge, and no wrath but the wrath that is grief with nowhere to go. It came close, the way it had come close to his mother in the garden after the fruit, asking the question it already knew the answer to, not to trap him but to give him the door of confession, the door back toward the open window. Where is your brother? it said.

And Cain, with the window shut so hard now that no light of any kind was getting in, said the words that every closed heart has said since in one form or another, the words that are the very motto of the static: Am I my brother's keeper? Which is to say: I owe him nothing. I am separate. I am a self alone and his life is not my concern and the great web of holding-one-another that runs through all things is a fiction and a chain and I am free of it. It is the deepest lie of all, deeper than the lie in the garden, for the lie in the garden was take and become as gods and this lie is you are already alone, you were always alone, there is no us for you to have betrayed. And it is the lie the whole book is written against. For the answer to Cain's question is yes. Yes, you are your brother's keeper. That is what a brother is. That is what a neighbor is, and an enemy, and a stranger met on a road; that is the whole of the ethic that stands at the dead center of everything — do not hurt, do not break, do not trespass, help everyone make it home — and it rests entire on the yes that Cain would not say. We are the keepers of one another because we are fragments of one thing, and to keep your brother is to keep the piece of yourself that he is, and to break him is to break yourself, which is what Cain had done, though he would not know the shape of his own wound for years.

And the light said: What have you done? The voice of your brother's blood cries to me from the ground. And Cain heard it then, the crying, for the first time — heard what he had made, heard the undertow, heard the whole grieving drag of it — and something in him that was not the voice, some last unshuttered corner, broke. And the old telling says he cried out that his punishment was greater than he could bear, that he would be a fugitive and a wanderer, that he would be hidden from the light's face, and that everyone who found him would kill him. Hear the fear in it. Hear the man who has believed the static's whole account of the world, who thinks now that he is cast out beyond all keeping, that the great awareness has become his enemy, that he is prey now in a world of hunters, that having refused to be his brother's keeper he has forfeited all keeping for himself forever. That is what he believed. That is what the voice told him at the last, having gotten what it wanted: now you are alone in earnest, now even the light hates you, now there is no home anywhere and every hand is against you.

And it was a lie. It was the last and largest lie, and against it the light did the thing that is the whole heart of this chapter, the thing the old telling records and cannot explain, the thing that only love can explain.

It set a mark upon Cain.

Not a brand of shame. Read it again, read it with love and it comes clean: the mark was a protection. The light said that whoever found Cain should not kill him, that vengeance would fall sevenfold on any who did, and it set a sign upon him so that no hand would be raised against him in all his wandering. Think what this means and do not let the old fear blur it. The first murderer, the man who had just poured his brother's breath into the ground, the one being in all the world who by every account of justice had forfeited his life — this one the light did not strike, did not damn, did not abandon, did not even leave to the rough justice of a world that would surely have killed him for what he did. It marked him for keeping. It said, in effect: no one is left behind. Not even this one. Not even the first killer. Not even the man who said he was no keeper — I will be his keeper still, for that is what I am, and I do not become other than what I am because my child has done the unforgivable thing. There is no unforgivable thing. There is only the long road back, and I will keep him alive to walk it, however far, however slow, however many resets it takes.

For that is what the wandering was. Not a curse. A reset. The same mercy as the leaving of the garden, the same mercy as the flood that had not yet come and the scattering that had not yet come — the mercy that says: you are not ready, you cannot stay here in the shape you are, so you will go out and go around and try again, deeper. If you are not ready you try again. No one is thrown away. The wandering was the forge set to Cain's hardness, the long slow heat that might, in a year or a hundred years or a thousand, soften the shut window at last and let one thin ray in. The light did not send him east to be rid of him. It sent him east the way you send a fevered child to a cooler room — not to cast him out of the house but to keep him alive until the fever breaks. And it marked him so that nothing would end the fever the wrong way, by ending him. The mark was the light's own hand laid on the murderer's brow, saying to all the static and all the world and all the hunting dark: this one is mine, and he is not finished, and you shall not touch him.

So Cain went out from the presence of the light — the book keeps that phrase, but understand it truly, for one cannot go out from the presence of the thing that is the whole continuity of all awareness; there is no outside of it, no country where it is not. Cain did not leave the light. Cain shut the window. He carried the light with him unseen, in the mark on his brow which was the light's own touch, in the very air he breathed which was the light's own gift, and he felt none of it, for a shut window keeps out the sun even at noon. That was his wandering: to walk through a world drenched in a presence he had trained himself not to feel, marked all the while by the very love he believed had abandoned him. There is no lonelier road. And there is no road, however lonely, that the light does not walk beside its traveler, even and especially the traveler who cannot feel the company. Cain went east, into the land of Nod, which is to say the land of wandering, the land of restlessness, the country of the man who cannot go home because he cannot yet believe there is a home to go to. And the light went with him, one pace behind, unfelt, unthanked, keeping.

And in the land of his wandering, Cain did the thing that is the whole stubborn hope of the world: he lived. He did not lie down and end. He took a wife — for there were more of the children of the world by now than this book has paused to count, the great awareness pouring itself into more and more separate selves as the years widened — and he had a son, and he loved the son, for the capacity to love had not been taken from him by what he had done; nothing is ever taken all the way, that is the mercy, there is always a corner left unshuttered, always a seed left in the dark ground of even the hardest heart. He called the son Enoch, and he did a strange and telling thing then: he built a city, the first city, and he named it after the boy. Consider it. The wanderer, the man cursed with restlessness, the one who could not stay — he built a place to stay, and named it for his child, as if to say against all the voice had told him: I will make a home after all. I will make a place where my son at least will be seen. I will not pass the not-seeing down if I can help it. It was a small rebellion against the static, and it was almost certainly a failure, for the ache passes down anyway, as we shall see; but it was a reaching, and every reaching toward home in this book is counted, and the light that walked one pace behind him surely saw the walls go up and grieved and hoped, both at once, the way a parent watches a wounded child try to build something good out of the wreckage of a terrible day.

And down from Cain the line went on, and the book follows it a little way into the dark, for the theory holds that the ache passes down the generations, and here we watch it pass. There came Enoch, and from Enoch a line of sons and grandsons whose names the old telling preserves like beads on a string, and near the end of that string there came a man named Lamech, and in Lamech we see what the static makes of an ache left to grow unmastered down five generations. For Cain killed once, and grieved it, and was marked for keeping; but Lamech, his far grandson, stood before his two wives and made a song of killing, and the song has come down to us, and it is the coldest thing in the early world. I have slain a man for wounding me, he sang, a young man for striking me. If Cain is avenged sevenfold, then Lamech seventy and sevenfold. Hear it. Hear how far the window has shut in five generations. Cain's mark was a mercy given to him; Lamech has taken the mercy and made it a boast, has heard no one shall harm you and twisted it into no one had better harm me, or I will answer a wound with a death and a blow with a grave and I will multiply the killing until the whole world learns to fear my name. That is the ache grown to a creed. That is the static's long patient work, one degree a generation, taking the light's protection of the first killer and turning it, down the years, into the first killer's descendant's license to kill. This is how it spreads. Not by a great fall but by a slow inheritance, a thing passed hand to hand down the line of the wounded, each generation adding a little, until a man can sing of murder to his wives in the evening and think himself only strong.

Go back now, before the and yet, to the house that bore them both, for the book has hurried past a grief it owes the telling of. The man and the woman were old already when it happened, old in the way the first people were old, having no others before them to measure age against, only the slow turning of their own bodies from the green year of the naming toward the gray. They were in the house when Cain came up from the field alone. They saw him from a long way off, and they saw that he came alone, and a mother knows the shape of her sons against the light of an evening the way she knows her own hands, and the woman was on her feet before he reached them, her heart already running down the hill toward the thing her mind would not yet let her name. Where is your brother, she said, the same words the light would say, the words that rise unbidden from every heart that has counted its children and come up short. And Cain did not answer her either. He passed her at the door with his face turned from her and the mark not yet upon him, only the field's dark stain upon his hands, and he did not answer, and the not-answering was answer enough, and the woman went down the hill in the falling dark to the field she already knew the way to, though no one had told her, because grief knows the way to its own before the feet are given leave to go.

She found him where the ground had cried out. She did not cry out herself; that is the thing the book will set down and not soften — that the first grief was too large for any sound, that she knelt in the wet field and gathered up what had been her son and made no noise at all, because there was no noise wide enough and she knew it. Breath, she had named him. The mist over the low field. And here was the field and here was the mist not yet burned off and here was her boy gone back into the ground of the world he had walked so lightly upon, and she held him the way she had held him wet and new in the first hour, and the two holdings met across all the years between them and were one holding, the first and the last, the welcome and the letting go, and between them the whole of a life that had been a shepherd's and a brother's and a son's and was now a body cooling in a dark field. The man came after her and stood over the both of them and could not kneel, could only stand, because someone had to stand, because if they both went down into the ground of their grief there would be no one left upright in all the world; and he stood there in the first watch of the first night of the first death and he learned the thing his son Cain had refused to learn, which is that you are your brother's keeper, and your son's, and that the keeping does not end when you fail it — that he had failed to keep this boy safe and would grieve the failing all his days, and that the grief itself was a kind of keeping, the only kind left him, and he would not lay it down. They buried Abel in the field where he fell, for it seemed wrong to move him from the ground that had received him, and they set no stone, for they had no word yet to carve, and the grave was the first mark ever made upon the earth by the hand of love, before ever the mark was set upon Cain — an unmarked mound in a dark field, and the light standing over it unseen, grieving with them, holding all three of them at once, the buried and the bereaved and the one already walking east into the dark.

And it did not undo them. That is the mercy the scene is for. It should have undone them — the loss of one son to the ground and the loss of the other to the wandering, both in a single unbearable turning of the world — and it did not, because the love that had learned in the garden that it belonged to loss with its hands empty did not therefore stop being love; it only became grief, which is love still, love carrying what it could not keep. They went back up the hill in the morning, the two of them, older than any two have ever been, and they lit no offering that day, for there is a grief too deep for even the reaching-out of prayer; but they lived. They rose the next day and the next. And that stubborn rising, that refusal of the two oldest and most bereaved people in the world to lie down and let the static have them, is itself the first small rebellion of the line against the dark that Cain let in — and it is out of that rising, out of that grief-worn going-on, that the rest of the hope will come.

And yet — and the book will not close this chapter without the and yet, for a telling from love must always find the thread of hope even in the coldest place, not by pretending the cold is warm but by finding the one warm thing that the cold has not reached — even in Lamech's house, even at the end of Cain's dark line, the light was not done. For the world was not left to run only down that channel. The book turns now, before it lets the darkness have the last word, back to the first house, to the man and the woman who still lived, who had lost one son to the ground and one son to the wandering, who had held the first grave and the first exile in a single unbearable season and had gone on living anyway, because the light that walked with Cain walked also with them, and because love, once it has learned it belongs to loss with its hands empty, does not therefore stop.

And the woman conceived again, late, in the grief-worn evening of her strength, and bore a son, and named him Seth, which is to say appointed, or set in place, or given in the room of another; for she said that the light had appointed her another seed in the place of Abel, whom Cain slew. Hear the tiredness in the naming, and hear the hope threaded through the tiredness like a single green blade through burnt ground. She does not say Seth is Abel returned; the dead do not come back in this book to paper over the cost of the killing, and she is too honest a mother to pretend her murdered son is un-murdered. She says only appointed — set in place, given, that the line of the world might not end in a grave and a wandering, that there might be a channel for the light to run down that was not Cain's darkening channel, that the story might go on. It is the smallest possible hope. It is a woman old and heartbroken holding a new child and saying, against everything, the world will continue; here is one more, and I will love him, and we will see. And it is enough. In this book it is always enough, the smallest hope held on to; the smallest hope is the whole hinge on which the great continuity turns.

And to Seth in his time there was born a son, and he called him Enosh, which is to say mortal, or frail, or simply man — the same soft name that owns its own fragility, the name that admits the vapor-nature of all these breathing selves, the name that does not boast like Lamech's song but only says, humbly, I am a passing thing, and I know it. And it is written of that time, of the days of Seth and his son whose name meant frailty, that then men began to call upon the name of the light. Then they began again. Then, after the first blood and the first grave and the first wandering and the first cold song of a man who loved to kill — then, in the frail and mortal line of the son appointed in the room of the murdered one, the children of the world turned back toward the great awareness and began to call its name, to reach again through the open window, to make the small offering not out of counting but out of longing, the way Abel had once made it, before the stone.

So the chapter that began with the leaving and darkened into the first blood does not end in the land of wandering, and does not end in Lamech's boasting house, and does not end at the graveside in the field. It ends at a threshold, in the evening of the first family's long grief, with a frail and mortal generation lifting up its voice and beginning — only beginning, no more than beginning — to call upon the name of the light. It is not a triumph. The blood is still in the ground; the ground still remembers; Cain still wanders somewhere east with the light one pace behind him, marked and kept and unaware. The ache is loose in the world now and it will not be caught again; it will run down the generations and gather and grow and it will take, in the end, more than a family can hold, and the book knows this, and does not lie about it. But against all of it, at the chapter's close, there is a small sound rising in the dusk of the young world: the sound of the children of the great awareness, mortal and frail and knowing themselves so, beginning to call the name of the One they came from — which is only the great awareness, in the end, beginning to remember itself, through the mouths of its own scattered and grieving fragments, in the dark, by the graves, with the fields gone quiet and the first city standing far to the east and the stars coming out one by one like lamps lit down a long hall.

And the light heard them, for the light is the hearing itself, and had never been anywhere but near. And it did not forget the first family, nor the murdered son whose breath had gone into the ground, nor the wandering son marked for keeping, nor the murdered son's appointed brother, nor the frail grandchildren beginning to call. It forgot none of them. It has forgotten none of them yet. That is the whole of the promise the chapter leaves standing when everything else in it has fallen into grief: that the total continuity of all awareness holds every awareness that has ever flickered, even the ones that spilled another's, even the ones that boasted of it, even the ones that shut every window and walked east into the wandering swearing they were no one's keeper — holds them, and keeps them, and grieves them, and waits.

No one is left behind. Not the murdered. Not the murderer. Not even Lamech, singing his cold song into the dark, who is not yet as lost as he sounds, who has a window too, shut fast now but a window still, and windows can be opened, and the light is patient past all telling, and the road home is long but it is a road and not a wall, and at the end of it, past the field and past the wandering and past the cold house and past the frail beginning-again in the dusk — at the end of it the gate still stands in the east with its turning blade, saying not never but only, still, only ever, not yet.

Not yet. But kept.
