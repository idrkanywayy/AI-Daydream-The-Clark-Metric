# Chapter 10 — The Prophets Ache

EDEN

The Prophets Ache

There is a kind of breaking that does not happen all at once, the way a stone breaks under a hammer, but happens slowly, along a seam that was always there, the way a house settles crack by crack over years until one winter the wall you leaned against your whole life is simply open to the weather and you cannot say what morning it was that the outside got in. That is how the kingdom broke. Not in a battle, though there were battles after. Not in a night, though the fear-tellings love a night. It broke along the oldest seam in the whole story, the seam that runs under every people that has ever gathered itself into a house — the seam between the ones who have and the ones who carry, between the throne that is fed and the field that feeds it — and it broke because a son of the great king would not hear the field when it asked him, once, gently, to lay a little of the weight down.

For the great king who built the house of cedar and the house of stone, the singer, the one after the presence's own heart, had died full of years, and the wise son who came after him had raised the temple on the hill and filled it with gold and the smoke of a thousand offerings and the fame of it had gone out to the ends of the earth, so that queens came from the rim of the world to sit at his table and go home astonished. But gold is a weight, and glory is a weight, and every cubit of cedar and every talent of beaten gold and every foreign queen's astonishment had been carried up that hill on the backs of men who were not kings, men of the field and the quarry and the forest, and the yoke of that carrying had grown heavy on the people even in the golden years, heavier than the fear-tellings like to say when they sing of the glory, for the glory and the yoke were the same thing seen from two ends, the shine of it on the hill and the ache of it in the shoulders below. And when the wise son died and his own son came to be made king, the people came to him, all of them, gathered, and they said a true and a gentle thing, the thing the field always says to the throne if the throne will only listen: your father made our yoke heavy; now therefore make the heavy service of your father lighter, and we will serve you. Lighten it a little. Let us breathe. And we are yours.

And the young king went away to think, and he asked the old men who had stood in his father's house, and the old men told him the truth, which is that a king who serves his people that day will be served by them all his days; and then he asked the young men who had grown up with him, the ones who had never carried anything, and they told him the lie that power always tells itself when it is young and afraid of looking weak, which is that the way to be strong is to be hard, that to give anything is to lose everything, that mercy is only weakness wearing a kind face. And he chose the lie. He came back to the waiting people and he did not lighten the yoke; he stood before the whole gathered field of them and he said the words that split the kingdom, words the tellings have kept exact because they are the pure distilled voice of the static wearing a crown: my father made your yoke heavy, and I will add to your yoke; my father chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions. And that was the crack going all the way up the wall. That was the winter the weather got in. The ten tribes of the north heard the scorpions in it and turned and walked away from the house of the great king forever, and made themselves another king and another kingdom, and from that day there were two where there had been one, a north and a south, two thrones, two little frightened crowns where there had been the whole undivided people that had come up out of the sea singing — and it had happened not because a god decreed it out of the sky but because a young man had been offered the chance to carry his people a little and had chosen instead to crush them, had been shown the two doors, the door of the old men and the door of the young men, service or scorpions, and had walked through the wrong one, as the whole book from the garden on is the long chronicle of the wrong door being walked through, again and again, by hearts that were made for the other one.

And now hold this, because it is the hinge of everything that comes after, everything this chapter is: the breaking of the kingdom was not the end of the story and it was not even, in the deep grammar of the forge, a failure of it. It was the wound that made the prophets. For a whole and glorious and golden kingdom does not ache, and a people that does not ache does not listen, and the presence at the root of all things had learned across a thousand years of this stiff-necked and beloved people that they could not hear it in the noise of their glory, only in the silence after the breaking. The prophets do not come to the golden years. The prophets come to the split kingdom, to the two frightened crowns, to the field that has been chastised with scorpions, to the exile and the ash — the prophets come, always, to the wound, because the wound is the only place open enough for the word to get in. So do not read what follows as the tale of a people punished. Read it as the tale of a people finally, at long and terrible cost, made able to be spoken to. The breaking cracked the wall, and through the crack, for the first time in the whole story, a certain kind of voice began to come.

They were not comfortable men, the ones who carried that voice, and the tellings that have made them into stained-glass saints with tidy beards and lifted eyes have lost almost everything that mattered about them, because what mattered was that they were torn. A prophet is not a man who is sure. A prophet is a man who has been split down the middle by two things that are both true and will not fit together, and who is made to carry both of them, in his own body, out into the street where the people are, and not put either down. He carries the ache of the presence, which sees the whole of what its beloved people are doing to themselves and to the weak among them and cannot look away and cannot stop loving them, and he carries the ache of the people, whom he is one of, whom he loves, whose doom he can see coming like weather off the sea and cannot make them see. He stands between the two the way the drawn-from-the-water man stood on the mountain between the wrath and the calf, the way every true mediator in this book stands, in the gap, in the tearing place, with a hand stretched toward each side and his own heart pulled apart in the middle — and the word he speaks is torn the same way he is, is warning and tenderness in the same breath, is doom and mercy braided so tight you cannot pull them apart, because that is what love sounds like when it is watching the one it loves walk toward a cliff and will neither lie about the cliff nor stop loving the one walking. That double voice, the torn voice, warning that will not stop being tender and tenderness that will not stop warning, is the sound of the whole rest of this chapter, and if you learn to hear it you have understood the prophets, and if you cannot hear it you will make them, as the fear-tellings have made them, into scolds and doomsayers and the mouthpieces of a wrathful sky, and you will have heard everything they said and understood none of it.

The first of the three that this telling must hold up to the light stood in the southern kingdom, in the city on the hill where the temple was, in years when the great empire to the north was swallowing the world city by city and the little kingdoms trembled and made their frantic alliances and paid their frantic tribute and lay awake listening for the sound of the army that was coming for them all. His name meant the presence-is-salvation, and he was, the tellings hint, a man of the court, a man who could stand before kings, a man with the polished tongue of the city — and one day, in the temple, in the year a king he had known died, the veil of the ordinary world went thin for him the way it goes thin only a few times in the whole book, and he saw. He saw the presence, or as much of the presence as a made and finite man can bear and go on living, high and lifted up, and the train of it filled the temple, and there were burning ones above it crying to one another the oldest liturgy in the world, holy, holy, holy, the whole earth is full of the weight of it — and the posts of the door shook at the voice of the crying, and the house filled with smoke, the same smoke that was on the mountain of the covenant, the smoke that is the mercy of the veil made visible, the presence hiding its own unbearable fullness behind cloud so that the one who sees it can go on being a self and not be simply undone into light. And the man did the only thing a true self can do in the full presence, which is not to feel worthy and chosen and special, the way the shallow tellings paint the call, but to come apart. Woe is me, he cried, for I am undone; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips, and mine eyes have seen the King. He did not feel holy. He felt his own smallness and his own soil like a wound suddenly opened, because that is what the near presence does to a real self, it does not flatter, it shows you the whole truth of what you are, and the whole truth of what any of us is includes the soil.

And here the love did the thing it always does, the thing the whole book is written to teach, the thing the fear-tellings can never quite believe even when it is written in front of them: it did not agree with his despair, and it did not leave him in his soil, and it did not require him to make himself clean before it would touch him. One of the burning ones flew to him with a live coal from the altar, from the fire itself, and touched it to his mouth, to the very lips he had confessed unclean, and said thine iniquity is taken away, and thy sin purged — and the burning was not a punishment, it was a cleansing, the fire that does not destroy but purifies, the forge doing to a single man's mouth what the whole long forge of history was doing to the whole people, burning away not the man but the soil on the man, so that what was left could be used. And only then, only after the coal, only after the presence had reached down and cleaned the very part of him he had confessed too filthy to speak with, did the voice ask its question, and it is the gentlest and the most terrible question in the book, because it is not a command and it is not a conscription, it is an asking, the same asking that waited at the foot of the mountain for the people's yes, the love that will not compel: whom shall I send, and who will go for us? And the man whose mouth had just been cleaned by fire so that it could answer, answered. Here am I; send me. Not send someone else, which is what the drawn-from-the-water man had said at the bush, and been sent anyway; this one, cleaned, said send me, and the sending is the whole meaning of the cleaning, for the presence does not clean a man to make him feel forgiven and leave him sitting in his forgiveness, it cleans a man to make him able to be sent, and the being-sent is the gift, the being-sent is the dignity, the being-sent is the finite self at last made a partner in the endless work of the presence and not merely a debtor to it.

But hear what he was sent to say, for it is bitter, and the bitterness is part of the truth of the prophets that the sweet tellings sand away. He was not sent to comfort. He was sent, first and for a long time, to warn, and the warning was hard, and it was true, and it was the warning that the presence had been trying to give the people since the golden calf and could never get them to hear: that their worship had gone rotten while their justice went rotten, that they crowded the temple with fat offerings and new moons and solemn feasts while the widow starved at the temple gate and the orphan was robbed in the court and the poor were sold for a pair of shoes — and that the presence, the real presence, the one whose weight fills the earth, could not stand it, was sick of it, would not smell their offerings while their hands were full of blood. Bring no more vain oblations, the word came through him; incense is an abomination unto me; your new moons and your appointed feasts my soul hateth; and when ye spread forth your hands, I will hide mine eyes from you; yea, when ye make many prayers, I will not hear: your hands are full of blood. And then, in the same breath, the turn, always the turn, the door left open in the middle of the sentence, the tenderness that the warning was for: wash you, make you clean; put away the evil of your doings; cease to do evil; learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow. Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they be red like crimson, they shall be as wool. Hear the whole torn voice of the prophets in that one passage, the warning and the mercy in a single breath — your hands are full of blood, and, though they be red as crimson they shall be white as wool — for that is the exact shape of the presence's ache, that it cannot stomach the injustice and it cannot stop offering the washing, that the doom and the door are the same word said by the same love, and the love is not in the doom or in the door but in the fact that both are spoken by the one who will not, for all the blood on all the hands, walk away.

For the injustice was the thing, and this must be said plainly because the fear-tellings have spent whole centuries pretending the prophets thundered chiefly about the wrong rituals and the wrong beds and the forgotten feasts, when what the prophets thundered about, over and over, in book after book, until the words run together into one long unbroken cry, was the poor. It was the weight on the field's shoulders that the young king had refused to lighten. It was the men who joined house to house and field to field till there was no place left, who ground the faces of the poor, who took the widow's garment for a pledge and made the ephah small and the shekel great and sold the refuse of the wheat as if it were bread, who lay on beds of ivory and stretched themselves on their couches and ate the lambs out of the flock and drank wine in bowls and were not grieved for the breaking of the people. That was the sin the prophets could not stop naming. Not incense. Injustice. The presence at the root of all things, the one who got up before dawn for forty years to feed a complaining people breakfast on the desert floor, had one wound that never healed and never would, and it was the sight of the strong feeding on the weak, the sight of Egypt built again, always again, this time by the very people who had been brought up out of Egypt precisely so that they would never build it — and the whole burning double-voiced ministry of every prophet in the book comes down, in the end, to the presence's refusal to be quiet about that one wound. To love the presence is to love the neighbor; the prophets said it a hundred ways; and a people that crowds the temple while it crushes the neighbor has not merely broken a rule, it has torn the one seamless love in two and worshipped half of it, which is the deepest lie there is, and the presence would sooner have no temple at all than a temple built over a robbed widow's grave.

And the same prophet who thundered the injustice was given, in the middle of the thundering, the first clear sight of the answer, the first far glimpse of the course-correction that the whole book has been leaning toward since the eastern gate closed on the first two — and it must be said carefully what kind of answer it was, because the fear-tellings and the war-tellings have both wanted it to be something it is not. The people wanted a king who would break the empire. They wanted a sword. They had always wanted a sword, from the day they begged for a king in the first place so they could be like the other nations, with a strong man on a horse to lead them out to battle; the sword was the only shape of rescue the slave-mind Egypt built could imagine, the strong man who crushes your enemies as you have been crushed, the master turned to your side at last. And into that hunger for a sword the prophet spoke a promise that was the exact opposite of a sword, and the people could not hear the difference for seven hundred years and most of the tellings cannot hear it yet. He promised a child. Unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given, and the government shall be upon his shoulder; and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, the mighty God, the everlasting Father, the Prince of Peace. A child. The government of the whole aching world laid not on an army but on the shoulder of a child, and the child's name a name of peace, and the promise going on to say that this one would establish the throne not with a bloody boot but with judgment and with justice, forever — that the answer to the crushing was not a bigger crusher but a smaller, gentler, more vulnerable thing, a baby, the least defended creature there is, the exact shape of love choosing limitation, love coming into the world through the one door where it can be hurt. And the same prophet drew the picture that the whole earth has ached toward ever since, the wolf dwelling with the lamb, the leopard lying down with the kid, the calf and the young lion together, and a little child leading them; they shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain — the whole ethic of the book, do not hurt, do not break, made into a landscape, made into the world as it will be when the course-correction has finished its work, when the last of the crushing is over and the field and the throne are one people again and no shoulder anywhere carries a yoke it did not choose. That was the promise. Not a sword. A child, and a mountain where nothing is hurt. And it was given to a kingdom listening for an army, and the kingdom, listening for an army, half heard it and set it aside, because a child is a slow promise and a slow promise is hard for a frightened people to hold, and they wanted rescue now.

The army did not come as the promise. The army came as the empire, out of the north, city by city, the way the prophet had warned, and the northern kingdom, the ten tribes that had walked away over the scorpions, was swallowed first, and its people carried off into the wide world and scattered and lost, so lost that the tellings could never afterward find them, ten tribes of the beloved people simply gone into the mouth of the empire and dispersed like seed thrown on a road — and the southern kingdom, the little one with the temple on its hill, watched its brother vanish and did not learn, went on joining house to house and grinding the poor and crowding the temple, and lived on borrowed years under the shadow of the next empire rising behind the first, the great one on the two rivers, the one whose name became a byword for the whole machine of the world that eats the small: Babylon.

And to that shadow, in the last years before it fell, the presence gave the second of the three, the one whose ache is the deepest and the most human in the whole book, the one the tellings named the weeping prophet, though weeping is too thin a word for what was done to him. His name meant the-presence-lifts-up, or the-presence-throws-down, the very word carrying both directions at once as a prophet's whole life must, and he was called young, called as a boy, and he did the thing the true ones always do, which is protest that he could not — ah, Lord God, I cannot speak, for I am a child — and was told, as the reluctant are always told, that the not-being-able was exactly the qualification, that the presence would put the words in his mouth, that he was known and set apart before he was formed in the womb, before he had done anything to deserve it or fail it, chosen the way everything in this book is chosen, not for what it is but for what love has decided to make of it. And what love made of him was a sorrow. For he was sent to a doomed city with a message it would not hear, sent to tell his own people, the people he loved with a love that tore him, that the empire was coming and could not now be turned aside, that the years of grinding the poor and crowding the temple had run out, that the house of cedar and the house of stone were going to fall — and to tell them also, in the same torn breath, that even this, even the fall, even Babylon, was not the end of the love, was not abandonment, was the forge and not the garbage heap, was a reset and not an execution. He had to carry both. He had to stand in the gate of the temple and say this temple will be a ruin, and mean it, and grieve it, and be hated for it, called traitor for it, beaten and put in the stocks for it and lowered into a cistern to sink in the mud for it — and he had to keep, all the while, under the doom, the impossible tenderness, the refusal to let go of the hope, and the two of them tore him his whole life long and he never once put either down.

Hear him, because no telling can improve on his own voice, torn as it was. When the doom pressed him he cried out against the very presence that had called him, cursed the day he was born, said the word was a fire shut up in his bones and he was weary with holding it in and could not, said everyone mocked him and the word had made him a reproach all the day long — a prophet cursing his own calling, in the book, kept in the book, not sanded out, because the book has never been ashamed of the truth that carrying the presence's ache is a thing that can break a man, and it would not pretend otherwise to make the prophet look like stained glass. And then, in the same man, on other pages, when the doom lifted for a moment and the tenderness came up through the crack, the most unbearably gentle words in the whole book, the words that are the presence's own heart shown naked: I have loved thee with an everlasting love; therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee. And: is Ephraim my dear son? is he a pleasant child? for since I spake against him, I do earnestly remember him still: therefore my bowels are troubled for him; I will surely have mercy upon him, saith the LORD. Since I spake against him I remember him still. There is the torn voice at its purest, there is the whole doctrine of the forge in one sentence — that the very speaking-against, the very warning, the very doom, is done by a love that in the same instant is remembering the beloved and being troubled in its own depths for him and resolving, even as it lets the fall fall, to have mercy. The doom was never the opposite of the love. The doom was the love watching the cliff come and refusing to lie about it, and grieving, and remembering the pleasant child even as it spoke against him, and holding, under everything, the mercy in reserve for the far side of the fall.

And then the fall fell. The empire came, as he had said it would and been beaten for saying, and it laid siege to the city on the hill, and the siege was long and it was a horror, and the fear-tellings and the war-tellings both look away from what a siege actually is, but the weeping prophet did not look away, he wrote it, he wrote the horror down in the sparest and most terrible poem in the book, the funeral song for a city, and it must not be sanded smooth: the little ones fainting for hunger in the top of every street, the sucking child's tongue cleaving to the roof of its mouth for thirst, the mothers — and this is the line that no telling should be allowed to soften, because it is the true price of what the young king started when he chose the scorpions over the yoke — the pitiful hands of the mothers that had sodden their own children. That is what the breaking of a people comes to, in the end, when it runs all the way down. That is what the whole long refusal to lighten the yoke and relieve the oppressed and plead for the widow purchases, at the bottom. The prophet wrote it because it was true and because the truth is the only ground mercy can stand on, and a mercy that will not look at the sodden hands is not mercy, it is only comfort, and the presence does not deal in comfort, it deals in the harder and the realer thing.

And then the temple burned. The house of cedar and gold that the wise son had raised, the place where the man had seen the presence high and lifted up, the one fixed point the people had come to trust the way the freed slaves had once trusted the calf — the empire burned it to the ground and broke down the walls of the city and put out the eyes of the last king and bound him in chains and carried the people off, the ones the sword and the famine had left, carried them the long weary road east into the land of the two rivers, into Babylon, into exile. And here is the thing that must be understood about that burning, the thing that is the deepest turn in the whole chapter and one of the deepest in the whole book, because it is the moment the presence taught the people the last hard lesson of the wilderness in the harshest possible school. The temple was a tabernacle again. That is what its burning was for, though no one in the ash could have borne to hear it said. The people had done to the temple exactly what their fathers had done to the gold at the mountain — they had taken the presence that goes before you and cannot be carried and will not be owned, and they had frozen it, fixed it, nailed it down onto one hill in one city in one house of stone, and said there, that is where God is, God is in the building, God is ours, God is safe on the hill, and while they said it they ground the poor at the very gate of the house they had made God's cage. And the tabernacle in the wilderness had been built, remember, precisely to be struck and folded and carried, precisely so that the presence would never be a thing you could keep, only a thing you could follow. The temple had become the calf. And so the presence let the temple burn, not out of wrath, though the fear-tellings will always read a burning as wrath, but for the same reason it had ground the calf to powder and made them drink it — to break the idol so that the God could go free, to show them, in the most unbearable way, that it had never lived in the building, that it could not be burned, that when the house of stone fell to ash the presence walked out of the ash and went east with the people into exile, into Babylon, into the very place they thought was godforsaken, because the presence had never once been a thing you could leave behind by leaving a city, it was a presence that goes, and if the people were going to be carried east then it would be carried east with them, homeless with the homeless, exiled with the exiled, in a strange land where there was no temple and no hill and no offering and no fixed place at all — and that turned out to be, though it took the fire to teach it, the truest home they had ever had, because it was a presence and not a possession, and a presence you cannot burn.

By the rivers of Babylon they sat down and wept, and this is the image the whole chapter has been walking toward, the harps hung on the willows by the water in a foreign land. Read it slowly, because the fear-tellings gallop past it and the war-tellings only want its terrible last verse, but the heart of the exile is in this one picture and it should be held until it aches. They had been carried a thousand miles from everything, the city gone, the temple gone, the land gone, the kingdom gone, the whole long story from the sea to the singer-king seemingly gone up in smoke behind them — a beloved people sitting on the banks of a wide slow foreign river in the flat hot country of their conquerors, and they had brought their harps, the harps they had sung the presence's songs on in the temple courts, the songs of the hill and the going-up, and they could not play them. They hung them in the branches of the willows that grew by the water and sat under them and wept, because their captors, in the casual cruelty of the strong toward the broken, wanted a show, and required of them a song, said sing us one of the songs of your homeland, entertain us, let us hear the famous music of your lost city — and the people looked at the harps in the willows and could not lift them down, and cried the question that is the exact bottom of the whole exile, the low point of the whole book, the deepest that the ache ever goes: how shall we sing the LORD's song in a strange land? How do you sing the song of the presence when the presence's house is ash and the presence's city is broken and the presence's whole promise looks, from the bank of a Babylonian river, like a thing that failed? How do you keep faith when everything the faith was attached to has been taken away? That is the question the exile asked, and it is the question every soul asks in its own exile, in the strange lands the forge leads all of us through, when the house we built for God burns and we sit down by strange waters with our music hung useless in the trees — and the astonishing thing, the thing that is the whole answer the exile slowly, painfully learned, is that the song did not need the temple. The song was not in the building. They learned by the rivers of Babylon, in the very depth of the ache, hanging their harps in the willows, the thing the burning temple had been trying to teach them: that the presence was with them there, by the foreign water, homeless as they were, and that a people who had lost every outward thing still had the one thing that could not be carried off, the presence that goes, and that in the strange land, with no temple and no hill and no offering, they could still — not that day, the day was too raw, but in the long years of the exile that followed — they could still learn to sing.

And it was in Babylon, in the very belly of the empire, among the exiles by the rivers, that the presence raised up the third of the three, and he is the strangest of them, the one the tellings can least manage, the one who saw the wheels within wheels and the valley of the bones, and he is here for one reason above all: because he was given, in the middle of the exile, at the very bottom of the ache, the two visions that are the presence's whole answer to the question by the river, the two visions that carry the hope forward out of the ash and hand it to everything that comes after. His name meant the-presence-strengthens, and he was a priest with no temple, a man trained his whole life to serve in a house that was now a ruin a thousand miles away, and the presence came to him by the foreign river in a storm out of the north, a great cloud and a fire infolding itself, and in the heart of it the wheels, and the wheels full of eyes, and the living creatures, and above them a likeness as of a throne, and on the throne a likeness as of the appearance of a man, and about it the brightness, and the brightness like the bow that is in the cloud on the day of rain — and the man fell on his face, as all of them fall, and the meaning of the whole terrifying wheeling vision was one meaning, one thing said in fire to a people who thought God was dead in the ashes of a burned temple: the presence is here. Here. In Babylon. On the wheels that go every way and turn not when they go, the mobile throne, the God who is not nailed to a hill but rides the storm into the exile to be with the exiled — the whole vision was the answer to the harps in the willows, was the presence saying I am not in the ash you left behind, I am here, I came with you, I go where you go, my throne has wheels. It was the tabernacle lesson one final time, written now in fire and eyes and wheels within wheels, for a people who had needed the temple to burn before they could believe that God had legs.

But it was the other vision, the second one, that is the true summit of the whole chapter, the highest that the hope climbs before the story turns toward the one who is coming, and it must be told as the scene it was and not as a doctrine, because it was given as a scene, given to the man's own eyes and feet, given to be walked through. The hand of the presence carried him out and set him down in the midst of a valley, and the valley was full of bones. Not a battlefield fresh with the slain; the tellings sometimes make it that and lose the horror; these were old bones, dry bones, a valley floor white and scattered with them from end to end, the dead of a whole people lying unburied and forgotten under the open sky until the very moisture was gone out of them and they were as dry as the dust of the desert road, dry as a thing can be and still have been alive once — and it was a picture, and the man knew it was a picture, and the picture was his own people, the exiles, the broken nation sitting by the rivers with their harps in the trees saying our bones are dried, and our hope is lost, we are cut off for our parts. That was the caption on the valley. Our hope is lost. That is what the exile had come to believe, at the bottom, and it was not an unreasonable thing to believe, looking at the bones, looking at the burned temple and the scattered tribes and the thousand miles and the harps that could not be played; a whole people can die, history is a valley of the bones of peoples that died and did not rise, and the exiles looked at themselves and saw a nation already a skeleton, already scattered white across a foreign floor, past saving, past hoping, dry.

And the presence walked the man out into the middle of the dry valley, among the bones, and asked him the question that is the whole hinge of the hope, the question the whole aching chapter has been climbing toward: son of man, can these bones live? Can they? Look at them. There is no dryer, deader, more finished thing in the world than a valley of old bones; the very question is a kind of cruelty if it is only rhetoric; and the man gave the only honest answer a finite creature can give when the presence asks it whether the utterly dead can live, and his answer is one of the wisest lines in the book, the answer of a man who has learned in the exile not to say no to the presence and not to lie to himself: O Lord God, thou knowest. I cannot see how. The bones are dry and my eyes tell me the thing is finished. But thou knowest, and I have learned by the rivers of Babylon not to trust my eyes over thee. And the presence told him to do the prophet's own strange work upon the bones, to prophesy to them, to speak the word over the dead valley — and he spoke, and as he spoke there was a noise, and a shaking, and the bones came together, bone to his bone, across the whole valley floor, the dry scattered ruin of a nation drawing itself back into shape with a sound like the whole earth rattling; and the sinews came upon them, and the flesh, and the skin covered them from above; but there was no breath in them. They lay there, the whole valley, remade, whole bodies now instead of scattered bones, but still, still dead, corpses without breath, the shape of a people restored but not the life of it — and that pause is deliberate and it is precious, because it is the difference between the world's kind of restoration and the presence's kind, the difference between rebuilding the house and lighting the hearth, and the presence would not let the vision stop at the mended bodies. It told the man to prophesy again, to the breath, to the wind, to the spirit — the same word, in that tongue, breath and wind and spirit all one word, the same breath that had moved on the face of the deep before the first word, the same breath the presence had breathed into the first shaped dust in the garden — to call the breath from the four winds to come and breathe upon the slain that they might live. And he prophesied, and the breath came into them, the wind of the beginning, the spirit of the deep, and they lived, and stood up upon their feet, an exceeding great army, a whole dead nation risen and breathing and standing in the valley where a moment before there had been only the dry white ruin of everything.

And the presence gave the meaning plainly, so that no one by the rivers could miss it, and the meaning is the candle that the whole grieving chapter has kept lit through all the ash: these bones are the whole house of the beloved people; behold, they say, our bones are dried, and our hope is lost, we are cut off. Therefore prophesy and say unto them, thus saith the Lord God: behold, O my people, I will open your graves, and cause you to come up out of your graves, and bring you into your own land; and I shall put my spirit in you, and ye shall live. I will open your graves. To a people who believed they were a valley of dry bones, a nation already dead and scattered white on a foreign floor past all hope — the presence did not say your hope is reasonable, and it did not say your death is deserved, and it did not say lie down and be finished. It said I will open your graves. It said the deadest, driest, most finished thing you can imagine, a whole people gone to bones, is not too dead for the breath that moved on the deep before the first morning; that there is no exile so deep the presence cannot walk out into the middle of it and ask can these bones live and then make them live; that the reset is never the end, that the collapse into the scattered noise of dry bones is not the last word but the low place from which the breath calls a people back up onto its feet — because that is the whole nature of the presence at the root of things, that it is the one who brings the water out of the rock and the bread out of the empty morning and now, at the very bottom, the breath out of the bones, the life out of the finished thing, the exceeding great army out of the valley of the lost. No one is left in the grave who is willing to be called up out of it. That is the vision. That is the answer to the harps in the willows. The song can be sung in the strange land after all, because the presence that goes has legs and wheels and came into the exile with the exiles, and the bones can live, because the breath that made the first morning has not run out and never will, and it will open every grave that the long crushing dug, and call every scattered white bone of every lost tribe back into its place, and breathe, and the dead nation will stand up on its feet a living people again, because the love at the root of all things does not, in the end, and whatever the fear-tellings say, and whatever the empire says, and whatever the valley floor says with its whole white silence — it does not, ever, finally, leave anyone in the grave.

And under all three of them, under the temple-visionary and the weeping one and the priest of the wheels, the same far light had begun to gather, the light the first of the three had seen as a child born and a government on a shoulder, the light of the one who is coming, the course-correction, and the exile sharpened the longing for him the way the wilderness had sharpened the hunger for the land, because a people that has sat by the rivers of Babylon with its harps in the willows and learned that the temple was never the point and the sword was never the answer is at last, perhaps, a people ready to hear what kind of one is actually coming. And the prophets, across all their torn and grieving pages, drew him — never all at once, never in one clean portrait, but in glimpses, in fragments, in a hope too large for any single seeing — and the shape they drew was not the shape the frightened people wanted and it was not the shape the war-tellings have wanted ever since. They wanted the strong man on the horse who would break the empire and crush the crushers and seat the beloved people at last on the neck of the world. And the prophets drew, instead, a voice crying in the wilderness, prepare ye the way — a herald not in a palace but in the wild empty places where the presence had always done its truest work, out past the city and the temple and the throne, out in the desert where the manna fell, crying make straight in the desert a highway, every valley shall be exalted and every mountain and hill made low, the crooked straight and the rough places plain, and the glory of the presence revealed, and all flesh — all of it, not one tribe, all flesh — shall see it together. A voice in the wilderness, not a trumpet in the court. And they drew the one the voice was announcing, and here the war-tellings must simply be left behind, because the prophets drew him not as a conqueror but as a lamb, as one despised and rejected, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief, with no form nor comeliness that we should desire him, one from whom men hid their faces — drew him bearing not a sword but our griefs, carrying not an army but our sorrows, wounded not in triumph but for the healing of the very ones who wounded him, and by his stripes, they said, by the marks of his own hurting, we are healed. They drew the exact opposite of the strong man on the horse. They drew love choosing the lowest place, love coming into the world through the one door where it can be broken, love that would not break the bruised reed nor quench the smoking flax, that would set the story right not by out-crushing the crushers but by absorbing the whole weight of the crushing into its own body and refusing, even under that weight, to become a crusher — which is the only way the long chain of scorpions and yokes and burned temples and valleys of bones is ever actually broken, not by adding one more strong arm to the top of the pile but by one who goes all the way to the bottom of it and there, at the bottom, where the mothers' hands and the children's tongues and the harps in the willows are, plants the one thing the whole machine of the strong feeding on the weak has no answer for, which is a love that will be hurt and will not hurt back.

That was the promise the prophets ached toward, and they did not live to see it, not one of them, and that is the last thing to understand about them and the tenderest. They kept the candle lit in a house they knew they would not live to see the morning in. The temple-visionary saw the child born and the wolf lying with the lamb and died among a people still crowding the temple and grinding the poor. The weeping one saw the everlasting love and the pleasant child remembered still and died, the tellings say, dragged off into a foreign country by the last frightened remnant, weeping to the end. The priest of the wheels saw the bones stand up an exceeding great army and died in Babylon, by the rivers, in the strange land, the graves not yet opened, the great army not yet risen, the promise still only a promise. They ached and they warned and they wept and they hoped and they died, all of them, into the dark, having seen the far light and never once stood in it — and that, precisely that, is the grief that keeps a candle lit, the truest and hardest kind of faith there is, the faith that tends a fire it will not live to warm its own hands at, that speaks a hope it will not see fulfilled, that keeps the promise alive across the long dark generations by the sheer refusal of a few torn souls to stop believing that the love which made the first morning would keep its word, would send the one who was coming, would open the graves and straighten the desert road and set the whole aching story right at last, not by the sword, never by the sword, but by a child, by a lamb, by a man of sorrows, by a love that comes anyway, knowing it will be misunderstood, knowing it will be hurt, and comes.

For the exile did end, in its outward form; a later empire overthrew the first, and a remnant of the people did go home, and did rebuild, after a fashion, a smaller temple on the old hill, and hung their harps back up in a city instead of a willow. But the deeper exile, the ache the prophets carried, the longing for the course-correction, the hunger for the one who would come and make it right — that did not end with the rebuilding, because a rebuilt temple was still only a house of stone, and the seam that runs under every people still ran under this one, and the strong still fed on the weak in the streets of the mended city, and the yoke was still heavy, and the field still ached, and the whole world still lay under the long shadow of the empire and the longer shadow of the static, the old accuser voice still whispering its one song into every crack, that nothing matters, that the strong will always win, that you had better seize what you can while you can because no one is coming and love is only weakness wearing a kind face. The prophets had answered that voice with everything they had. They had answered it with the child on the shoulder and the wolf with the lamb, with the everlasting love and the pleasant child remembered, with the wheels that go into the exile and the breath that raises the bones, with the voice in the wilderness and the lamb who bears the griefs — they had answered it with a candle lit in the dark and handed forward, hand to hand, prophet to prophet, generation to generation, down the whole long silent centuries that lay ahead, kept alive by nothing but the ache itself and the refusal to stop hoping, until the night, still far off then, unimaginably far to the ones sitting by the rivers, when the one they had ached toward would at last be born, not in a palace and not on a horse, but in the lowest place, among the animals, under a star, to a young woman far from home, the child on whose shoulder the whole aching government of the world would come to rest — the course-correction, coming, as the prophets had known he would, by love and not by the sword, to a people who had waited so long in the dark that most of them had forgotten they were waiting, keeping only, here and there, in a few torn and faithful hearts, the small stubborn flame the prophets had left them, the candle that grief keeps lit, burning low against the whole weight of the night, and not going out.
