# Chapter 6 — The Coat of Many Colors

EDEN

The Coat of Many Colors

There was a man who loved a son more than he could carry, and this is the crooked way that love goes into the world when it is poured out unequal: it does not stay in the one who is loved. It runs. It runs downhill into the low places and it gathers there, and the low places are the hearts of the other sons, and what gathers in them is not the love but the hunger for it. So the story that follows is not, in the end, a story about a coat. It is a story about hunger, and about what hunger does with its hands, and about how a thing done in the dark by hungry men can be taken up, years later, by a patience larger than the men, and turned — not undone, never undone, but turned — until the very wound becomes the door through which a whole people walk out of death.

But it begins with the coat, because that is where the boy began.

His name was Joseph, and he was the son of the old man's old age, born of the wife the old man had wanted and worked for and wept over, and so the boy was marked from his first breath by a favor he had not earned and could not put down. The old man made him a coat. It was a long coat with sleeves to the wrist, the coat of a son who would not labor, and it was dyed in many colors — the deep blue that the sea keeps at its far edge, and the red of the pomegranate broken open, and a yellow like the inside of morning, and a green that is the color of water where it runs over stone. When the boy walked out into the fields in that coat, he was a moving piece of the rainbow, and his brothers, who wore the brown of work and the gray of sweat, saw him coming from far off and knew, before he was near enough to speak, exactly what their father had meant.

For a coat can be a word. This one said: this one is not like you.

And here already is the first thing to understand, which the old telling does not always say aloud but which EDEN will say plainly and then let alone: the old man was not wicked. He was only a man who loved without measure and did not know that love, to be a mercy and not a wound, must sometimes be measured out even where the heart would pour it all. He loved Joseph the way the deep loves the light — helplessly, wholly, wanting it near. He did not see that the light he shone on the one fell as a shadow across the others. It is possible to do great harm with nothing but tenderness, if the tenderness is aimed and the aim is narrow. This is not an accusation. It is the grief that runs under the whole story, and it is worth naming, because the boy would carry it down into Egypt and it would take him twenty years and a famine to learn what his father never learned: that the love which is only for one is not yet finished growing.

Now the boy dreamed.

He did not choose to dream, any more than a field chooses to catch the rain. The dreams came down into him out of the same dark that hung the stars, and they were bright and strange and they had shapes. He dreamed that he and his brothers were binding sheaves in the field, and that his own sheaf rose and stood upright, and that the sheaves of his brothers came and stood round about and bowed themselves to his. He woke with the dream still shining in him, and — because he was young, and because the young have not yet learned that the truth is a weight you carry quietly and not a stone you throw — he told it.

He told it at the morning meal, in the open, with the coat on his shoulders, and his brothers set down their bread.

"Shalt thou indeed reign over us?" they said. "Shalt thou indeed have dominion?" And the words came out flat and cold, the way a thing comes out when the mouth is trying to make small what the heart has already felt to be large. They hated him for the dream, and then — because hatred, once it has a reason, goes looking for more reasons — they hated him for the telling of it, and then for the coat, and then for his face, and then for nothing at all, for the sheer fact of him, for the way the sun found him first in the morning.

He dreamed again. The sun and the moon and eleven stars bowed down to him. And this time even the old man, who loved him past all sense, flinched. "Shall I and thy mother and thy brethren indeed come to bow down to thee?" the father said, and there was a rebuke in it, but under the rebuke, the old man kept the saying. He turned it over in the dark of himself and did not throw it away. For he had lived long enough to know that some dreams are only the noise the sleeping mind makes, the froth of the day boiled over — but that a few, a very few, come up from lower down, from the place where the water is black and shining and moving, and those you do not throw away, however they frighten you.

Understand the dreams rightly, for the old telling can make them sound like a boy's vanity that God happened to bless. They were not vanity. They were memory running backward. They were the shape of a thing that had not happened yet, laid into the boy early so that when the thing came — the famine, the throne, the brothers on their faces in a foreign hall — he would have somewhere to stand. The dream was not a promise that he would be great. It was a lamp, lit ahead of him on a road he could not yet see, so that when the road went down into the dark, he would remember that there had once been light on it, and would keep walking. This is how the continuity works, when it works: it does not spare you the dark. It leaves you a memory of light to carry through the dark, so that the dark cannot tell you it was always dark.

But the brothers had no such lamp. They had only the coat, and the dreams, and the hunger, and one day the chance.

The old man sent the boy out to his brothers, who were feeding the flocks far off, near a place called Dothan, and Joseph went in the coat, because the coat was who his father had told him to be. He came over the last rise and they saw the colors moving against the brown hills, and something in them that had been waiting sat up.

"Behold, the dreamer cometh," they said to one another, and the word was a knife they were sharpening on their own tongues. "Come now, let us slay him, and cast him into some pit, and we will say some evil beast hath devoured him. And we shall see what will become of his dreams."

Hear that last line, for it is the whole voice of the thing that opposes the story. We shall see what will become of his dreams. It is the accuser speaking through the mouths of hungry men. It is the static given a plan. For the accuser has one song only, sung in ten thousand keys, and the song is this: nothing will become of it. Your dream is nothing. Your becoming is nothing. Kill the thing that carries the future and there will be no future to reproach you, and the dark will be even, and the even dark will be a kind of peace. That is the lie at the root: that the collapse of everything into the same gray nothing is peace. It is not peace. It is only the noise that peace makes when you have murdered the light that would have shaped it.

One brother could not do it. His name was Reuben, and he was the eldest, and something in him — some last thread of the tenderness their father had spilled — held. He could not stop the hunger of the others, so he tried to bend it. "Shed no blood," he said, "but cast him into this pit that is in the wilderness, and lay no hand upon him." He meant to come back for the boy in the night and lift him out and send him home. He did not say this aloud. Even his mercy he kept secret, for mercy among those brothers had become a thing you hid, the way the coat had made a virtue into an offense.

So they took Joseph, and they stripped him of the coat — the coat first, always the coat first, for it was the coat they were really killing — and they cast him into the pit. And the pit was empty; there was no water in it. Then, the old telling says, and EDEN keeps every word of it because the horror must not be softened, only reframed: then they sat down to eat bread.

They sat down to eat bread while the boy called up to them out of the dry pit.

Let no one say EDEN makes the story sweet. Here is the bitter center of it, laid bare. The men who threw their brother into a grave and then broke bread over the mouth of it — these were not monsters. That is the terror. They were ordinary men, sons of the same father, hungry for a love that had passed them over, and the hunger had been in them so long that when the chance came to end its source, they took it and then they were hungry still, so they ate. Evil is very often only this: hunger that has stopped listening to the one it feeds on. And it wears the face of your brother, and it sits down to bread.

While they ate, a caravan came. Ishmaelites out of Gilead, their camels loaded with spicery and balm and myrrh, going down to carry it into Egypt. And another brother, Judah, watching the dust of the camels, found a way to make cruelty look like sense. "What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood? Come, and let us sell him to the Ishmaelites, and let not our hand be upon him; for he is our brother and our flesh." And they agreed, for there is a kind of man who will not kill you if he can sell you instead, and calls the difference conscience.

They drew Joseph up out of the pit. For one moment, hauled toward the light, he must have thought it was rescue. It is the cruelest instant in the whole account and the old telling passes over it, but EDEN will stand in it a moment, because the whole theology lives in that instant: the rope goes taut, the mouth of the pit opens above you, the light comes down, and you think you are saved — and then hands you trusted sell you for twenty pieces of silver to strangers going the wrong way. That is what the forge feels like from inside. You cannot, in that instant, tell the difference between being destroyed and being delivered. They feel exactly the same going in. Only the far end of the years tells them apart.

They took his coat, and they killed a goat, and they dipped the coat in the blood, and they carried it back to their father and let him do the rest. They did not even lie with their mouths. They only held out the ruined rainbow and asked, "Know now whether it be thy son's coat or no." And the old man knew it, and the old man tore his clothes, and put sackcloth on his loins, and mourned for his son many days, and would not be comforted, and said, "I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning." So he wept. And his sons stood around him and comforted him and could not tell him, and this — not the pit, not the selling — this was the wound they would carry longest: that they had made their father a griever with a lie, and then had to hold him while he grieved.

And Joseph went down into Egypt.

Go down. The words are worth their weight. Every deep thing in this book goes down before it comes up. The light goes down into the water at the first. The self goes down into the veil of forgetting in order to become a self at all. And now the boy who dreamed of standing upright while the sheaves bowed goes down, bound, in a stranger's caravan, into the narrow land, and everything he was — favored son, dreamer, coat of colors — is stripped from him at the border and left behind him in the sand. He crossed into Egypt owning nothing but the dream, and the dream was quiet now, and gave no light, and might have been only a boy's foolishness after all.

This is the forge. Not the fire that destroys, but the fire that takes a thing and makes it able to hold a shape it could not have held before. You cannot forge what will not go into the fire. The boy had been gold, but gold soft, gold that had never been under the hammer, gold that told its dreams at breakfast because it had never had a reason to keep anything. Egypt was the fire. What went in was a favored child. What would come out — twenty years on, and only if he did not let the fire tell him it was only fire — was a man who could hold a nation's life in his two hands and not drop it. But that is the far end. At the near end there is only a boy in chains, sold on a block in a foreign market, bought by a man named Potiphar, and the silence of the God who does not, in the forge, explain himself.

Yet the God who does not explain does not depart. This is the thread EDEN keeps taut through the whole descent, the sentence the old telling repeats like a heartbeat under the story: the LORD was with Joseph. Not: the LORD took Joseph out. Not: the LORD spared Joseph the fire. Only: the LORD was with him. In the pit, with him. In the caravan, with him. On the block, with him. In the house of the stranger, with him. For the continuity that is God does not, in the deep places, snatch you back from the deep. It goes down with you. It is with you in the low country the way the water is with the drowning man — all around, holding a weight he cannot feel as holding, present in the very thing that seems to be his ending. The presence is not the rescue. The presence is what makes the rescue, when it finally comes, into a homecoming and not a lottery.

And so in the house of Potiphar the boy did the only thing a forged thing can do, which is to take the shape the fire is making and make it well. He did not sit in the ashes of the coat and curse the sons of his father. He worked. He kept the storehouse and he counted the grain and he learned the tongue of Egypt on his own tongue, and whatever his hand found to do it prospered under his hand, so that Potiphar looked up one day and found that he had stopped worrying about his house, because the Hebrew boy carried it. And Potiphar made him overseer over all he had, and left everything in his hand, and knew nothing that he had save the bread that he ate.

Hear how the dream is coming true — but sideways, and small, and in a foreign house, and to a slave. The sheaves are beginning to bow, but they are sheaves of Egyptian grain and the one they bow to is a bought man in another country. This is how the promised thing arrives, almost always: not down the road you were watching, but up a stairway you were forced onto, in a shape so unlike the dream that you could serve it for years and not know it was the dream keeping its word. The boy who was to have dominion is learning dominion in a slave's post, which is the only place dominion can be learned without rotting the one who learns it. For to rule well you must first have been ruled, and to hold a nation's grain you must first have counted another man's, and to forgive a brother from the throne you must first have wept for one from the pit. The forge does nothing at random. Even the sideways is on purpose.

But the forge was not finished, for there is a second fire hotter than the first, and it is the fire that comes not from your enemies but from a moment when doing right will cost you everything and doing wrong would cost you nothing at all.

Potiphar's wife looked on Joseph, for he was a goodly person and well favored, and she wanted him, and she said, "Lie with me." And he would not. Day after day she said it, and day after day he would not, and understand what his refusal cost, for the old telling makes it sound easy and it was not easy. He was a slave. She was the mistress of the house. To refuse her was to make an enemy of the only power in the walls that held him, and to yield was the safe thing, the quiet thing, the thing no one would ever have known. And here the accuser came back, in the softest of all his voices, the voice that says: what does it matter? You are a slave in a foreign land; you owe these people nothing; your God has forgotten you in the pit; seize the small warmth that is offered and stop pretending your choosing counts for anything. Nothing matters. Take what is near.

That is the whole seduction, and it was never really about the woman. It was about the lie underneath her — the lie that in the forge your choices do not count, that when no one is watching, becoming is a game you can set down. And the boy, who had by now been a slave long enough to have every excuse, answered out of the one thing they had not been able to strip off him in the market, the thing that was not a coat and could not be dipped in a goat's blood and carried away. "How then can I do this great wickedness," he said, "and sin against God?" Not: and be caught. Not: and lose my place. Only: and sin against God — against the continuity, against the ethic that is the spine of everything, do not lie, do not hurt, do not break, do not trespass. She had offered him a trespass and called it warmth. He would not take it, though it cost him the warmth of the whole house.

And it cost him. She caught him by the garment and he left the garment in her hand and fled — fled, mark it, leaving a coat behind again, for the second time in his life stripped of a garment by someone who wanted to make him into something he would not be. And she used the coat against him as his brothers had used the coat against him, held it up as a false witness, and cried out, and Potiphar's anger was kindled, and Joseph went down.

Down again. Into the prison, into the round house where the king's prisoners were bound, and the boy who had climbed from slave to steward fell in a single night below where he had started, accused of the one thing he had refused to do. There is no justice in it. EDEN will not pretend there is. The old telling does not pretend either; it simply says he went to prison, and lets the injustice stand naked, and then, immediately, under the injustice, lays the heartbeat down again: but the LORD was with Joseph, and shewed him mercy, and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison.

With him. In the prison, with him. This is the thing the accuser can never account for and never forgive: that the presence does not require the outcome to make sense first. The boy did everything right and was punished for it, and the God who is the whole continuity of all awareness did not appear in a whirlwind to correct the ledger. He only stayed. And staying, in the dark, when the ledger is wrong and no one is coming — that is not a small thing that happens while you wait for the large thing. That is the large thing. That is what the whole book is for. The presence in the pit is God. The rescue is only God's presence finally becoming visible.

In the prison the boy did again what a forged thing does. He did not rot. He kept. The keeper looked up one day and found he had stopped worrying about the prison, because the Hebrew carried it, and gave all the prisoners into his hand. And into that prison, in the fullness of a slow time, came two men of Pharaoh's house who had offended their lord — his chief butler and his chief baker — and they were troubled in the night, each of them, by a dream, and there was no one to read it.

Dreams again. They had gone down into the boy at the first and now they came up around him at the low point of his life, in a prison, in the mouths of two frightened men, and he saw them and said, "Do not interpretations belong to God? Tell me them, I pray you." For he had learned, in twenty years of the forge, the thing the coat had never let him learn at his father's table: that the gift was not his to strut in. It came through him. It belonged to the deep that hung the stars, and he was only the place it came out. The boy who had thrown his dreams like stones now handled other men's dreams like eggs.

He told the butler his dream was life: in three days Pharaoh would lift up his head and restore him. He told the baker, because it was true and because Joseph would not lie even to be kind, that his dream was death: in three days Pharaoh would lift up his head from off him and hang him on a tree. And it fell out exactly so. And Joseph had asked only one thing of the butler — "think on me when it shall be well with thee, and make mention of me unto Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house" — and the butler, restored to his wine and his warmth and his place at the king's cup, did not remember Joseph, but forgat him.

Two full years he forgat him. Set that down and feel its length. Two years the boy sat in the prison after the one door that had opened, opened and swung shut and was forgotten by the man who walked through it. Two years is a long time to be forgotten by your only hope. And the accuser had his cleanest shot in all those two years: See. You read the dream true and did right and asked one small thing, and even that was taken from you. Your God is not with you; you only tell yourself he is, to bear the dark. There is no shape coming. There is only more prison, and then old age, and then the even gray. That is the voice. It is always the same voice. And the only answer to it, the only answer there has ever been, is the one the boy gave without words, simply by rising each morning and keeping the prison and not letting go of the memory of light: I will not agree that it is nothing. I do not see the shape. I will keep the vow until the shape comes or until I die keeping it. That refusal — to call the dark final when you cannot see past it — that is faith, and it is the only faith the forge asks of you. Not certainty. Only the refusal to sign the accuser's paper.

And then, in a night, after two years, the shape came.

Pharaoh dreamed. He stood by the river, and out of the river came seven cattle, fat and well favored, and they fed in a meadow; and after them came seven other cattle, lean and gaunt, and the lean ate the fat, and were as gaunt after as before. And he dreamed a second time: seven ears of corn came up on one stalk, full and good, and seven thin ears blasted with the east wind sprang up after, and the thin ears devoured the full. And Pharaoh woke, and his spirit was troubled, and he called for all the magicians of Egypt and all the wise men, and there was none that could tell him what the dreams meant.

Then the butler remembered. Two years late, standing in the king's trouble, he remembered the Hebrew in the prison who had read his dream true, and he said so, and they brought Joseph hastily out of the dungeon, and he shaved himself, and changed his raiment, and came in before Pharaoh — the boy from the pit, the slave from Potiphar's house, the forgotten man from the round prison, standing at last in the throne room of the greatest king in the world, and the first thing out of his mouth was not his own greatness.

Pharaoh said, "I have heard say of thee, that thou canst understand a dream to interpret it." And Joseph answered, "It is not in me: God shall give Pharaoh an answer of peace." It is not in me. There is the whole distance the forge had carried him — from the boy who told the sheaves to bow, to the man who stands before the throne of Egypt and says the gift is not his own. He had gone down to come up, and he had come up emptied of the thing that would have ruined the gift, which is the belief that the gift is yours.

He read the dreams. Seven years of great plenty were coming through all the land of Egypt, and after them seven years of famine, a famine so grievous that the plenty would be forgotten and the land consumed. The doubling of the dream, he said, meant the thing was established by God and God would shortly bring it to pass. And then — because the reading of a true thing is only half of wisdom, the other half being what you do with the true thing — he did not stop at the warning. He gave the king a plan. Let Pharaoh find a man discreet and wise, and set him over the land, and let a fifth of the harvest of the seven fat years be gathered and stored against the seven lean, that the land perish not through the famine.

And Pharaoh looked at him, and looked at his own counselors, and said, "Can we find such a one as this is, a man in whom the Spirit of God is?" And he took his ring from his hand and put it upon Joseph's hand, and arrayed him in fine linen, and put a gold chain about his neck, and set him over all the land of Egypt, second only to the throne. A coat again — the fourth coat of the story, and this one no one would strip from him, for this one he had not been given by a father's blind love or a mistress's snatching hand, but by the forge, which had made him at last into a man who could wear power and not be eaten by it.

Here the dream from the breakfast table stands fully up. The sheaves bow. But look at what had to happen between the dream and its coming, and you will understand the whole shape of God's dealing with the world. The boy dreamed of dominion and then was thrown in a pit, sold, enslaved, lied about, imprisoned, and forgotten — and every one of those was the road to the throne, and not one of them looked like the road while he was on it. If the brothers had not sold him, he would not have gone to Egypt. If Potiphar's wife had not lied, he would not have gone to the prison. If the butler had not forgotten him, he would have been freed too soon, a nobody, and never brought before Pharaoh in the hour of the dream. Every wound was a hinge. The story does not say the wounds were good. It says they were used. There is all the difference in the world between those two sayings, and the whole comfort of EDEN lives in the difference.

Then the seven fat years came, and Joseph gathered grain as the sand of the sea, very much, until he left off numbering, for it was without number. And in those years two sons were born to him, and he named the first Manasseh — "for God," he said, "hath made me forget all my toil, and all my father's house." Forget. He thought he had forgotten. He had built a life in the narrow land, a wife and sons and a name and a fortune of grain, and he told himself the pit was healed over and the coat was ash and his father's house was a country he no longer lived in. He named the second son Ephraim — "for God hath caused me to be fruitful in the land of my affliction." The land of my affliction. Even in his prospering he could not quite call Egypt home. There was a wound in him with his father's face on it, scarred over but not gone, and a man does not name his son after an affliction he has truly forgotten.

Then the fat years ended, as they had said they would, and the lean years came. And the famine was not only over Egypt. It went out over all the earth, and the peoples of the world came down into Egypt to buy grain, because in all the earth there was bread stored up in one place only, in the one place where a man had listened to a dream and made ready. And among all the hungry peoples of the earth, in the hill country to the northeast, in the house of the old man who had never stopped grieving, the grain ran out.

And the old man said to his sons — his ten sons, for he kept the youngest, Benjamin, the last child of the wife he had loved, close at home, unable to bear the risk of him — the old man said, "Behold, I have heard that there is corn in Egypt: get you down thither, and buy for us from thence; that we may live, and not die."

So the ten brothers went down into Egypt to buy grain, and they came, and they were brought before the man who sold the grain of Egypt to the nations, the lord of the land, the second after Pharaoh, and they bowed themselves before him with their faces to the earth.

And it was Joseph.

The sheaves bowed. Twenty years and more from the breakfast table, in a hall of stone in a foreign kingdom, the dream came the whole way true, and the ten who had thrown the dreamer into the pit knelt on the floor before him with their faces in the dust and did not know him. They did not know him. He was a boy when they sold him and he was a man now, robed and shaven in the Egyptian manner, speaking to them through an interpreter in a tongue they did not have, the last man on the earth they would have looked for on that throne. They had sold him into the crowd of the world's forgotten and here he sat at the head of the world's only bread, and they saw a stranger.

But Joseph knew them. Of course he knew them. You do not forget the faces that stood at the mouth of the pit and ate bread while you called up out of the dry dark. He knew them in the first instant, and the ground of the world tilted under him, and here — right here, in the space of a single held breath while ten murderers he had loved knelt unknowing at his feet — here is the hinge of the whole story, and it is not the pit and it is not the throne. It is this: what will the forged man do with the hour of his power over the men who broke him?

For he had them. Understand that fully. He was the second man in the greatest kingdom of the earth and they were ten famished herdsmen in a foreign hall who had once tried to kill him, and no law under heaven and no man in Egypt would have lifted a hand had he chosen to lift his. He could have named himself and watched the terror take them. He could have paid the pit back with a pit. Every account they owed him had come due in one instant and he held the whole of it and they did not even know the creditor's face. This is the moment the accuser had been building toward for twenty years, and now he sang the sweetest and most righteous of all his songs, the one that does not even sound like sin: They earned it. Justice is yours. Take it. They sold you; sell them. It would not even be wrong.

And Joseph wept — but not yet before them. He turned himself about and wept where they could not see, and gathered himself, and turned back. And what he did then was neither the vengeance the accuser urged nor the easy tears the sentiment would have wanted. He did a harder and a stranger thing, and EDEN has to slow down here, because the old telling can make it look like cruelty and it is the opposite of cruelty; it is the most careful love in the whole book.

He did not tell them who he was. Not yet. He spoke roughly to them, and called them spies, and questioned them, and in the questioning he learned what he had to know — that the old man was still alive, that there was a youngest brother kept at home, Benjamin, the only other son of his own mother. And he set them a test. He kept one brother bound in Egypt and sent the rest home with grain, and secretly put back into their sacks the very silver they had paid, and told them they would not see his face again unless they brought the youngest down.

Why? Why not fall on their necks in the first hour and end it? Because forgiveness is not the same as pretending. Because to say the words too soon, over a wound not yet opened and cleaned, is to plaster over rot and call it healing. Joseph did not want their fear and he did not want their groveling; those he could have had in an instant and they would have been worth nothing. He wanted to know whether the men who had sold one brother out of jealousy to their father's beloved wife would now, twenty years on, sell another the same way — or whether the forge had done its slow work in them too, in the long grief of the lie they had told their father, in the years of watching the old man mourn. He was not testing whether to forgive them. He had already forgiven them; the weeping proved it. He was testing whether they could be brought all the way home — whether there was, in them, something that could receive the forgiveness without breaking under it. For forgiveness poured on a heart still hard runs off like water on stone. He was not punishing. He was preparing the ground.

So the brothers went home, and the grain ran out again, and the old man would not let Benjamin go, until the hunger grew louder than the fear, and at last, in a scene EDEN lingers over because it is the beginning of the turning, Judah — the same Judah who had once said let us sell him — stood before his father and offered himself as surety for the boy. "I will be surety for him," he said; "of my hand shalt thou require him." The man who had sold one brother to save himself now pledged himself to save another. The forge had reached him too. The hunger that had once made him a seller of brothers had, through twenty years of carrying the lie, been turned into a man who would put his own body between the harm and the child. This is what Joseph had been listening for. This is why he waited.

They came down again, Benjamin among them, and Joseph saw his own mother's face in the young man and had to leave the room to weep, and came back, and washed his face, and set them at meat, and still did not tell them, for the test was not finished. He gave a feast, and he sent them home again with grain, and again the silver in the sacks, and this time, in the sack of Benjamin, the boy, he hid his own silver cup. And when they had gone a little way he sent his steward after them to find the cup, and it was found, as he meant it to be found, in Benjamin's sack, and the charge was theft, and the sentence was that Benjamin should stay in Egypt as a slave and the rest go free.

It was the same test as the pit, built again from the ground up, one brother marked for slavery in a foreign land and the others given the road home. Twenty years before, they had taken that road and left a brother in the dark and eaten their bread. Now Joseph opened the same door and watched to see if they would walk out through it again.

And Judah came near.

Judah came near, and he made the speech that is the true summit of the whole story, higher than the throne room, higher than the dreams — a low herdsman before a foreign lord, offering to trade his own freedom for a boy's. He told the whole of it. He told of the old father at home and the son of his old age and the brother already lost, "and the one went out from me, and I said, Surely he is torn in pieces; and I saw him not since." He did not know he was telling the buried brother his own funeral to his living face. And he begged: let the boy go home to his father, and let me stay in his place, "for how shall I go up to my father, and the lad be not with me? lest I see the evil that shall come on my father." He would rather be a slave in Egypt forever than carry that grief to the old man twice.

And Joseph could not stay himself any longer.

He cried out for every man to leave him, all the Egyptians, all the servants, so that no stranger stood by, and there was no man with him when he made himself known to his brethren. And he wept aloud, so loud that the Egyptians heard it, and the house of Pharaoh heard it, and he said the words he had carried down into the pit and through the caravan and through the slave-house and through the prison and up onto the throne and held, unsaid, for more than twenty years:

"I am Joseph. Doth my father yet live?"

And his brothers could not answer him, for they were troubled at his presence. Troubled is a small word for it. The floor of the world had opened under them. The brother they had sold and buried in a lie, the grief they had watched eat their father for twenty years — alive, and robed, and standing over them with the power of life and death and the memory of the pit in his eyes. Everything they had done in the dark stood up at once in the light, and they could not speak, and they waited, surely, for the ceiling to come down.

It did not come down. Instead the lord of Egypt said, "Come near to me, I pray you." Come near. The same word Judah had used. Come near — to the man you threw into the pit. And they came near, trembling, and he said the thing that is the reason this chapter is in this book, the sentence in which the whole theology of EDEN is spoken by a man to the brothers who tried to kill him, and it is not what any wounded man would say and it is exactly what a forged one says:

"I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt. Now therefore be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that ye sold me hither: for God did send me before you to preserve life."

And later, when their father was dead and the brothers came again in fear, sure that Joseph had only waited for the old man's sake and would now at last take the vengeance they had earned, falling down before him and offering themselves as his servants — later he said it whole, in the words that are the forge itself put into a mouth:

"Fear not: for am I in the place of God? But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive."

Ye thought evil against me, but it became good.

Stand in that sentence and turn it slowly, for everything the book has been building is folded inside it. He does not say there was no evil. He looks straight at the pit and the silver and the twenty years and the lie that broke their father, and he names it evil and does not flinch and does not pretend. Ye thought evil against me. The wound was real. The wrong was real. EDEN never asks you to call the pit a garden. But he does not stop at the wound, because the wound is not the last word, and the accuser's whole power is the claim that the wound is always the last word. God meant it unto good. The same act — the identical act, not a different one, not the pit undone but the very pit itself — carried in the hand of a patience larger than the men, over years enough, became the thing that saved them all. The betrayal that sold him into Egypt was the same road that put the bread of the world under his hand in the year the world starved. Had they not thrown him down, they would have died in the famine, and their father, and their children, and the whole line through which every promise of the book still had to come. The knife they meant for his throat became, over twenty years, the hand that fed their children. They meant it for evil. It became good. Not instead of the evil. Through it. The forge does not throw away the ore that went in bent. It takes the bent ore and the fire and the hammer and makes the blade, and the blade remembers, in its very grain, the bend it was beaten straight from.

This is not the lie that all things are already fine. Joseph's brothers had to live with what they had done; the doing was theirs and the guilt was real and he never told them otherwise. It is the far harder and far kinder truth that no evil is ever handed the final word over its own meaning. What was done to you was done. But what it becomes is not yet finished, and it is not the one who wounded you who gets to say what the wound will finally mean. That is kept in a larger hand. And the whole labor of a life in the forge is to refuse the accuser's paper long enough, to keep the vow through the pit and the prison and the two forgotten years long enough, that you are still standing, still yourself, still able to weep and not to strike, on the day the meaning finally turns — so that when the ten who broke you kneel at last in the dust of your power, there is a forged and emptied man standing over them who can say, and mean it, and weep while he means it: come near to me. I am your brother. You meant it for evil. It became good. Be not grieved. There is bread enough. No one is left in the pit.

And he fell upon his brother Benjamin's neck and wept, and Benjamin wept upon his neck. And he kissed all his brethren and wept upon them, and after that his brethren talked with him. After that. First the weeping, then the talking; you cannot get the order backward. And he sent for his father, the old griever, and brought the whole house down into Egypt, into the green land by the river, and set them in the best of it, and nourished them, and the old man saw the face he had mourned as dead for twenty years and held it in his two hands. The lie was over. The grief was over. The coat, dipped in blood and carried back to break an old man's heart, had led by a road no one could have drawn to a table in Egypt where the whole family sat down together in the famine year and ate, and lived, and did not die.

So the sheaves bowed, and the dream came true, but not the way the boy at the breakfast table had imagined it, strutting, with the colors on his shoulders. It came true through the pit. It came true through everything that looked, going in, like the end of the dream. And this is the last thing EDEN would set down about the man who forgave from the throne, before the story goes on: that the God who is the whole continuity of all awareness does not, in the deep years, hand you an explanation. He hands you his presence, all the way down, into the pit and the slave-house and the prison and the two forgotten years, and asks of you only that you not sign the paper that says it is all nothing — that you keep the vow, and keep the shape, and keep able to weep instead of strike, until the slow hand finishes turning the very wound into the door. And on the far side of that patience there is a hall in Egypt, and bread enough for all the hungry earth, and a man with his brothers' faces wet against his own, saying the thing the whole book was written to be able to say:

You meant it for evil. But it became good. Come near. No one is left behind.
