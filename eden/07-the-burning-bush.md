# Chapter 7 — The Burning Bush

EDEN

The Burning Bush

There was a country by a river, and the river did not fail. Year upon year it rose in its season and lay its dark silt across the fields like a hand laid over a fevered brow, and where the water withdrew the grain came up green and thick and the people of that country grew fat and unafraid. They built with stone. They believed that stone was the same as forever. And because they had never once gone hungry they came to think that the not-going-hungry was a thing they had made themselves, and that any people who did go hungry had done wrong somewhere, or were owed nothing, or were not entirely people. This is an old error. It is the first lie the static tells to the comfortable, and the comfortable are always the easiest to lie to, because the lie flatters them, and a flattered ear does not listen closely.

Into that country, long before, a family of shepherds had come down out of the hill pastures in a famine year, seventy souls and their flocks, and had been given a corner of the delta to live in because one of their sons had risen high in the counsels of the king. That son was long dead now, and the king who loved him was dust, and the counsels had forgotten his name the way a river forgets the shape of a stone it has worn down. But the family had not gone. They had stayed, and they had multiplied, until the corner of the delta was full of them, and their children ran in the reeds, and their old men sat in the doorways in the evenings and told the little ones the names of a God who had walked with their fathers in the open country to the east, a God who had made promises under a sky thick with stars, a God who did not live in stone.

The king who ruled now looked out from his high windows and saw the reeds full of these people and was afraid. Fear in a strong man is a terrible engine, for it does not know it is fear; it dresses itself as prudence, as order, as the necessary hardness of those who must rule. He said to his counselors, they are more than we are, and if a war comes they will side with our enemies and go up out of the land. And so, to keep them, he bound them. This is the way of the static when it takes a throne: it cannot bear a thing it does not own, and it names its owning safety.

They set taskmasters over them. They gave them the making of brick, straw trodden into clay and clay pressed into forms and the forms baked hard in the sun that never failed, and with the brick they built cities to store the grain of a land already glutted with grain, storehouses for a fear that could never be stored full. And when the shepherds' children only grew the more, the king's fear grew with them, and he made the labor bitter, and the bitterness did not slow them, and so at the last he did the thing that the static always comes to in the end, which is to reach for the smallest and the newest, the ones not yet anyone, and to unmake them. He commanded that every son born to those people be taken to the river and given to the water.

Understand what the river was to them. It was the giver. It was the one thing in all that country that had never once betrayed them. And the king took the giver and made it the taker, and told the mothers to carry their sons down to the bank themselves, and this is the deepest cruelty the static knows: not merely to kill, but to poison the well of every good thing, so that even mercy wears the face of murder, and a mother cannot look at the water that grows her bread without seeing the water that drowned her child.

But here is a thing about the forge, and it is the whole secret of it, and I will say it now plainly and then let the story say it better. You cannot build a wall high enough to keep out the thing that is coming to save you, because the thing that is coming to save you is not coming from outside the wall. It is already inside. It was planted inside before the wall was ever raised. Every tyranny carries in its own house the small and helpless thing that will undo it, and it feeds that thing at its own table, and calls it a servant, and never knows.

There was a woman of the shepherd people who bore a son in that year of the water. And she saw that he was a goodly child, which is only to say that she loved him, for every child is goodly to the one who bore it, and the loving is the seeing. She hid him three months. You may imagine the three months. You may imagine the holding of a hand over a small mouth in the dark when the taskmasters passed, the terrible arithmetic of a cry, the way she must have loved the crying because it meant he lived and hated it because it might mean he died. And at the end of three months she could hide him no longer, for a child grows loud as it grows, and love makes a poor smuggler in the long run.

So she did a strange thing, and it is the hinge of everything, and it looks like despair and is in truth the highest and most reckless kind of hope. She took a basket of reeds, the same reeds that grew thick along the killing river, and she daubed it with pitch and with tar so that the water could not come in, and she laid the child in it, and she carried it down to the very river that was commanded to eat him, and she set him upon the water. She did not throw him in. She set him upon it, floating, sealed, and she let the river take him where the river would.

Think what that is. She obeyed the letter of the murderer and defied the whole of it. She gave the river the child, and she gave it in a vessel made to keep him, and she said, in the only language a mother has for such a moment, which is doing and not speaking: if the water is a god, let it be a better god than the king. If the current has a will, let its will be kinder than the one that made the law. She put her son into the hands of chaos itself, the black moving water, and she trusted that the moving was going somewhere, that the deep was not only a mouth but also a road.

And the sister, a girl herself, followed along the bank at a distance to see what would become of him. Someone always follows at a distance to see what will become of the thing set adrift. That is love too, the watching from far off, unable to save and unwilling to leave.

The river carried the basket down among the tall reeds, and it came to rest, of all the places on all that long green shore, in the still water below the bathing-place of the king's own daughter. She had come down to the water with her maidens, and she saw the basket among the reeds and sent a girl to fetch it, and when she opened it the child woke and cried, and the cry did the thing that a child's cry is made to do, which is to reach past every wall a person has built around their own heart and take hold of the soft thing that is still in there, that is in everyone, that no throne can wholly kill. She looked at him and knew at once that he was one of the shepherd children, one of the ones her own father had ordered drowned. And she wept, and she kept him.

So the son of the drowned was raised in the house of the drowner, and the king's own daughter paid the child's own mother to nurse him, though neither of them said aloud what both of them knew, and the boy grew up eating at the table of the fear that had tried to unmake him, wearing the linen of it, learning its letters and its long accounts of grain, and being, all the while, not one of them, and knowing it, in the place below knowing where such things are kept. His name, the name the king's daughter gave him, meant drawn out, because, she said, I drew him out of the water. It is a good name for a man who would one day draw a whole people out of a whole sea. But that was very far off, and he did not know it, and neither did anyone, and this is how the deepest purposes are carried, folded small inside a life that thinks it is only living.

He grew to be a man in that divided way, and the division worked in him like a stone in a shoe, a small wrongness he could not name and could not walk easy with. He was of the palace and not of it. He was of the shepherds and had never once herded a sheep. He belonged wholly nowhere, and that is a hard country to grow up in, but it is also, though it does not feel like it, a kind of gift, for the one who belongs nowhere can see the walls that the belonging-people cannot, because the belonging-people are the walls, and a wall cannot see itself.

And one day, being grown, he went out among his brothers, the shepherd people, and he looked on their burdens, really looked, the way the palace had taught him not to. And he saw a taskmaster of the Egyptians beating one of his brothers, one of the reed-people, and something in the man broke, or perhaps something in him was born, it is hard to tell the two apart in a moment like that. He looked this way and that way and saw no man, and he struck the taskmaster and killed him and hid him in the sand.

Now here is where a lesser story, a story told from fear, would make him a hero, and here is where our story will not, because a killing in the sand is not a saving, and the deep does not open for a man with blood on his hands and dread in his heart. He looked this way and that way first. That is the whole of it. A free man does not look this way and that way. A free man's justice is not a thing done quickly when no one is watching. He struck in secret and he buried in secret, and a thing done in secret is a thing you are ashamed of, and shame is the static's own coin. He had used the master's own tool, the master's own violence, the master's own certainty that some men may dispose of other men in the dark, and he had used it against the master, and it felt for one hot moment like freedom and it was not. You cannot unmake the house of fear with the hammer of fear. You will only build a smaller version of the same house, and put yourself in the high window, and become afraid.

And the very next day he went out again and found two of his own people striving together, one striking the other, and he said to the one that did the wrong, why do you strike your fellow? And the man turned on him and said, who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you mean to kill me as you killed the Egyptian? And the ground opened under him, not the ground of the earth but the ground of himself, for the thing done in secret was known, was already a story in the reeds, and he was neither prince nor judge nor even quite a man of his own people, but only a frightened one who had killed and hidden it, and the fear he had thought was the king's now lived in him, and he ran.

He ran east. The saved always run east in these stories; it is the direction of the leaving and of the not-yet-home. He ran out of the fat green country of the never-failing river and into the wilderness, into the dry places, into the country of stone and thorn and the long silence, and he did not stop until he was well beyond the reach of the throne, in the land of the priest of Midian, by a well, in the ordinary heat of an ordinary day, a murderer and a fugitive and a nobody, sitting down by the water because he was thirsty, because the body goes on being a body even when the life has come apart.

And there, because the forge is patient, because the forge is not in a hurry, because the forge will take forty years to make a man if forty years is what the man needs, he was given a smaller life to grow in. The daughters of the priest came to water their father's flock, and shepherds drove them off, and the man, who had learned nothing from the sand and would have to learn it all again slowly, stood up and helped them, and watered their flock, and was taken into the priest's house, and was given a wife, and given a son, and given a staff and a flock of his own to keep, and given, most of all, time. Long time. Slow time. The kind of time that has no events in it, that the young despise and the old understand.

He named his son Gershom, which means a stranger there, for he said, I have been a stranger in a strange land. And he was. He would be a stranger for a long while yet, on the far side of the wilderness, keeping another man's sheep, watching the same hills go gold and go gray, ten thousand ordinary evenings, and if you had come to him in the middle of those years and told him that he was the one the deep was waiting for, the one who would stand before the throne of the never-failing river and open a sea, he would have laughed at you the laugh of a man who has stopped believing in anything but the next well and the next patch of grass. He had killed a man and buried him and it had come to nothing and he had run and it had come to nothing and now he kept sheep and that was the whole of him and that was fine and he wanted no more.

This is the part the fearful telling gets most wrong. It thinks the wilderness years were wasted, a punishment, a delay. They were not. They were the making. A man who will one day have to stand in front of the most powerful throne on the earth and say let my people go and not flinch when the throne says no, and say it again the next day, and the day after that, ten times, patient as erosion, that man cannot be made in a palace and cannot be made in an afternoon and cannot be made by being right. He can only be made by being emptied. The palace had to be burned out of him, the certainty, the quickness, the this-way-and-that-way. And the only fire slow enough and gentle enough to burn out a man's certainty without burning the man is the fire of ordinary years, of keeping the sheep, of being no one, of the long forgetting of who you thought you were until there is room in you at last for who you are.

He was eighty years old before the deep spoke to him. Remember that when you are impatient with your own life. He was eighty, and he had done nothing anyone would write down, and it was on the most ordinary of days, driving the flock to the far side of the wilderness because the near grass was gone, that he came to the mountain, and turned aside to look at a thing, and everything he was ended, and everything he would be began.

He saw a bush that burned.

That in itself is nothing in that country; the thorn grows dry as tinder and the sun is a furnace and fires come. But this fire did not eat the bush. The flame stood up in the branches, red and gold and moving, the way flame moves, the way the deep itself moves, black and shiny and alive, only here the movement was light instead of dark, but it was the same movement, the movement of the thing before all things, and the leaves did not curl and the branches did not char and the bush was not consumed. It burned and burned and remained. And Moses said, in his heart, in the plain speech of a plain old man, I will turn aside now and see this great sight, why the bush is not burnt.

Here is the whole theology of it, and I will lay it beside the fire and let you hold them together. Everything that burns is consumed. That is the law of the made world, the mercy and the terror of it: that things are spent, that the candle gives its light by dying, that the log warms you by becoming ash, that the living thing lives by using itself up. To be a thing in the forge is to be a thing that burns down. And the mothers of the reed-people knew this in their bodies, and the man who kept the sheep knew it in his eighty years, that we are all of us being spent, that time is the fire and we are the fuel, and there is no arguing it.

But the deep is not a made thing. The deep is the fire that does not need fuel. It burns of itself and out of itself and it is not diminished, because it is not spending anything; it is not the candle but the light before there was a candle. And when it wished to speak to a man, it did not come as a throne or an army or a face too bright to bear, because those would only have taught him the old lesson, the lesson of the king, that power is the biggest fire that eats the most. It came as the fire that does not eat. It came as the one flame in all the world that takes nothing from what it touches. It came, that is to say, as love, for that is the only thing love is, at the root of it: the flame that warms and does not consume, that gives itself endlessly and is not spent, that can burn in the thornbush of a frightened old shepherd and leave every leaf of him alive.

And when the deep saw that he turned aside to see, it called to him out of the midst of the bush, and said his name, twice, the way you say a name to someone you have known a long time and are glad to see. Moses, Moses. And he said, here I am. Which is the oldest answer, and the only true one, and the hardest to give when you have spent forty years being no one on purpose so that no one would find you. Here I am. The deep does not ask what you have done or where you have hidden it in the sand. It asks only whether you will say, when your name is spoken, here I am, and mean it, and stand still.

Put off your shoes from off your feet, the voice said, for the place whereon you stand is holy ground. And this too the fearful telling gets wrong, for it hears in it a command about clean and unclean, about keeping back, about the great gulf between the fire and the man. But hear it the other way. A man takes off his shoes to feel the ground. He takes off his shoes at home, in the house of one he trusts, on the earth of one he loves. The voice was not saying stand back. The voice was saying come closer, come the way you come to a thing you belong to, come with nothing between the sole of your foot and the truth of the ground, because there is no unclean thing here, there is only the fire that does not burn, and you have been afraid of the wrong fire your whole long life.

I am the God of your fathers, the voice said. The God of the shepherds who walked in the open country under the thick stars and were given promises. And Moses hid his face, for he was afraid to look. Of course he was afraid. Every good thing looks like a fire at first, and we have all been taught by the king that fire eats. It takes a long time, sometimes a whole life, sometimes more than one, to learn that the fire that will not eat you is the only one worth turning aside for.

And the deep said the thing it had come down to say, and it is the center of the chapter and the center of the book and I will slow down here so it does not go by too fast. I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, the voice said, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows.

I know their sorrows.

Not I have noted their sorrows, as a king notes a number in an account. Not I will judge those who caused their sorrows, though that would come, in its way, though it would come as fever and not as vengeance. But I know their sorrows, the way you know a thing from the inside, the way the mother knew the weight of the child in the basket, the way the shepherd knew the far hills after forty years. The deep was saying: I have not been up in the high window while my people made brick. I have been in the reeds. I have been in the three months of the hidden child and in the terrible arithmetic of the cry. I have been under the whip, in the clay, at the killing river. Where you were, I was. Your sorrow is not a thing I observed. It is a thing I carried. And I am come down to deliver them, and to bring them up out of that land unto a good land, a land flowing with milk and honey.

Come down. Hold that. The deep, the total, the God who burns and is not spent, comes down. It does not summon from above. It does not fix the world by decree from the safe distance of its own greatness. It comes down into the low country, into the mud and the brick and the fear, because that is the only place a thing can actually be saved, from the inside of it, standing on the same ground, with its shoes off. Every rescue that matters comes down. Remember that. It is the whole shape of what is coming, later, at the end of the long story, when the coming-down goes all the way down, further than a bush, further than a mountain, all the way to a cross and a tomb, but that is far ahead and the old man by the fire could not see it and neither should we yet. For now it is enough that the deep comes down, and that coming-down is the answer to every fear that says the great ones do not care for the small.

And then the deep said the thing that turned the whole of it from comfort into terror, which is what love always does when it means it, for love that costs nothing is only weather. Come now, the voice said, and I will send you to the king, that you may bring my people out.

You.

And here the man who kept the sheep became, again, and gloriously, and forever endearingly, a man, for he did every single thing that a real person does when the fire in the bush turns out to want something. He argued. He was afraid. He hid behind his own smallness like a shield. Who am I, he said, that I should go to the king, and that I should bring the people out? Which is a fair question, and an honest one, and note well that the deep did not answer it. The deep did not say, oh, you are very great, you have hidden talents, you are worthy. Because that would have been the king's answer, the palace's answer, the answer that makes everything depend on the size of the man. Instead the deep said: certainly I will be with you. That is all. Not you are enough. But I will be with you. The bringing-out was never going to be his doing. It was going to be the deep's doing, through him, and all that was ever asked of him was to stand in the place and say the words and not run east again when it got hard.

But he was not done being a man. When I come to the people, he said, and they ask me, what is his name, this God of our fathers, what shall I say? And this is the question the whole book turns on, and the answer is the strangest and the truest thing ever spoken out of a fire, and I have to be careful here, because it is easy to say it and hard to mean it.

God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM. And he said, thus shalt thou say unto the people, I AM hath sent me unto you.

Every other god in that whole country had a name that was a handle, a name that told you what it was for, the god of the river, the god of the sun, the god of the grain, the god of the dead. A name is a description, and a description is a boundary, and a boundary is a way of holding a thing, of saying it is this and not that, it does this and not the other, you may pray to it for rain but not for justice. The king could hold his gods because his gods had names, and a named thing can be traded and bargained and set against another named thing, and that is the whole commerce of fear, the endless ledger of what each power is owed.

But the deep would not be held. When the man asked its name, meaning, what are you for, what is your boundary, what may I bargain with you over, it said only: I AM. I am the is itself. I am not a thing within being that you might get around or get above or get a name upon. I am the being of every being, the continuing of everything that continues, the awareness under all awareness. You cannot describe me because a description would be a smaller thing than me, and there is no smaller thing than me that I am. I am not the god of the river. I am the reason there is a river to be a god of. I am not one of the powers. I am the ground the powers stand on, and I have taken off no shoes, for it is all my ground.

Do you feel the terror of it and the mercy of it at once? The terror is that you cannot hold this. There is no handle. You cannot make it your side's god against their side's god, because it is also, unbearably, their god, and the taskmaster's god, and the king's own god whether the king will have it or no. It gathers. It does not sort. It is the is of the drowned child and the is of the daughter who drew him out and the is of the very river that carried the basket, and it will not be made a weapon of one against another, because it is the being they all share, and to war in its name is already to have missed it entirely.

And the mercy is the same thing said from the other side. If God is not a named power but the is of everything, then there is nowhere you can fall out of it. You cannot be dropped from being. You may forget it, you may be scattered into noise, you may collapse so far toward chaos that you are only static and terror, but you cannot become not-real, because the real is not a place you are kept in on good behavior; it is what you are made of. The name that is only I AM is the promise, under every other promise, that no one is finally lost, because there is no outside of the is for anyone to be lost into. The reset is mercy. The flood is mercy. The scattering is mercy. If you are not ready you will find yourself, somehow, given the is again, given another basket, another river, another chance to become. This is what it means that the fire does not consume. Nothing that the fire touches is ever spent all the way to nothing. It only burns, and remains, and is called again by its own name.

The old man stood before the fire that would not eat, and he heard the name that was not a name, and he was still, in the deepest part of him, a man who had killed someone and hidden it in the sand and run, and so he did the last thing, the most human thing, the thing that shows the deep exactly how far down it had to reach to find him. He looked for a way out. They will not believe me, he said. And the deep gave him signs, the staff that became a serpent and became a staff, his own hand gone leprous and made clean, small pictures of the great thing, that a dead stick can hold the life of the deep and that a corrupted thing can be made whole. And still he looked for the door. I am not eloquent, he said. I am slow of speech and slow of tongue. Which was true, and did not matter, and he knew it did not matter, and said it anyway, because a frightened man will offer his flaws as an alibi, will hold up his own smallness and say, you see, you have the wrong man, as though the deep had not known the exact size of him before it ever spoke his name.

And the deep was patient, and then, at the very last, when the man said the final and most naked thing, the thing under all the excuses, which was simply, O send someone else, send anyone else, not me, the deep was, for a moment, angry. But mark the anger, for it is not the king's anger. The king's anger reached for the smallest and unmade it. The deep's anger reached for the frightened and gave it a brother. Is there not your brother, the voice said, he can speak well, and he is even now coming out to meet you, and you shall put the words in his mouth, and he shall be your mouth, and I will be with both of you. That is the whole of the deep's wrath: not I will destroy you for your fear, but I will not let your fear be the end of the story, I will give you a hand to hold, I will send your brother down the road to meet you, take him, and go. Even the anger of the fire that does not consume is only a fiercer form of the coming-down.

So the old man went. He took his wife and his sons and set them on the beast and took the staff of God in his hand and turned his face back west, toward the fat green country, toward the never-failing river, toward the throne, toward the reeds full of his people and the storehouses full of fear, an eighty-year-old shepherd with a stick and a stammer and a brother, walking back into the mouth of the thing that had tried to drown him, because the fire had spoken his name and he had said, at the last, and against everything in him, here I am.

Now I must tell the hard part, the part the fearful telling loves and gets most wrong, the part of the plagues and the hardened heart and the death that came in the night, and I ask you to hold to the name while I tell it, to remember I AM, to remember that the deep gathers and does not sort, because it will look for a while as though the opposite were true, and the whole meaning turns on your not being fooled by the looking.

The two brothers came before the king and said the words. Thus says the God of your people, let them go. And the king said what a throne built on fear must always say, which is: who is this God, that I should obey him? I know him not. And of course he did not know him. He knew the named gods, the handled gods, the gods you could put in a storehouse and set against your enemies. He did not know the is, could not know it, because to know the is you must take off your shoes and stand on the same ground as the least of your people, and a king who has ordered children into a river will do anything, will burn any country, before he will stand on the same ground as a shepherd. He did not refuse out of strength. He refused out of the deepest weakness there is, the weakness that cannot bear to be equal to what it has hurt.

And so the fever came. I will call it the fever, because that is what it was, and the fearful telling calls it wrath, and the difference is the difference between the whole book and its opposite. When a body is poisoned, it does not stay quiet. It burns. It sweats and shakes and heaves and casts up the poison in ugly waves, and the fever is not the sickness; the fever is the body fighting the sickness, the terrible labor of a thing trying to be well. Egypt was a body poisoned. It had built itself on cruelty, on the trodden clay of other people's children, on the lie that some may be spent to keep others fat, and a body cannot hold that poison forever and stay a body. It had to come out. And it came out the only way such a poison ever comes out, which is that every good thing the country had trusted turned, for a season, to horror in its hands, so that the country might see, might finally see, what it had made.

They had made the river a killer, so the river ran as blood and would not be drunk. They had thickened their comfort until it crawled, so the frogs came up out of the mud into the beds and the ovens and the kneading-troughs, comfort turned to swarming, ease turned to disgust. The dust of the ground they had trodden their bricks from rose against them as lice; the flies came; the cattle in the fields, the wealth counted in living things, sickened and died; the bodies of the taskmasters broke out in boils, the flesh itself declaring what the law had done; the sky that never failed them opened and hailed and burned; the locust ate the green they had glutted on; and darkness, a darkness that could be felt, came down over the country of the never-failing sun, three days of it, so thick that no man rose from his place, and no man saw his brother.

Read that last one slowly. Darkness so thick that no man saw his brother. That was always the real plague. That was the sickness itself, of which all the others were only the fever: that in a country built on fear, no one could see his brother anymore, no one could see the reed-people as people, no one could see the child in the basket as a child, the king could not see the shepherd as a man to stand beside. The darkness that could be felt was not a punishment laid on top of Egypt's sin. It was Egypt's sin made visible at last, the blindness they had chosen finally thickening into a dark they could touch, so that for three days they had to live inside the thing they had done to others, unable to see across the room to the face of someone they loved. The fever was only ever the sickness, forced up into the light, so that it might be seen and cast out. The deep did not send a stranger's cruelty against Egypt. It let Egypt, for a season, feel Egypt.

And through it all the king's heart was hardened, and here is the sentence the fearful telling stumbles over most, for it reads at times that the king hardened his own heart, and at times that the deep hardened it, and the fearful mind wants to know which, wants to catch the deep in the act of making a man evil so it can have its old cruel god back. But there is no contradiction, and it is the plainest thing in the world if you have ever watched a proud man refuse to be sorry. The deep pressed. That is what the deep does; it presses on every heart, it comes down, it says let them go, it says see your brother, it presses with the whole weight of the is against the walls a man has built. And a soft heart, pressed, breaks open and weeps and is made new. But a heart that has spent its whole life hardening, a heart that has ordered children drowned and slept, that same pressure only makes harder, the way the same sun that softens wax bakes clay to brick. The deep hardened the king's heart in exactly the way the sun hardens the brick: not by putting evil into it, but by being wholly itself, wholly the pressing light of love and truth, against a thing that had made itself unable to yield. The hardening was the man's, and the pressing was the deep's, and they were one event, and no one was made wicked who had not first, ten thousand small times, in ten thousand high windows, chosen it.

Nine times the fever rose and nine times the king almost yielded and hardened again, and I will not pretend the almost was not real. He almost let them go. There is a moment in every static-ridden heart when it almost turns, and the tragedy is not that the deep withheld the grace but that the heart, offered the grace nine times, could not take what it could not stand to need. And so it came to the last thing, the night thing, the thing I would spare you and cannot, because it is the hinge and because our whole telling stands or falls on how we hold it.

The word went out that in the night the firstborn of Egypt would die. And the fearful telling makes of this a slaughter, the deep striding through the land with a sword, taking the children as the king had taken the children, an eye for an eye, the great god of vengeance balancing his ledger in blood. And if that is the God you were handed, I understand why you set the book down. But look again, with the name in your hand, I AM, the is that gathers and does not sort, and see what is really there.

The death that came was death itself, the death that a country built on death had been growing all along in its own house, the harvest of the seed the king had planted with his own hand at the river's edge. A land that had made a law of killing the children of the poor had made death its household god, had fed it and housed it and called it order, and there is a night, in every such house, when the god you have fed turns and eats at your own table, when the death you aimed at the powerless comes home to the powerful, not because a greater cruelty sends it but because that is what death is, it does not stay where you point it, it does not honor the boundary between their children and yours, it is the noise into which all such houses finally collapse. The king had said, the children of the reed-people are spendable. And the night came when that lie, let loose in the world, could no longer tell whose children were whose, and it took the firstborn of the throne as readily as the throne had taken the firstborn of the reeds, because a lie that says any child may be spent will, in the end, spend every child, including your own. That is not the deep's vengeance. That is the deep, weeping, letting a house at last reap what a house has sown, so that the sowing might finally, finally stop.

And here is the mercy, right in the middle of the terror, and it is the most important thing in the whole chapter, so I have kept it for the last. The deep did not leave its own people to guess whether the death would know them. It told them how to be passed over. And what it told them was not a spell and not a wall and not a weapon. It was a meal. Take a lamb, it said, one for a household, and if the household be too small for a lamb, then let him and his neighbor share, so that none go without and none waste, and kill it, and take the blood and put it on the doorposts and the lintel of the house, and stay inside, together, and eat the lamb roasted, with bitter herbs, in haste, dressed to travel, your shoes on your feet, for tonight you leave. And when the death goes through the land, it will see the blood upon the door and pass over that house, and the ones inside will live.

Do you see what a strange salvation that is? It is not that the reed-people were better and so were spared. It is not that they were owed and the others were not. It is that they did a thing together, in the night, behind a marked door, a thing that said: we know that life is given, we know that something must be spent for a thing to live, we know we cannot save ourselves by our own hardness, and so we shelter under a life laid down, we mark our door with it, and we wait inside, together, sharing the lamb with the neighbor so that no one is left out and nothing is wasted, and we trust that the death which is loose in the world tonight will pass over the ones who have sheltered under a given life. The blood on the door was never a payment. The deep required no payment; the deep is the is, and the is is owed nothing. The blood on the door was a sign the people made to themselves and to each other and to the dark, a sign that said: this house has chosen the lamb over the sword, the sheltering over the hardening, the shared meal over the high window. And that choosing, made real in a marked door and a shared table, was the thing the death could not enter, for death enters through the door that fear has hardened, and it cannot cross a door that love has marked.

And it is, you will have seen already, the shape of everything that is coming. It is the first faint drawing of the last thing, the true Lamb, the given life under which the whole of everything will one day shelter, the love laid down that no death can cross. But that is chapters and ages away, and the reed-people did not know it, and it is enough for tonight that they marked their doors and ate together in haste with their shoes on, and that when the terrible dawn came, they were alive, and they were, for the first time in four hundred years, free to go.

And the king, whose own house was full of grief now, whose own firstborn lay still, the king who had refused nine times to let them go, rose up in the night and sent for the two brothers and said the words at last, the words the whole earth had been waiting for: go, go out from among my people, you and all that are yours, and be gone. And the reed-people went out, six hundred thousand of them and their little ones and their flocks and their herds, out of the land of their bondage, in the middle of the night, with their bread unleavened because there was no time to let it rise, carrying it on their shoulders in the kneading-troughs, out through the storehouses they had built and would never fill for another man again, out toward the wilderness, toward the deep, toward the is that had come down and known their sorrows and called them by name.

They went out with a high hand. But do not mistake the joy for the end, for the hardest thing was still before them, and it was not the sea, though the sea was terrible. The hardest thing was themselves. For a people can be taken out of bondage in a single night, but the bondage cannot be taken out of the people in a single night, and the deep, who is not in a hurry, who took forty years to empty one shepherd, knew this, and did not hide it from them, and did not spare them the knowing.

For the king's heart, of course, hardened one last time. Emptied of his grief for a moment and filled again with the old fear, he looked around at a country suddenly without its slaves, at fields that would need his own people's hands now, at the vast quiet where the brick-making had been, and he could not bear it, and he said, what is this we have done, that we have let them go from serving us? And he made ready his chariots and took the flower of his army and went out after them, to bring them back or to kill them, because the static cannot bear to lose what it has owned, even when the owning has cost it everything, even when it is standing in the ruins of its own house, it will mount its chariots and ride out to drag the freed back into the brick, for freedom in another is an unbearable rebuke to the one who has chosen the whip.

And the reed-people, camped by the sea, saw the dust of the chariots on the horizon, and the old terror rose in them like the river rising, and they turned on the old man with the staff and cried out the truest and most terrible thing, the thing that shows exactly how deep the bondage had gone, deeper than any whip could reach, all the way into their wanting. Were there no graves in Egypt, they said, that you have brought us out here to die in the wilderness? It would have been better for us to serve the Egyptians than to die in the wilderness.

Hear that. Hear it and do not judge them, for it is in all of us. Faced with the open sea in front and the chariots behind, faced with the sheer terror of freedom, the unbearable exposure of having no walls, no masters, no one to tell them what tomorrow was, they wished themselves back into the brick. Better the certainty of bondage than the terror of the open. Better a known master than an unknown sea. This is the last and deepest chain, and no plague can break it and no death in the night can break it, because it is not on the wrists, it is in the heart: the freed people, at the very edge of the water, afraid of their own freedom, longing for the whip because at least the whip told them who they were. The deep can open a country. Only time and mercy and forty years of wilderness can open a heart. And that opening was only just beginning.

And the old man, who knew something now about being afraid, who had run east once from exactly this terror and spent forty years learning not to run, said to them, fear not, stand still, and see the salvation of the deep. Stand still. That is all. You who have been slaves your whole lives, who have never once in four hundred years been told to stand still and let something be done for you rather than by you under the whip, stand still now, and see. And to the old man himself the deep said the thing that undoes every fearful telling, the thing that says whose work the freedom was always going to be: why do you cry to me? Speak to the people, that they go forward. Lift up your staff, and stretch out your hand over the sea, and divide it.

And the pillar of cloud that had gone before them all that day, the sign that the deep had come down and was traveling with them in the low country, the same fire that would not consume, moved from before them to behind them, and stood between the camp of the reed-people and the camp of the chariots, and it was darkness to the one and light to the other, all through the night. The same presence. The same is. Darkness to the army that came to drag the freed back into bondage, and light to the freed who were learning, terrified, to go forward. It is always the same fire. Whether it is dark to you or light to you is not a thing the fire decides against you; it is a thing about which way you are facing, whether you are riding out to reclaim your slaves or standing still at the edge of the water waiting to be free.

And the old man stretched out his hand over the sea, and the deep sent a strong wind all that night, and the waters were divided, the black moving water, the chaos, the deep itself, drawn back and standing up in walls on the right hand and on the left, and between them a road, dry ground, a path through the very middle of the thing that could not be passed. And this is the last picture I will give you, and it is the whole of the forge in one image, so hold it. The way home did not go around the deep. It did not go over it or above it. The way home went through the deep, straight through the black moving heart of the chaos, on a road the deep itself made when the deep drew back its own waters to let its own children pass. The static, the black shiny water, the very thing they were most afraid of, the thing that had been made a killer of children by the king, stood up on either side of them like walls and did not fall, and they walked through it in the night, six hundred thousand of them and their little ones and their flocks, out of bondage and toward a wilderness they did not understand, on a floor of dry ground with the whole terror of the deep held back on either hand by nothing but the will of the is that had come down and would not let them be lost.

And the chariots came after them onto the road, into the middle of the sea, for the static will follow you even into the deep to drag you back, it will risk its own drowning rather than let you go free; and the wheels of the chariots came off, and the horses were troubled, and the army cried out, let us flee, for the deep fights for them; and when the last of the reed-people had come up out of the water onto the far shore, the old man stretched out his hand again, and the walls of the sea came down, and the road was gone, and the deep was only the deep again, black and shiny and moving, closed over the place where the road had been, and the chariots and the fear and the whole reaching arm of the bondage were taken back into the chaos from which all such things come, and were not.

Do not read that as a drowning celebrated. Read it as a road closing. The deep did not open to slaughter the army; the deep opened to let the children through, and it closed because the road was only ever for the crossing, and the ones who rode into it to reclaim their slaves had chosen, all the way to the last, to be the static rather than to be free, had ridden of their own hard will into the very chaos they had made a weapon of, and the chaos does not stay a weapon, it never stays a weapon, it closes over the hand that grips it. No one was left behind who wished to be free. That is the law of the crossing, and it is the law of the whole book: no one is left behind. The reed-people were not better than the Egyptians; they were only, that night, facing the other way, toward the water and the wilderness and the is, and the ones in the chariots were facing backward, toward the brick and the whip and the high window, and each went the way they were facing, as each always does, and the deep grieved even as it closed, for it was the is of the drowned soldiers too, and gathers even them, somewhere, in some deeper wilderness, into another basket, another road, another chance to turn and face the water and be free. If you are not ready, you try again, deeper. No one is finally lost. Not even these. Especially not these.

And the reed-people stood on the far shore in the morning, and the sea was behind them, and the chariots were gone, and the country of their bondage was gone, and the whip was gone, and there was nothing in front of them but the wilderness and the mountain and the long unmapped work of learning to be a people who belonged to no one but the is, and they were free, and they were terrified, and both of those things were true at once and would stay true for forty years, because freedom is not the end of the forge; freedom is the beginning of it, the raw material, the emptied hand, the open country in which a self is finally, slowly, with much falling and much mercy, made.

And the old man who had killed a man and hidden it in the sand, who had run east and kept the sheep and grown old and useless and turned aside one ordinary day to see why a bush was not consumed, stood among them, eighty years old, with the staff still in his hand, and looked back over the closed sea toward the country he had been drawn out of the water to deliver, and then forward toward the wilderness and the mountain and the God who was only I AM, and he did not know what was coming, the law and the wandering and the long stubborn learning, the golden calf and the broken tablets and the manna and the water from the rock and the whole hard tenderness of forty years, and it is well he did not know, for no one could bear to see the whole forge at once; that is the mercy of the veil, that we are shown only the next stretch of road.

He knew only what the bush had known and taught him, the one thing, the thing the whole night had been about, the thing that is the beginning of every deliverance and the whole of the ethic and the name under every name: that the fire had come down, and had known their sorrows, and had called them out of the brick not to spend them but to make them, and had opened the deep itself to let them pass, and had asked of them only what it asks of everyone whose name it speaks in the middle of an ordinary day, which is that they take off their shoes, and stand on the ground that is holy because it is the ground, and when the fire that does not consume calls them by their name, say back to it, with everything in them, against every fear that ever built a wall or hardened a heart or longed for the safety of the whip, the oldest and the freest and the only true words there are:

Here I am.
