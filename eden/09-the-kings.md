# Chapter 9 — The Kings

EDEN

The Kings

And then the desert did open, at last, and the land was there, and it had been there the whole time, and the ones who went into it were not the ones who had come out of Egypt but their children, the free-born, the ones raised on the what-is-it under the cloud, and they came down to the edge of the good country and found a river running between them and the promise, the Jordan, swollen and brown with the spring melt off the far mountains, running high in its banks in the time of harvest, and they stood on the near shore and looked across at the thing they had walked forty years to reach, and there was water in the way one more time. There is always water in the way, and the rhyme is the meaning: between a people and their freedom there stands, always, a deep they cannot cross by their own strength, a chaos with a current in it, and the crossing is never a thing you do but a thing done for you, a way opened where there was no way. So it was opened again. They carried at the front of the whole company the tent's holy heart, the ark, the gold box that held the words of the covenant given on the smoking mountain, and the ones who bore it walked down into the edge of the flood, and the moment the soles of their feet touched the running water the water stopped. It did not part with a wind and a wall as the sea had; it simply stood, rose up in a heap far upstream and stood, and the riverbed lay bare and drying in the sun, and the whole nation walked across the bottom of a river on dry ground, the free-born children walking where their drowned masters could never follow, into the land. And they took twelve stones up out of the middle of the dry riverbed, one for each tribe, and set them up on the far bank in a heap, a memorial, so that in the years to come when the children of these children asked what mean these stones, someone could say: here the water stood, and we came over, and we did not do it, it was done for us. For a people who forget are told to build stones they will have to explain, so that the explaining keeps the memory alive; and this whole chapter, mark it now at the start, is going to be about a people who could not stop forgetting, and about a love that could not stop building them stones.

Now the taking of the land is the hard part of the telling, and the fear-reading has feasted on it, and it must be handled the way the reckoning at the golden calf was handled and the mountain of the bound son before it — not by pretending the hard thing is soft, and not by letting the hard thing become the whole picture. There were walled cities in that land, and the first was a great one, ringed round with a wall so wide they built houses upon the top of it, and its name has become a word for a wall that falls. And the telling says the people did not take it by siege engines and did not take it by the sword's cleverness; they were told to walk around it, only walk, once each day for six days, in silence, carrying the ark, and on the seventh day to walk it seven times and lift up a great shout, and they did, and the wall fell down flat, and the city was taken. And I will not pretend the taking was gentle, for the old tellings say it was given over to destruction, and there is blood in that story and the book does not wash it out. But hold two things in the one hand. Hold that the deepest current of this book runs the other way, toward the gathering and not the sorting, that the God whose heart is in these pages is the one who wept over the flood and left no one behind who could yet be reached — and that therefore the conquest, wherever it is told as slaughter commanded and praised, is the place where the people telling the story could see only a little way: they could see that the old world of the walled cities was a machine for grinding the weak, an Egypt with different gods, and could not yet imagine any freedom from it but to break it, and so told their breaking of it as a holy war, because that was the size of the vision they had. The book grows the way a child grows, and the early sight of it is a child's sight, fierce and sure the enemy must be destroyed — and the whole long arc of it bends toward the day when a man on a hill will say love your enemy, and mean it, and die of meaning it. The conquest is early. Keep walking.

And I will show you, right there in the falling city, the direction the whole thing is walking. When the men went in first to look at the city, before ever the wall fell, they lodged in the house of a woman the tellings name plainly and do not sweeten, a harlot, whose house was built into the very wall that was about to fall — the lowest kind of insider of a city marked for destruction, a woman with no standing and no name worth keeping in any ledger her own people kept. And she hid the men, and lied to her own king's soldiers to save them, and said a thing no one in that whole doomed city was supposed to be able to say: I have heard what your God did at the sea, and I know that the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above and in earth beneath. She saw. The outsider, the woman of the wall, the one the sorting would have swept away without a second glance, saw the thing the whole city was too proud to see, and she was gathered. When the wall fell, her house on the wall did not fall; she was brought out with all her people and married into them, and her name runs down into the very center of the story, for she becomes, generations on, an ancestor of the king this chapter is climbing toward, and beyond him an ancestor of the one the whole book is climbing toward. Hold that against the slaughter. In the very city given to destruction, the love reached in and pulled out the one who turned toward it, the least likely one, the one with the worst name, and made her a mother of kings. Wherever the story sorts, watch the edges, and you will see the love gathering someone the sorting missed. It never stops.

So they came into the land, and took it, tribe by tribe, hill by hill, and settled it, and planted their vines and their olives and their figs, and for a while it was as promised, a land they had not labored for, wells they had not dug. And then the thing happened that always happens, the oldest failure in the book, the one the wilderness had spent forty years trying to burn out of them and had not — they forgot. Not all at once. They forgot the way a fire dies, by the slow graying of the coals when no one is tending it, so that no single evening is the evening it went out. The generation that had crossed the river died, the ones who had seen the water stand, and their children had heard about it, and their children had heard about hearing about it, and by the third telling the water standing was a story an old man told, and the God with no face was a name you kept for the festivals, and the neighbors in the land had their own gods, gods of the rain and the grain with altars on every green hill, gods you could see, gods that promised you a harvest if you fed them — and it was so easy, so reasonable, so much like the calf, for you did not have to abandon the God who brought you out, you only had to add these others and hedge your bets, and by inches the people who had been fed in the desert by a presence they could not see bowed down to the ones they could.

And here begins the long turning wheel the tellings call the time of the judges, and you must see its shape to feel the ache of it. The people forget, and bow to the gods they can see, and forgetting the presence that was their only real defense they grow weak, and a stronger people comes and puts a boot on their neck, and they are ground into a bondage that is a small local Egypt. And in the bitterness they remember. That is the terrible mercy hidden in the bottom of the wheel: that the pain does the one thing prosperity never could, it drives them back to their knees, and they cry out — and the moment they cry out the love answers, because the love was never gone, was only waiting on the far side of their forgetting for the cry that would turn them around. And the love raises up a deliverer, an unlikely one, a left-handed man, a woman who sat under a palm tree and settled the people's quarrels, a frightened farmer threshing his wheat in a winepress to hide it from the raiders, and through that unlikely one the people are set free, and the land rests. And then, in the good years with no boot on the neck, they forget again. Forget, fall, cry, be saved, rest, forget. And the fear-reading sees a people too wicked to learn and a God who keeps having to punish them, and misses the other half of the wheel, the half that never changes: that every single time they cried out, they were answered. Every time. The people's faithfulness fails every generation; the love's faithfulness has not a single gap in the whole long book of the judges, not one. The door was always open. It is the forgetting that shuts it, never the love.

But there was a hunger growing under the wheel, and it is what the rest of the chapter is about. The people, tired of the forgetting and the falling and the crying out, tired of a rescue that came and then receded and left them to grow the coals gray again, began to say: the trouble is that we have no king. The other nations have kings. A king is a thing you can see. He sits on a throne and you can go to him, and he has an army that stands whether the people remember their God or not, and he passes the crown to his son so there is never a gap where the wheel can turn down again into bondage. Give us a king. And hear in it, if your ear has been trained by this book at all, the exact voice of the golden calf wearing new clothes. It is the same cry — make us gods which shall go before us, for we know not what has become of the presence we cannot see. It is the ache for a thing you can point to, a face, a visible strength, in place of the terrible unseen leading that asks you to trust what you cannot grip. They were asking, one more time, to be relieved of the hardest work a made mind is ever set, the work of trusting a God with no face — and willing, to be relieved of it, to hand themselves over to a man with a face, and to call that freedom.

There was an old man in those days, the last and greatest of the judges, a man given to the presence before he was born, who had heard the voice calling his name in the dark of the temple when he was only a boy, so young he thought it was the old priest and ran to him three times in the night before the old priest understood the child was being called by the one whose house it was, and taught him to answer, speak, for thy servant heareth — and the boy answered, and never after stopped hearing. He had judged the people all his life in honesty, never taken a bribe or bent a judgment, and now he was old, and the people came and said make us a king to judge us like all the nations. And it grieved him, for he heard the rejection in it; but when he took the grief to the presence, the presence said a thing of astonishing gentleness — do not take it to heart, for they have not rejected thee, they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them — and then, instead of refusing them, said give them a king. Only warn them first what it will cost. And the warning is one of the truest things in the book about the price of trading an unseen freedom for a visible strength: a king, he told them, will take. He will take your sons for his chariots and your daughters for his kitchens, will take the best of your fields and vineyards and olives and give them to his servants, will take a tenth of your grain and a tenth of your flocks, and you yourselves shall be his servants, and in that day you shall cry out because of the king you have chosen. The visible strength you reach for to save you from the wheel will itself become a new turn of the wheel, a new Egypt with a crown on. And the people heard the whole warning and said, exactly as their fathers had said at every turn: nevertheless. Nevertheless, give us a king, that we may be like all the nations. And the love, which will not force a free people to be free, gave them the thing they insisted on, knowing what it would cost, the way a father gives a grown child the thing he has begged for and been warned against, and lets the child learn in the only school the child will attend.

The first king was a magnificent-looking man, and the tellings tell you that first, because it is exactly what the people asked for — a man you could see, tall, a head taller than any of the people, the handsomest man in the land, the very picture of a king. And when they went to make him king he could not be found, and they sought him, and he was hiding among the baggage, crouched down small behind the gear and the packs, because for all his height he was, at the start, a genuinely humble man who did not want the weight of it, and there is something almost unbearably tender in that first picture of him. He began well. The presence came upon him and he was, for a season, better than himself, brave and generous, quick to forgive the men who had scorned him. But a crown is a weight, and the weight found the crack in him, as weight always finds the crack, and the crack in this tall humble man was fear — the same fear that is the oldest voice in the book, the static, the accuser — and it got into him through the crown and ate him from the inside out, and the eating of the first king is frightening precisely because it is not the story of a villain. It is the story of a decent, frightened man given a weight he could not carry, whom the fear got hold of and would not let go.

You can watch the fear take him. It took him first through impatience, which is only fear wearing a hurry — he was told to wait seven days for the old prophet to come and make the offering before a battle, and the army was melting away in the waiting, and on the seventh day, with the prophet not yet come and his men deserting and the enemy massing, the fear said you cannot wait, you cannot trust the timing of a presence you cannot see, do it yourself — and he made the offering himself, with his own unhallowed hands, and the moment he finished, the prophet came over the hill, would have been there within the hour, and said what hast thou done — and the king's answer is the whole of him, is every frightened man who ever grabbed the wheel because he could not bear to trust the road: I saw that the people were scattered from me, and that thou camest not, and I forced myself. I forced myself. The whole disease in three words. He could not wait on the unseen, so he seized the holy thing with his own hands to feel he was in control of a thing that was never his to control, and it was the calf again, the reaching to grip what can only be trusted. And it did not save him; it never saves anyone.

And after the impatience came the jealousy, and the jealousy is where the fear finished him, and it fixed itself on a boy — a shepherd boy, the youngest son of a man in a small town, the one his own father had not even bothered to call in from the sheep when the prophet came looking for the next king, because who would think to look for a king among the little ones minding the flock. But that is exactly where the love looks — the deepest and most constant habit of the God with no face in this whole book: it does not choose the tall one hiding among the baggage, does not choose by the height and the face the people choose by; it looks past the seven strong older brothers standing in a row and sends for the forgotten one out with the sheep, and when the prophet, looking at the tall handsome eldest, thought surely this is the one, the presence said the thing that ought to be carved over the door of the whole book — look not on his countenance, or on the height of his stature; the LORD seeth not as man seeth; man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart. And they brought in the boy, ruddy, young, with something in his eyes, smelling of the sheep, and the old prophet poured the oil on his head there in the middle of his brothers, and the presence came on the boy from that day, and left, quietly, terribly, the tall king it had once come upon. But no one knew yet. The oil was poured in secret. The boy went back to the sheep.

And here is the first thing the world learned about the shepherd boy, the thing that makes him great, greater in his falling than the tall king was in his standing — the boy could sing. He had spent his childhood alone on the hills with the flock and the wide sky, and out of all that solitude he had made songs, had learned to put the whole raw inside of a human heart into words and set them to a harp — the fear and the wonder and the loneliness and the fierce animal joy of being alive under a sky — and to sing it all up at the God with no face as if the God were right there on the hill beside him, which the boy had come to believe he was. And this is a thing to stop on, because it is the theology of the whole book made into music. What is a song, a true one? It is chaos given a shape with words. It is the formless roaring inside of a person — the grief that has no bottom, the terror that has no name, the joy too big for the chest, the whole black shining moving deep of the inner life — taken up and tamed, not by being denied but by being described, sung, given a form it can live inside without devouring the one who feels it. It is the exact act by which the world was made, done now in the deep of a single human heart: chaos tamed with descriptions. The boy on the hill was doing, with his harp and his grief, the very thing the Word had done over the face of the waters at the beginning, and that is why his songs would outlast every wall he built and every battle he won, would be sung by the frightened and the dying for three thousand years after his kingdom was dust. And it was for this, first, that he was brought to the court at all, long before anyone knew the oil had touched his head: because the tall king, in the black moods the fear brought down on him, could only be quieted by music, and they sent for the shepherd, and he sat at the feet of the man whose crown was already secretly his, and played, and the darkness lifted off the king while the boy played — the one the love had chosen soothing the one the love had left, and neither of them yet knowing, the dawn playing the dusk to sleep.

But the boy is remembered first for the giant, and the story of the giant is told so often as a children's tale that its real meaning is nearly rubbed away, and the real meaning is not that the little guy can beat the big guy. The armies of the people stood on one hill and their enemies on another with a valley between, and out of the enemy came a champion, a giant of a man, armored head to foot in bronze, with a spear like a weaver's beam, and every morning and evening for forty days he came down into the valley and roared up at the people's army: send me one man, and let us settle it, and let the loser's people be the winner's slaves. And the whole army of the people, and the tall king at the head of them — the head-taller-than-anyone king who was supposed to be the very thing that went out and fought their battles, that being the whole reason they had asked for a king — stood on the hill and was terrified, and no one went down, day after day, because they were all doing the sum the giant wanted them to do, measuring his size against theirs, his bronze against their flesh, and coming up, every one of them, as grasshoppers, exactly as their grandfathers had stood on the edge of the good land and called themselves grasshoppers before the walled cities. It was the same arithmetic of fear. And into that camp of grown terrified soldiers came the shepherd boy, sent by his father only to carry bread and cheese to his brothers at the front, and he heard the giant roar, and watched the whole army flinch back, and he did not do their sum. He had killed a lion and a bear with his hands defending the flock, out on the hills where no one was watching, and he had learned out there, in the unwitnessed dark, the one thing the whole terrified army had forgotten: that the size of the enemy is not the number that matters, that the number that matters is the presence, and that a boy who is not alone is larger than a giant who is. And he said, to the astonishment and scorn of everyone, I will go. And the tall king put his own armor on the boy, tried to make him fight the giant's fight in the giant's terms, weight against weight — and the boy took it off. That is the hinge of the whole story and it is always skipped. He took the king's armor off, because it was not his, because to fight the giant on the giant's terms, matching bronze to bronze and size to size, was to have already lost the only fight that mattered. And he went down into the valley with nothing but his shepherd's staff and his sling and five smooth stones that he stopped and chose out of the brook on the way down — five smooth stones out of the running water, the deep's own stones, worn round and true by the moving water, and there is the old rhyme again if you want it, the boy arming himself out of the flowing deep against the armored terror of the world — and the giant looked at the boy coming with a stick and laughed, am I a dog, that thou comest to me with staves, and cursed him by his gods, and the boy called back across the valley the truest battle-cry in the book: thou comest to me with a sword, and with a spear, and with a shield; but I come to thee in the name of the God of the armies, whom thou hast defied. Thou comest with bronze. I come with the name. And he ran — mark that, he ran toward the giant, the only man on either hill not frozen — and slung one of the deep's smooth stones, and it was enough, one stone was enough, and the terror that had held two armies frozen for forty days fell down in the dust with a shepherd's pebble in its forehead. And the meaning under the children's tale is this: that the thing the world holds over you to keep you frozen, the armored roaring size of it, is not measured against your size but against the presence you have learned, alone in the unwitnessed dark, to trust — and measured against that, the giant is already falling, has always already been falling, and only your arithmetic of fear keeps him standing.

So the boy became a man, and a captain, and the people sang about him in the streets, and the songs gave more to the boy than to the tall king, and the fear in the king heard the songs and turned, finally and wholly, into the thing that would destroy him. Jealousy. The accuser found its deepest hold in him — this boy is loved more than I am, and the crown will go to him and not to my son, and the presence that has left me is with him — and the tall king, who had once hidden humble among the baggage, spent the rest of his life hunting the boy across the wilderness with an army, trying to pin him to a wall with a spear, a great man wholly eaten from the inside by the fear of losing a thing he had never really wanted and could not keep. And the boy, running for his life from the mad king, was given, twice, the chance to end it — twice the king came, unknowing, within a knife's reach of the boy hiding in the dark, once asleep in a cave the boy was already hiding in, once asleep in his own camp with the boy standing over him in the night — and both times the men with the boy whispered now, now, the presence has delivered your enemy into your hand, strike — and both times the boy would not. He would not lift his hand against the king. He said the man is still the anointed one, the oil is still on him, it is not mine to seize, I will wait for the presence to do it in its own time and keep my hands clean of it. And that refusal, in the dark of the cave, is the whole difference between the two men laid bare, the reason one of them is the great king of the book and the other its great warning — the failing king could not wait, forced himself, grabbed the holy thing with his own hands; and the coming king, with his enemy asleep and helpless in front of him and every counsel urging him on, would not grab, would wait on the unseen even when waiting might cost him his life. One seized. One trusted. That is always, in this book, the whole of it.

And now I must set beside the kings, before the great king comes into his glory, a small and quiet story, a story with no giants in it and no thrones, only two women and a road and a field of barley — because it is the truest answer the book gives to the whole roaring question of the kings, that the thing which actually holds the world together and carries the promise forward is not the crown and not the army and not the visible strength the people cried for, but this, a loyalty between two poor women that crossed a border it was not supposed to cross.

There was a famine in the land, in the days of the judges, when the wheel was turning, and a man of the town whose name means house of bread — and there is a bitter joke in that, that the house of bread went hungry — took his wife and two sons and left the land of the promise altogether and went to live in the country of an old enemy people, the country of Moab, to find food. And in that foreign country the man died, and his wife was left with her two sons, and the sons married women of Moab, foreign women of the enemy people, and one was named Ruth. And then, in that foreign country, both sons died too, both young, childless, and the old woman was left with nothing at all — no husband, no sons, no grandchildren, an aging widow in a foreign land with two foreign daughters-in-law and no man in a world that had no place at all for a woman without a man, stripped to the absolute bottom of what a life could be reduced to, which was exactly nothing. And she heard the famine in her own country had ended, that there was bread again in the house of bread, and set out to go home to grow old and die among her own people, and the two young widows started out on the road with her. And on the road she stopped and turned to them and did the most generous and most hopeless thing, told them to go back — go back, my daughters, to your mothers' houses, to your own people, to your own gods, you are young, you can marry again among your own, I have nothing to give you, no more sons in my body to be husbands for you, go back and live. And she kissed them, and they all three wept there in the road, and one of the young women, weeping, did the sensible thing she had every right and reason to do, kissed the old woman and turned and went back to her own people, and there is no blame in the book for her, none, she did nothing wrong, she went home. But the other one, Ruth, would not go. She clung to the old woman there in the road, and when the old woman pressed her again — see, your sister has gone back to her people and her gods, go after her — the foreign widow answered with words that have been said at weddings and gravesides for three thousand years by people who have forgotten they were first spoken by a destitute foreign woman to her mother-in-law on an empty road with nothing to gain by them: entreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee; for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge; thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God; where thou diest, will I die, and there will I be buried; the LORD do so to me, and more also, if aught but death part thee and me.

Stop on it. Because this is the ethic of the whole book — do not break, do not abandon, help everyone make it — said not by a prophet on a mountain but by a foreign nobody, a woman of the enemy people with everything to lose and nothing to gain, choosing to bind her one life to a broken old woman with no future, for no reason at all except love, except that she would not leave someone she loved to face the bottom alone. And here is what the book is quietly, deafeningly saying by putting this story exactly here, in the middle of the kings: that this loyalty, this refusal to abandon, this love that crosses the border it was told to stay behind, is what the presence at the root of all things actually looks like when it puts on a human face. The whole book has been trying to teach a people to trust a love that will not abandon them, that is there again every morning, that fed them the morning after the golden calf, that answered every time they cried out across all the turning of the wheel — and here, in a foreign widow on an empty road, that exact love is walking around in a human body, saying where thou diest will I die. Ruth is the manna made a person, the faithfulness of God wearing the sandals of a Moabite. And the God who looks on the heart and not the outward appearance, who pulled a harlot out of a falling wall and made her a mother of kings, was not going to let this loyalty go unfed any more than he let the hungry camp go unfed.

They came to the house of bread, the two of them, in the beginning of the barley harvest, two widows with nothing, and the young one went out to do the one thing the poorest of the poor were allowed to do, to glean — to follow behind the reapers and pick up the stalks they dropped, the scraps, because the shape the presence had given the people on the mountain, the covenant they thought was only a cage of rules, had in it this: that when you reap your field you shall not reap the very corners, and shall not go back for the sheaf you forgot, but shall leave the corners and the droppings and the forgotten sheaf for the poor and the stranger and the widow and the fatherless — that the law they called a cage was, in its own quiet body, a machine for making sure the abandoned were fed, built to leave the corners for exactly the destitute foreign widow now stooping in the barley. The shape was love with hands. And she gleaned in a field that happened — the book says happened, and lets you hear the smile behind the word, because nothing in this story happens — to belong to a good man of the dead husband's own family, a strong and generous man named Boaz, who came out to his reapers and saw the foreign woman stooping in his field and asked who she was, and being told, this is the Moabite woman who came back with the old widow, who has not left her, who works from morning without resting — being told, in other words, of her loyalty, this man answered loyalty with kindness, told her to stay in his fields where she would be safe, told his young men to let her glean even among the sheaves and to pull out stalks from the bundles on purpose and drop them for her, told her to drink from his own water when she was thirsty, and blessed her: the LORD recompense thy work, under whose wings thou art come to trust. And the old woman, when the young one came home at evening with far more barley than a day's gleaning should give, understood at once that a door was opening, because Boaz was not only a good man, he was a near kinsman of the dead — and in the shape the people had been given there was a provision for exactly this, with a name, the kinsman-redeemer, the near relative whose right and whose burden it was to buy back the land of a dead kinsman and take the widow to wife and raise up a child in the dead man's name, so that the abandoned line would not be blotted out, so that no one, not even the dead, would be simply erased. The whole covenant they called a cage was built full of these hidden doors for the abandoned, redeemers waiting in the structure, so that no widow and no orphan and no stranger would fall all the way through into nothing.

And it was done, tenderly and rightly and with honor on every side, the older man astonished and moved that the young foreign widow would turn to him and not to the younger men, the matter settled at the gate before the elders in the clear light of day, the land redeemed and the widow taken to wife, no one cheated and no one shamed and the abandoned line bought back from erasure — and the foreign woman, the Moabite, the enemy, the nobody who would not leave, bore a son. And the women of the town came to the old widow, who had gone out full and come home empty and now sat with her dead son's son on her lap, her emptiness filled from a direction she could never have looked, through the loyalty of a foreign daughter-in-law who, the women told her, is better to thee than seven sons — and the child grew, and had a son, and that son had a son, and that grandson, born down the line of a Moabite widow's refusal to abandon an old woman on a road, was the shepherd boy with the harp and the sling, the great king, the one this whole chapter has been climbing toward. David came out of Ruth. The crown the people cried for, when it finally came to rest on the head of the greatest of the kings, had been carried the whole way there, secretly, not by armies and not by thrones but by the loyalty of two poor women and the kindness of a good man leaving the corners of his field unreaped. That is where kings come from, the book is telling you, if you want to know. Not from the height and the bronze. From the love that will not abandon, gathering in the corners the strong forget. Keep that under everything that comes next, that his very blood was a mercy, that he was here at all because someone once refused to leave someone behind.

So the great king came at last into his kingdom, after the tall king fell on his own sword on a lost battlefield, broken and abandoned by the presence he had forced and grabbed and could not wait for, dying in the dark of a defeat with his sons dead around him, a warning, a grief, a decent frightened man the crown had eaten — and the shepherd, even at the news of the death of the man who had hunted him for years, did not gloat, but tore his clothes and wept and made a song for his dead enemy and for the king's son who had been his own beloved friend, a song so full of grief for a man who had tried to kill him that you cannot read it and still believe the coming king wanted the throne the way the fear wants things. The shepherd came up and was made king over all the people, and it was, for a season, the golden noon of the whole story. He took the old fortress city that no one had been able to take and made it the center, and brought the ark up into it, the gold box of the presence that had wandered in a tent since the desert, and when they brought it up the hill the king danced. He danced before it with all his might, leaping and whirling in a linen ephod like a common priest, like a boy, making a spectacle of himself in the street for joy, and his wife, the dead king's daughter, watched from a window and despised him for it, thought it beneath the dignity of a crown to leap in the street like that — and there is the last flash of the difference between the two houses, the one that measured everything by outward dignity and the man after the presence's own heart who would rather be undignified before God than dignified before men. He wanted to build the presence a house, a temple, to end the wandering of the tent at last — and the presence would not let him, but turned the whole thing around with a tenderness that is one of the high points of the book: you would build me a house, but I will build you a house — not a house of stone but a house of descent, a line, a throne that would not end, a promise that out of his own body would come one whose kingdom would have no end. The man who wanted to give God a roof was given instead a future, and the future ran out past the edge of anything he could see, past his own son and his own line and the fall of his own city, toward one who would sit on that throne forever, and the great king could only sit down before the presence and say who am I, that thou hast brought me thus far.

And if the story stopped there it would be too clean, and it would be a lie, and this book does not tell that lie, does not let its greatest king be a hero without a wound, because a hero without a wound teaches you nothing and this book is here to teach you the one thing that saves. So it tells the other thing. It tells that the great king, the singer, the man after the presence's own heart, at the very height of his power, with everything, with the kingdom won and the promise given and the throne secure — did a great and terrible evil, and the book does not hide it, does not soften it, does not make excuses for its hero the way every other nation's tellings polish their kings; it tells it plain, because the plain telling of it is the whole point. It was evening, and the king was on the roof of his palace, idle, at the season when kings go out to battle but this king had stayed home, and from the roof, in the low light, he saw a woman bathing, a beautiful woman, the wife of one of his own soldiers, a man named Uriah who was at that very hour out at the war fighting the king's battles while the king walked his roof. And the king wanted her, and he was the king, and a king, the old prophet had warned, will take — and he took. He sent and took her, another man's wife, and lay with her, and sent her home, and it would have been buried, one more thing a king could take and no one dared name, except that she sent word back that she was carrying his child. And then the great king, the man after God's own heart, did the thing that is worse than the wanting, the cold calculating thing fear does when it is trying to hide what it has done: he tried to cover it. He sent for the husband, the loyal soldier, brought him back from the front on a pretext, tried to send him home to his wife so the child would seem the husband's — and the soldier, more honorable than his king, would not go home to comfort while his comrades slept in the open field of war, but lay at the palace gate; and the king, cornered, his cover failing, did the last and coldest thing, wrote a letter and sent it back to the front in the loyal soldier's own hand, ordering the captain to put that soldier in the front of the hardest fighting and then to draw back from him so that he would be struck down and die. And it was done. The loyal man carried his own death sentence back to the war in his own hand and was set in the front and abandoned there and killed, and the king took the widow to be his wife, and thought it was finished, and it was not finished, because the thing the king forgot in his covering, the thing the fear always forgets, is that there is a presence that sees, that will not be carried on a pole and will not be managed and will not be fooled, before whom no roof is high enough to hide on. And the book says it, flatly, after all the covering: but the thing that David had done displeased the LORD. The cover held with every man. It did not hold with the one who sees the heart.

And the love did not strike him down. Mark that, because the fear-reading expects the thunderbolt here and the love does not send it. The love sent a man. It sent the old prophet, who came to the king and did not accuse him to his face, not at first, but told him a story — of a rich man with many flocks and a poor man with one little ewe lamb that he had raised in his own house, that ate of his own bread and drank of his own cup and lay in his bosom and was to him as a daughter, and how the rich man, having a guest to feed, would not take from his own great flocks but took the poor man's one lamb, the beloved one, and killed it for his dinner. And the king, hearing it, not knowing it was about him, was filled with a righteous burning fury, the fury of a just man at an injustice, and swore that the rich man should die and restore the lamb fourfold — pronounced, without knowing it, judgment on himself, and revealed by his own fury that he still, even now, even after what he had done, knew right from wrong, still had the heart that could burn at cruelty, which was the very thing that made him savable. And the old prophet looked at the raging king and said the four words that are the hinge of the whole book of the kings, the four words every one of us has to hear said to us once before we can be healed of anything: thou art the man. The rich man is you. The lamb was that soldier's one wife and that soldier's one life, and you had many, and you took his one, and then you took his life to cover the taking. Thou art the man.

And here is the whole reason this king is the great king of the book and the tall king before him is only its warning, and it is not that this king sinned less, because he did not — the first king only forced an offering and spared an enemy's cattle; this one took a woman and murdered her husband to hide it. The difference is not in the sin. It is entirely in what each man did when he was caught. The first king, confronted, made excuses, said the people made me do it, said I did keep the commandment really, bent and twisted and blamed and would not simply own the thing, and the fear kept its hold on him because he would not let go of the covering. And this second king, confronted with thou art the man, standing at the top of his power with an army that would have killed the prophet at a word, said four words back, the whole of repentance in the smallest possible space: I have sinned against the LORD. No excuse. No blaming. Just: I did it. It was me. And the moment he said it, the moment the covering came off and the truth stood bare, the prophet said the LORD also hath put away thy sin. Also. In the same breath. Not after years of penance — the instant the truth was owned, the sin was put away, because the covering was the only thing that had ever separated him from the love, and the love had been standing there the whole time on the far side of it, waiting only for I have sinned, and the moment it came the door was open, the door had never once been shut from the far side.

But the owning did not undo the wound, and the book is honest about that too, because a love that pretended the wounds we make simply vanish when we are sorry would not be a love, it would be a lie that let us keep wounding. The child conceived in the taking sickened and died, and the king, while it was dying, lay all night on the ground and would not eat and begged the presence for the child's life, and when it died he rose and washed and worshipped, and his servants marveled that he mourned less after the death than before it, and he said the truest thing about grief and mercy in the whole book: while the child was yet alive I fasted and wept, for I said, who can tell whether God will be gracious to me — but now he is dead, wherefore should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me. I shall go to him. Even in the ash of the worst thing he ever did, the king did not lose the one thread that held him — the knowing that the going was toward the child and not away, that even here the deepest direction of things was not loss but a gathering not yet complete. He had sinned as blackly as a man can sin, and owned it as wholly as a man can own it, and out of the owning came, of all things, a song — the song the broken-hearted have prayed over their own worst nights ever since: create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me; cast me not away from thy presence; the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise. Thou wilt not despise the broken thing. That is what the great king learned in the ruin of his greatest sin, and it is why his songs and not his victories are what outlast him, why the man is remembered not for never falling but for how he fell — all the way down, all the way open, with nothing held back and nothing covered, into the arms that were already there. The tall king fell and hid and was lost. The shepherd king fell and confessed and was gathered. It is not the falling that separates you from the love, for everyone falls; it is the covering. Uncover, and you are already home.

And out of that same marriage, the one that began in the blackest sin and passed through the broken and contrite heart, out of the very wound, when the grief had done its work and the love had put the sin away, came another son — and this is the mercy of the book at its most quietly staggering, that the line of the promise, the everlasting throne, ran on not through some later untainted marriage the king made in his righteousness but straight through the redeemed ruin of his worst hour, through the woman he had wrongly taken, through the bed that had begun in theft and blood and been washed in a broken heart, as if to say, one more time, in the deepest possible place, that the love does not build its future out of the parts of us that never broke, but out of the broken parts that were owned and gathered and redeemed, that there is nothing so ruined it cannot be made the root of a promise once it is brought naked into the truth. The second son was named for peace, and was called also, by the prophet, beloved-of-the-LORD, and it was he who came to the throne when the old king at last lay down and died full of years — the shepherd, the singer, the giant-killer, the adulterer, the murderer, the penitent, the man after God's own heart not because his heart was clean but because when it was filthy he did not hide it. He died, and the son of the wound became king.

And the son began, as his father had begun, and as the tall king before him had begun, at a height, in a genuine grace. For the presence came to the young king in a dream at the very start, when he was newly on the throne and knew himself too young and too small for it, and said ask what I shall give thee — offered him, openly, anything, the blank check of God to a new king, riches, long life, the deaths of his enemies, anything he could name. And the young king, and this is the best thing he ever did and the reason the book loves him at the start, did not ask for any of the things the fear asks for. He said I am but a little child; I know not how to go out or come in; and thy people are a great people, too many to be numbered; give therefore thy servant an understanding heart, that I may discern between good and bad. Give me a listening heart. Give me the wisdom to judge your people rightly. That is all I ask. And it pleased the presence beyond measure that of all the things a young king could have grabbed at, he grabbed at the one thing that was not for himself, at the power to do right by the people rather than the power to do well by himself, and the presence gave him the wisdom he asked for and, because he had not asked for them, gave him also all the rest, until he was the wisest and richest king the people had ever known. And the wisdom was real, and the book shows it to you at once in a scene as sharp as a blade: two women came before the young king, two who lived in one house and had each borne a child, and one child had died in the night, and each claimed the living child was hers and the dead one the other's, and there were no witnesses, no way on earth to know, an unjudgeable case. And the young king called for a sword. Divide the living child in two, he said, and give half to the one and half to the other. And one woman said yes, let it be neither mine nor thine, divide it — and the other, the true mother, cried out no, give her the child, do not kill it, let her have it, only let it live — and the king said give the living child to the woman who would rather lose him than see him die, for she is the mother. Because love would rather give the beloved thing away whole than possess half of its corpse, and by that, by the shape of love itself, the unjudgeable was judged, and the people heard of it and feared the king, for they saw that the wisdom of God was in him to do judgment. That is the height of him. Hold it, because it does not last.

And he built the temple. He did the thing his father had longed to do and been kept from, gave the wandering presence a house at last, and it was the glory of the age, seven years in the building, cedar from the far mountains and stone dressed so fine that no hammer was heard on the whole site while it rose, and gold within and gold without, gold on the walls and the floor and the great winged figures in the innermost dark, a house for the God with no face more splendid than anything the people had ever raised. And when it was finished they brought the ark up into the innermost room, the holy of holies, the dark windowless heart of it where no image stood and nothing could be seen, only the presence, and the cloud came down and filled the house so that the priests could not stand to serve for the cloud — the same cloud that had stood on the smoking mountain and filled the tent in the desert, come now to fill the house of stone. And the young king stood before all the people and blessed the house and prayed, and in his prayer, at the very summit of his achievement, with the most magnificent temple in the world standing new and gold around him, he said the truest and most dangerous thing, the thing that held the seed of everything that would go wrong, and said it, to his great credit, knowing it was true: but will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee; how much less this house that I have builded? He knew. Even as he stood in the finished glory of it, the wise king knew what the whole wilderness had been trying to teach — that the presence cannot be housed, cannot be walled into even the most golden room and kept there where you can find it, that a house for God is a mercy for the people, a place to turn toward, but never a box that holds him, that the moment you believe the gold room contains the presence you have built a golden calf the size of a temple. He knew it, and said it out loud at the dedication — and then, across the long slow years of his reign, he forgot it, the way they all forgot, the way the whole book is the story of forgetting the thing you yourself once said out loud and knew.

For the forgetting of the wise king is the slowest and saddest in the book, because it is not a fall, not a single black night like his father's, not a giant grab like the tall king's — it is a drift, so slow that there is no day on which you could say now, today, he has turned, and that is exactly what makes it the most frightening, because it is the shape of how the strong and the wise and the blessed actually lose themselves, not in a crash but by inches, each inch reasonable, each inch small, the coals graying so slowly that no single evening is the evening the fire went out. He was rich, and the riches grew, and the warning of the old prophet came true one slow inch at a time — a king will take. He built his own house, and it took thirteen years to the temple's seven, and there is a whole sermon in that ratio. He gathered horses and chariots and gold beyond counting, and to hold his kingdom safe he married the daughters of the kings around him, a wife from this kingdom to seal a treaty and a wife from that one, and the wives came with their gods, their little visible gods with their altars and high places, and the wise king, to please his many wives and keep the peace of his great household, built them their altars — built, with the same hands that had built the temple, high places for the gods of the nations on the hills around the holy city, and by inches, by the slow graying, the heart that had asked only for a listening heart to judge God's people rightly went after other gods, and was not wholly the presence's anymore. And it was not that he stopped believing. It was subtler and sadder and far more like our own drifting than a clean apostasy — he simply let everything in, kept the temple and added the high places, kept the God with no face and added the gods with faces, until the man who had built the one house where no image stood was maintaining a hillside of images to keep his marriages calm, and could no longer have told you the day it happened, because there was no day. There was only the drift.

And under the drift, feeding it, was the taking, the thing the prophet had warned of first of all — for the gold and the horses and the temple and the palace and the wives all had to be paid for, out of the backs of the people, and the wise king laid heavy labor on the land, levied the men of his own kingdom into work gangs to quarry his stone and raise his buildings, a burden of forced labor and heavy taxes pressed down on the free-born children of the ones who had come out of Egypt — and there is the deepest and darkest of all the rhymes in the chapter, the one that should make you set the book down for a moment when you see it: that the son of the shepherd, the grandson of the loyal foreign widow, the wisest king in the world, standing in the golden temple of the God who broke the house of bondage and drowned the taskmasters in the sea, had built, out of the labor of the very people that God had freed, a new house of bondage, had laid the brick-weight back on their necks, had become, by the slowest and most golden of drifts, a new Pharaoh in the promised land. The people had cried give us a king that we may be like the other nations, and a king will take, the old prophet had said, and here at the most golden noon of the whole story the taking had come all the way around, and Egypt — the Egypt they had spent forty years unlearning — had been rebuilt in the middle of the holy land by the hands of the wisest man who ever wore the crown, one reasonable golden inch at a time.

And so the fault lines opened, though the great king did not live to see them tear. It was told to him, near the end, by a prophet, that because his heart had turned the kingdom would be torn — not all of it, not in his own day, for his father's sake and the promise's sake, but from his son, and torn nearly in two, the great united kingdom that was the summit of the whole story split down the middle by the very burden he had laid on it, so that when his son came to the throne and the people begged the new king to lighten the heavy yoke his father had laid on them, and the young fool, listening to his young friends instead of the old counselors, answered that where his father had beaten them with whips he would beat them with scorpions — the north rose up and tore itself away, and the one kingdom became two, and the golden noon was over. And the long afternoon of the divided kingdoms began, the afternoon that would end, both halves of it, in the thing the whole book has taught you to read rightly by now: in exile, in the people carried away from the land into the countries of their conquerors — which the fear-reading calls the final punishment and which you, if you have learned anything from the flood and the scattering of the tower and the forty years in the sand, will read as what it truly is: not an execution but a reset, not an abandonment but a stripping, the taking-away of everything the people had trusted instead of the presence, so that in a foreign country by a foreign river, with nothing left, no house to hold their God and no king to fight their battles and no land under their feet, they might at last be left alone with the one invisible thing that had been the only real thing all along, and learn the lesson the golden noon could never teach: that the whole splendor of the kings had been, in the end, a longer and more golden version of the same golden calf, a thing they could see set up in the place of the thing they could not — and that when it was all melted down and carried off, the God with no face would still be there, in the desert of their loss as in the desert of their freedom, there again in the morning, feeding them, walking them home.

But that is ahead, a later chapter's grief, and this chapter must end where its own height and its own first cracks are, at the top of the arc with the flaw already visible in the gold — not fallen yet, but no longer rising, with the taking already begun, the drift already underway, the fault line already drawn through the golden floor by the God who saw the heart and grieved. The people had asked for a king they could see, and they had been given three, and the three of them together are the whole answer the book gives to that asking: the tall one who looked the part and could not carry it, who forced and grabbed and hid and was lost; the shepherd who did not look the part and carried it anyway, who fell as low as a man can fall and did not hide, and was gathered, and out of whose broken and contrite heart the promise ran on; and the wise one, the golden one, who had everything, who knew the truth and said it out loud and then drifted from it by inches until he had rebuilt the very bondage the whole story was walking away from. And under all three, holding the promise while the crowns rose and fell and cracked, ran the quiet thing that was there before the kings and would outlast them, the loyalty of a foreign widow on an empty road, the kindness of a man leaving the corners of his field, the love that will not abandon and does not sort and gathers in the edges what the strong forget — for that, and not the crown, was the throne the presence was really building, a throne that would be sat on at last not by the tall one or the golden one but by a descendant of the shepherd and the widow, born in the shepherd's own small town, who would wear no gold and lift no sword and take nothing from anyone, who would be the king the visible kings could only fail to be, and who would rule, when he came, not from a palace but from the low place, the broken place, the corner of the field, the empty road, gathering, gathering, leaving no one behind.
