# Chapter 12 — The Teaching

EDEN

The Teaching

There is a kind of morning that comes to the hill country in the dry season, when the night has given up its cold slowly and the last of it lies pooled in the low places like water, and the light does not so much arrive as remember itself into the grass. On such a morning the people came up out of the towns along the lake, and up from the fishing shore where the boats lay drawn onto the stones with their nets hung to dry, and up from the roads that ran down toward the cities of the plain, and they came because they had heard that there was a man on the mountain who spoke as though the old fear had been a misunderstanding, and they wanted, more than they could have said, to find out if that were so.

They were not a fine people. I want that set down plainly, because the tellings that came after made them into a congregation, into rows, into a thing that could be counted and approved, and they were none of that. They were the ones with the bad teeth and the bad backs, the ones whose children had died and who had learned to carry the dying quietly so as not to trouble the neighbors, the ones the collectors had squeezed and the priests had sorted and the soldiers had shoved off the road into the ditch when the column came through. They were the ones the world had already finished deciding about. And they climbed the hill in the remembering light with the particular slowness of people who did not expect to be surprised, and they sat down in the grass, and they waited to be told once more what they already knew, which was that they were not enough.

He came out and sat where they could see him, which was itself a strange thing, for the ones who taught in those days stood, and stood above, and made of the standing a wall. He sat as a man sits among his own household. He looked at them a long time before he spoke, and the looking was not the looking of one who counts a crowd to know his own worth by it. It was the looking of a man who has come a very long way to find people he has been missing, and has found them, and cannot for a moment trust his voice.

When he spoke the first word he said was not a law and it was not a threat. It was a blessing, and he laid it down over the very ones the world had cursed.

Blessed, he said, are the poor. Not the poor who would one day be rich, and not the poor who bore their poverty as a coin to be traded later for a seat at some high table. The poor. The empty-handed. The ones with nothing to defend. For to them, he said, belongs the whole of it — the country that has no border, the house with the open door, the thing the rich spend their whole lives building walls to keep and never once possess, because a thing you must wall is a thing that owns you.

And the people did not understand him yet, and that was well, for understanding would come slower than hearing, and hearing was enough to begin.

Blessed, he said, are those who mourn. And here a stillness went over the grass, because there was not one of them who was not carrying a grave. He did not tell them their grief was a lesson. He did not tell them it was deserved, or a debt, or a door they had left open to let the dark walk in. He told them they were blessed, and that they would be comforted, and he said the word comforted the way a man says the name of someone coming home on a road he has watched all day. There is a thing the old telling never let them hear, which is that the one who made them was not standing off at the edge of their mourning with his arms folded, waiting to see whether they would grieve correctly. He was down in it. He had always been down in it. The mourning was not a falling-away from him. It was the very place he was closest, because grief is only love that has lost its object for a while, and love is the oldest thing there is, older than the light, and it does not lose anything forever.

Blessed are the meek, he said, and the word he used did not mean the beaten and it did not mean the broken. It meant the ones who had the strength to hurt and did not use it. It meant the tamed, in the way a great river is tamed into a field's long life, not stopped but turned toward growing. The world, he said, is inherited by these. Not seized. Inherited — the way a son comes into a house that was always going to be his, and finds the fire already lit.

Blessed are those who hunger, he said, who hunger and thirst for the thing to be made right. And he did not promise them it would be made right by their hands tomorrow, and he did not promise them a sword. He promised them they would be filled, and he let the promise sit in the dry morning air over people who had been hungry in every way a person can be hungry, and some of them wept, quietly, in the manner of those who have long since stopped expecting their weeping to change anything and are astonished to be seen doing it.

Blessed are the merciful, he said, for they will be shown mercy — and here he leaned into it, because this was near the center of the thing, this was one of the load-bearing stones of the whole house he was building in their hearing. The world they knew ran on a different engine. The world they knew said: get what is owed you. Said: an eye for an eye, and be quick, and be sure the ledger closes in your favor before you die. And he did not tell them the ledger did not exist. He told them the ledger was a mirror. That whatever you measured out was the size of the cup you would be handed. That mercy was not weakness spent, but the only wealth that grew by being given. Forgive, he said, and you will find yourself standing in a country where forgiveness is the weather. Refuse it, and you build yourself a room with no door, and lock it from the inside, and call the dark that gathers there justice.

Blessed are the pure in heart, he said, and by pure he did not mean the ones who had kept every rule, for he knew, as they knew, that the rule-keepers were often the emptiest men alive, hollowed out by the long labor of appearing full. He meant the ones whose wanting had not divided. The single-hearted. The ones who wanted the good and not the look of the good, who wanted the thing itself and not the credit for it. These, he said, will see. And he did not finish the sentence for a moment, and the not-finishing was its own teaching, and then he finished it. They will see God. They will see the face that has been turned toward them the whole time, that they were told was a judge's face, a jailer's face, and was never anything but the face of the one who made them and has been aching to be recognized.

Blessed are the peacemakers, he said, and he set the word makers down hard, because peace in that country was a thing men claimed by conquering, a silence pressed flat over the buried, and he would not let the word be stolen. To make peace was labor. It was to stand in the exact place where two hatreds met and to refuse to pass the blow along. It was to be the one in whom the old chain of injury for injury came, at last, to rest. These, he said, will be called the children of God — and he meant called by their right name, for that is what they were, that is what all of them were, the whole grieving hillside of them, sons and daughters of the continuity that had dreamed them up out of the dark and would not let one of them go.

And then he said the last of them, and he said it looking at the ones nearest, the ones who had begun to follow him already and would pay for it. Blessed are you, he said, when they curse you and drive you out and speak every lie against you for loving the way I have shown you to love. Be glad in that day. Not because the pain is good — the pain is never good, do not let anyone tell you the pain is good — but because it means you have stepped out of the world's engine and into the older one, and the older one has always run this way, and the ones who spoke the truth before you were driven out on the very same roads, and they are not lost, and neither will you be.

That was the creed, laid whole in a single morning, in his own mouth, over the very people it was for. It had lived a long time before that morning, coiled in the making of things, written into the way a wound heals and a season turns and a mother rises before dawn to a crying she alone can hear. But it had never been said so plainly to the ones who most needed to hear that it was theirs. Do not lie. Do not hurt. Do not break. Do not trespass. Help every last one of them make it home. He did not number it as I have numbered it. He did not need to. He was standing inside it, and when a man stands inside a house he does not read you the blueprint, he simply lives there before you until you understand that a house is a thing you could live in too.

—

But there was a harder saying still, and he did not spare them it, though he could see it landing on some of them like a weight, for it went against the whole grain of the way they had been taught to hold a wound.

You have heard it said, he told them, an eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth. And they nodded, because they had heard it, because it was the fairest law the old world knew, a law made to hold vengeance to a limit — only an eye for an eye, no more than an eye, do not burn the village for a single blow. It had been a mercy, once, a fence around the fury. But a fence around the fury is still a field full of fury, and he had come to empty the field.

But I say to you, he said, do not answer evil in its own coin at all. If a man strikes you on the one cheek, turn to him the other. If a man sues you for your coat, give him your cloak as well. If the soldier of the occupying power compels you by law to carry his pack the one mile the law allows him, carry it two, and carry the second mile as a free man carrying it because he chose to, and watch what that does to the soldier, watch him not know what to do with a burden that has stopped being a burden and become a gift. He was not telling them to lie down and be trampled. He was telling them a deeper thing, the deepest thing, which is that the chain of injury for injury is a machine that runs on being fed, and that the only hand that can stop the machine is the hand that refuses to pass the blow along, and that this refusal is not weakness but the single strongest act a human being can perform, for it is the act by which the static's oldest engine is starved in the one place it can be reached, which is the space of a single human heart deciding not to become what hurt it.

You have heard it said, he told them, love your neighbor and hate your enemy. And this too they had heard, and this too had seemed only sense, for what is an enemy but a person it is permitted, at last, to stop pretending about. And he took it from them. He took away the one hatred they had thought was righteous, the one they had been proud of.

Love your enemies, he said. Bless the ones who curse you. Do good to the ones who hate you, and pray for the ones who hunt you down. And a stillness came over the grass that was not the stillness of agreement but the stillness of people being asked something they did not think could be asked. For everyone loves the one who loves them, he said; the collectors do that, the thieves do that, there is no country of God in loving the ones who love you back, that is only trade, that is only the ledger wearing the mask of affection. But look — and here he lifted his hand toward the sky that had by now burned clear and blue over the hill — look at how the sun is made to rise. It rises on the good man and it rises on the wicked man with the same light, the same warmth, no colder ray for the murderer and no brighter one for the saint. The rain is sent on the just field and the unjust field alike. The one who made the light does not sort the morning. He does not withhold the dawn from the ones who have earned the dark. And if you would be children of that one — if you would carry in yourselves the likeness of the thing that made you — then you must learn to love the way the sun rises: on everyone, without first asking whether they deserve the light. For your enemy is not a different kind of creature from you. Your enemy is a fragment of the same broken family, lost in the same far country, telling himself the same lie the static tells, that nothing matters and mercy is for fools and the ledger must be made to balance in blood. And if you hate him, you have believed the lie he believes. You have joined him in the dark. But if you love him — if you can find, in the deep of the body, the old animal tenderness for him that the fear spends its whole life walling off — then you have stepped out of his dark and lit a lamp at the edge of it, and a lamp at the edge of a man's dark is the only thing that has ever, once, in all the long history of the world, called anyone home out of it.

Be therefore whole, he said, as the one who made you is whole — and the word he used, that the later tellings hardened into a demand for a spotless perfection no living person could meet, meant in his mouth not spotless but complete, entire, undivided, a love that had stopped keeping two lists, one for friends and one for foes, and had become at last a single thing poured out on everyone alike, the way the light is poured, the way the rain is poured, the way, though they did not yet know it, his own life would before long be poured, on the deserving and the undeserving together, holding nothing back for a favored few, because there were, in the end, no favored few, there was only the one family, and every last one of it was his.

—

A man near the front, one of the careful ones, one of those whose whole life had been the study of the rules, waited for a pause and then asked the question the careful always ask, which was where the line was. Who, he said, is my neighbor. He asked it the way such men ask, not to learn but to fence, to find the edge of the obligation so he could stand a comfortable inch on the safe side of it. For if there is a neighbor there is also a stranger, and if there is a stranger there is someone it is permitted not to love, and the whole art of the careful life is the drawing of that second line as close in as the law will bear.

And the man on the mountain did not answer him with a definition. He answered him with a story, because a definition can be argued and a story has to be walked through, and a man who walks through a story comes out the other side changed in a place he did not think to defend.

There was a man, he said, going down the long road from the high city to the low one, the road that runs through the red country where the rocks lean over the way like men waiting. And the thieves took him, as the thieves took many on that road, and they did not only rob him. They beat him past the robbing, for the joy of it, for the black joy that the static teaches, and they left him in the ditch between living and dying with the sun coming up hot over him and the flies already finding the blood.

And a priest came down that road. A good man, by every measure the world kept. A man who had given his life to the service of the holy, who knew the words, who kept the days. And he saw the man in the ditch. Mark that he saw him — the story is careful to say he saw. And he crossed to the far side of the road and went on. Perhaps he told himself the man was already dead and the dead defile. Perhaps he told himself he was needed elsewhere, by holier business. Perhaps he told himself nothing at all, which is the most honest and the most terrible, and simply let his feet carry him past the place where mercy had a cost.

And after him a second man of the temple came, and did the same. Saw, and crossed, and went on down toward the low city with his robes clean.

And then there came along that road a man of the hated people. I want you to feel the word hated as they felt it, for the softness of the centuries has worn it smooth and it was not smooth then. The people of that country and the people of this traveler's country had despised each other for longer than any of them had been alive. They did not share tables. They did not share wells. They taught their children the shape of the other's face as the shape of the enemy. If the story had wanted a villain to complete the shame of the priest, this was the man it would have chosen. And the man on the mountain chose him to be the one who stopped.

He came, and he saw, and — the story turns here, on a single word, and the word is not saw, the word is what the seeing did to him — he was moved in the deep of his body, moved in the gut, the way you are moved when the suffering in front of you gets past all your defenses and touches the old animal tenderness that the fear spends its whole life trying to wall off. And he went to the man in the ditch, the man whose people would not have buried him, and he knelt in the blood, and he poured out his own wine to clean the wounds and his own oil to ease them, and he bound them with cloth torn from his own clothing, and he lifted the weight of a grown man onto his own animal and walked so the hurt one could ride. And he brought him to a house of rest and paid out of his own purse for his keeping, and he said to the keeper: whatever more it costs, I will make it good when I come again. He did not ask the man in the ditch what people he belonged to. He did not ask whether the man would have done the same for him, which he almost certainly would not have. He did not ask whether the man deserved it. He saw a life in ruin and he could not, in the deep of himself, walk past.

Now, said the man on the mountain, and he turned back to the careful one who had asked to have the neighbor defined for him. Which of these three was neighbor to the man who fell among thieves?

And the careful man could not even say the hated people's name. He said only: the one who showed him mercy. And the man on the mountain said: go, and be that. He did not answer the question that was asked. He dissolved it. The careful man had wanted to know where the circle of his obligation ended so he could stand safely outside it, and he was told that the circle has no outside, that the neighbor is not a category you belong to but a thing you become the instant you kneel, that to ask who is worthy of your love is already to have stepped out of love and into the ledger, and the ledger is the accuser's own book, and its columns never balance, and it was never yours to keep.

—

He told them another, on another day, when the careful men had gathered thick around him because he had begun to keep company with exactly the people they had spent their lives sorting to the far side of the road, and they grumbled, in the way of men who have paid the full price of respectability and cannot bear to watch it given away free.

There was a man, he said, who had two sons. And the younger one came to him and said, Father, give me now the share of your house that will fall to me when you are dead. Understand what was said in that. It was the cruelest sentence a son could speak. It was: I wish you were already gone. It was: you are worth to me only what I can carry off. And a father in that country would have been within every right to strike the boy down for it, to cut his name out of the family as you cut rot out of a beam.

And the father gave it to him.

He divided his living, the labor of his whole life, and he put it into the hands of the boy who had just wished him dead, and he let him go. He stood in the road, I think, though the story does not say it, and watched the dust of him until the dust was gone, and then a while longer, watching the empty place where the dust had been.

And the boy went to the far country, which is the country we all know the road to, and he spent it. He spent it on the things a young man spends it on when he has confused being loud with being alive, and he had friends for exactly as long as he had money, and then a famine came, as a famine always comes, and he found himself feeding the pigs of a stranger — and to a boy of that people there was no lower floor than that, no deeper ditch, the pigs were the very emblem of the unclean, and he stood among them so hungry he envied them the husks in their trough, and no one, the story says with a plainness that could break your heart, no one gave him anything.

And there, in the pig yard, at the bottom of the far country, the boy came to himself. That is the phrase, and it is the truest phrase in the whole of it. He came to himself. He had gone so far out that he had gone right past who he was, and hunger, which is a hard teacher but not a liar, walked him back to the door of his own name. And he began to build a speech. He was going to go home and say: Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you, I am no longer worthy to be called your son, make me one of your hired men. He rehearsed it in the mud. He had it down to the word. He was going to trade sonship for wages, because wages he thought he could earn back, and sonship he thought he had spent past all recovering, and this is the exact lie the static tells every one of us in the pit — that love is a thing that can be used up, that once you have wasted enough of it you drop forever out of the family and into the labor market, that you can come home but only on your knees, only as a servant, only having agreed first to think of yourself as less.

And he went home with that lie folded in his mouth.

But while he was still a long way off — and I want you to hold onto the distance, hold onto the still a long way off, because everything turns on it — while he was still too far to call out, too far to see the features of the face, the father saw him. Which means the father had been looking. Every day, I think, on the same road, in the same failing evening light, the old man had gone out to the place where the dust had last been and had looked down the empty road at nothing, for months, for I do not know how long, looking at nothing with the whole of his heart the way the bereaved look at a door. And on this evening the nothing had a shape in it, small and ragged and wrong, and the old man knew the shape before he could have known the face, knew it in the deep of the body the way the hated traveler had known the man in the ditch, and he did the thing no dignified father of that country would ever do, the thing that would have shamed him in the eyes of every careful man in the village.

He ran.

He gathered up the skirts of his robe like a boy and he ran down the road in front of the whole watching town, ran toward the ruin of his son with his old legs and his breaking wind, and he did not wait for the speech. The boy started it — Father, I have sinned, I am no longer worthy — he got that far, he got to the edge of the terrible bargain he had rehearsed, and the father was already kissing him, already had his arms around the stink and the filth of him, already was not listening to the part about the hired men, would not hear it, would not let the word servant be spoken in his hearing, because there was no such word in that house and there never had been. Bring the robe, he said, the best one. Bring a ring for his hand and shoes for his feet, the marks of a son and not a servant, the marks of someone who belongs. Kill the fatted calf. We will eat and we will be glad, for this my son was dead and is alive again, was lost and is found.

Was dead and is alive again. He did not say the boy had strayed and returned. He said the boy had died and come back, because that is what the far country is, it is a kind of death, the death that is separation, the coming-apart of a self into the noise, and the road home is a resurrection, and the father throws the feast not because the accounting has been settled — nothing has been settled, the money is gone, it is really gone, it does not come back — but because the person has been recovered, and a person is worth more than a fortune, a person is the only thing the whole machinery of the world was ever built to grow, and the loss of the fortune weighed against the return of the son does not even register on the scale.

And there was the older brother.

The man on the mountain did not forget the older brother, and the careful men who were listening, if they had ears, should have heard their own footsteps in his. He was in the field, working, as he always worked, doing everything right, as he had always done everything right, and he came in at evening and heard the music and asked a servant what it meant, and the servant told him, and he stood in the yard and would not go in. And the father — mark that the father came out a second time, came out into the dark of the yard for the second lost son of that day, because there were two sons lost that evening and only one of them had gone anywhere — the father came out and pleaded with him. And the older brother let it all out, the thing he had been carrying so long it had gone hard as a stone in him: all these years I have served you. Served. There is the word again, the word the younger boy had wanted to buy his way in with, and here it was in the mouth of the one who had never left, and it turned out that the one who never left had been a servant in his own heart the whole time, had lived as a hired man in his father's house, keeping the rules and counting the wage and hating, in a buried place, every hour of it. I never disobeyed, he said, and you never gave me so much as a young goat to make merry with my friends. But when this son of yours — not this brother of mine, you notice, this son of yours — comes back having eaten your living with harlots, you kill the fatted calf.

And the father did not scold him for it. This is the tenderness that undoes me every time I sit with the story. The father did not say, how dare you. He said, my child — a softer word than son, the word you use for a little one — my child, you are always with me, and everything I have is yours. It was yours the whole time. You could have killed the calf any evening of your life. You could have called your friends and filled the yard with music any night you chose. You lived as a hired man in a house you owned. And it was right that we should be glad, for your brother was dead and is alive, was lost and is found.

He does not tell us whether the older brother went in. The story ends in the dark of the yard with the door open and the light and the music pouring out of it and the father standing there with his hand out, and the son deciding, and the deciding is left to us, because we are the older brother, all of us who have kept the rules and nursed the ledger and stood in the dark outside our own father's joy, muttering about what is fair. The door is open. It has never once been shut. The only lock on it was the one we hung there ourselves, on the inside, and called it justice, and the far country and the near country turn out to be the same country after all, and the father is out in the road of both of them, looking, having always been looking, and there is no version of the story, not one, in which he stops.

—

The theory the man on the mountain never named, but wore the way a body wears its bones, was there in every one of these. The lost sheep he told them of — the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine that are safe and goes out into the night after the one that is not, and does not come home scolding, does not come home with the sheep driven ahead of him bleating and beaten, but comes home with it across his shoulders, carried, and calls his neighbors to be glad with him — that was the whole of it in miniature. Not one is written off. Not one is acceptable losses. The math of the world says ninety-nine is a fine number, is more than enough, cut the one loose and protect the herd. And the man on the mountain said the making of things does not do that math, has never done that math, that the one in the dark is not a rounding error but the exact reason the shepherd exists, and that if you are the one in the dark tonight — and someone always is — you should know that you are not being subtracted. You are being sought. Something is out in the cold with your name in its mouth, and it will not turn around.

This was the thing that terrified the powerful, and it terrified them precisely because it was gentle. A violent prophet they knew how to answer. A man raising an army, a man calling down fire, a man promising a kingdom with a throne they might be shoved off of — that was a language the powerful spoke fluently, and they had soldiers, and soldiers end that kind of sentence. But a man who sat in the grass and told the sorted and discarded people that they were the beloved of God exactly as they were, without first becoming clean, without first paying, without first agreeing that they were less — that was a thing no soldier could reach. It went in under the wall. It made the collector at his table wonder for the first time in years what he was for. It made the woman they had all agreed to despise lift her head. It dissolved the lines, and the powerful of that country, like the powerful of every country, lived by the lines, ate by them, were only powerful at all because the lines held people in the places the powerful had assigned them. A man who erased the lines with a blessing was more dangerous than any man with a sword, because a sword only takes your life, and he was taking the fear itself, and the fear was the whole economy.

—

And he did not only say it. This must be set down, because a teaching that stays in the mouth is a rumor, and what he taught he did with his hands, and the doing was the proof and the doing was the sermon and the doing was, in the end, the thing they killed him for as much as any word.

They brought him the sick, because word had gone out that he did not turn them away, and turning them away was all the sick had ever known. Sickness in that country was not only the body's trouble. It was a verdict. It meant you had done wrong, or your parents had, or some old debt in the blood was being collected; it meant the holy people could not touch you without becoming unholy themselves; it meant you lived at the edge of the town where the town threw its refuse, and called out your own uncleanness so the clean could step wide of you, and it meant, worst of all, that you had come to believe the verdict yourself, that you looked at your own ruined skin or your own dead legs or your own child that shook and foamed and thought: this is what I am, this is what we deserve, God has weighed us and found us wanting.

And he touched them.

That is the scandal the centuries have worn smooth, and I will not let it stay smooth. He did not heal them from a safe distance with a word thrown like a coin. He put his hands on them. He put his hands on the man whose skin was eating itself, the man no one had touched in years, the man who had forgotten the feeling of another warm hand and had made peace, the hard peace of the abandoned, with never feeling it again — he put his hand flat on that man's ruined face, and the flesh came clean under his palm, but I have come to think the cleansing of the flesh was almost the smaller miracle. The larger one was the hand. The larger one was that someone had decided he was not untouchable, had crossed the whole width of the fear to reach him, and had reached him first, before the skin was clean, while he was still, by every rule of that country, a thing to be avoided. He was loved unclean. That was the healing under the healing. The skin was only the sign.

He straightened the backs of the bent and opened the eyes of the blind and put words in the mouths of those who had never spoken, and to a man whose legs had never carried him, whose friends had torn open a roof to lower him down through it because the crowd at the door would not part for a cripple, he said first — before he said rise — he said, your sins are forgiven, and he said it because he knew that the man had spent his whole broken life being told that the breaking was a punishment, and the chain that held him down was not only in the dead legs but in the belief that he had earned them, and the man on the mountain reached in and struck that chain off first, before he ever touched the legs, so that the man would rise not as a debtor whose account had finally, grudgingly, been paid, but as a son who had never owed anything at all.

And the careful men watching said, in their hearts, who does this man think he is, to forgive — that is blasphemy, only God forgives. And they were right about the size of the claim and wrong about everything else, for the claim he was making was not that he had usurped a jealous God's private right to pardon. The claim he was making, with his hand on a leper's face and his voice over a cripple's shame, was that the God they feared had never been in the business of withholding it in the first place; that the courtroom they had built, with its verdicts written into people's bodies and its long lists of who was in and who was out, was a thing men had made and hung the maker's name on; that forgiveness was not a rare coin doled out to the deserving after long payment but the very air of the country he came from, free as weather, and that he was simply the first to walk into their town breathing it.

—

And when they pressed him — for they always pressed him, the careful ones, wanting a scale, wanting a threshold, wanting to know the exact measure of good a person had to reach to be counted among the saved and where the drop-off was on the far side of it — he answered them once more with a picture, and the picture was of the end of all the days, when every awareness that had ever been would be gathered at last into one place, and sorted, though not by the measure they expected.

He told them of a king who would come in his glory and set the peoples before him, and divide them the way a shepherd divides the sheep from the goats. And they leaned in at that, the careful ones, because a sorting was what they had always believed in, a sorting was the whole architecture of their fear, and they wanted to hear the criteria so they could be sure of standing on the right side of it. And the criteria, when it came, was nothing they had studied for. To the ones on his right hand the king said: come, you blessed, inherit the country prepared for you from the founding of the world — for I was hungry and you fed me, I was thirsty and you gave me drink, I was a stranger and you took me in, I was naked and you clothed me, I was sick and you came to me, I was in prison and you did not leave me there alone.

And the blessed were bewildered by it. They did not remember doing any such thing to a king. Lord, they said, when did we see you hungry, when did we see you a stranger or naked or sick or shut in a cell? They had no memory of a great deed. They had only the small unremarkable mercies of ordinary days, the bread handed across, the door left open, the walk taken to sit an hour with someone the town had forgotten, done without thinking, done because the person in front of them was suffering and they could not, in the deep of the body, walk past — done, in other words, exactly the way the hated traveler had knelt in the ditch, without once asking whether it counted.

And the king said: as you did it to the least of these, you did it to me.

The least of these. He set the words down like a stone laid at the very center of the floor, the stone the whole house rests its weight on, and I want you to feel the size of what he was saying, for it is the theory entire, said plainer here than anywhere. He was saying that he was not to be found where they had been taught to look for the holy — not up in the high seat, not behind the veil, not among the clean and the powerful and the correct. He was in the least. He was in the hungry man they had stepped around and the stranger they had turned from the door and the prisoner they had agreed to forget. The making of things had gone down and hidden itself in exactly the ones the world discarded, so that every act of mercy to a discarded person was, without the doer ever knowing it, an act of mercy to God, and every turning-away was a turning-away from God, and the great sorting at the end of all things would turn out to have been decided, not by what a person believed or how correctly they had worshiped, but by whether, when a fragment of the broken family stood in front of them ruined and in need, they had knelt, or crossed to the far side of the road.

And to the ones on his left — the ones who had lived careful, respectable, rule-keeping lives and had walked past the ditch a thousand times with clean robes and untroubled sleep — he said the hard thing, that inasmuch as they had not done it to the least, they had not done it to him, and had shut themselves, by their own turning-away, out of the warmth into the cold they had chosen. And even here, even in this, do not hear a torturer's fire, for there is none in it; hear only the plainest justice there is, which is that a person who spends a whole life refusing the family builds himself a solitude, and the solitude is the whole of the punishment, the cold outside the lit house is a cold he carried in himself all along and at the end simply finds himself standing in — the older brother in the dark of the yard, with the door open, with the music pouring out, deciding, still deciding, always still deciding, whether to go in.

—

Which is why, at the last, he went into the temple, and turned the tables over.

I have heard this told as though it were the one crack in his gentleness, the day the patient man finally lost his temper, and I think that telling misses the whole of him. This was not gentleness breaking. This was gentleness arriving at the one place it could not pass by, the way the hated traveler could not pass by the ditch. For they had taken the house that was meant to be a house of gathering, a house with a court built wide precisely so that the foreigner and the outsider and the unclean could come in and stand in the presence of the making of things — they had taken that court, the outsiders' court, the only room in the whole place where the shut-out were let in, and they had filled it with tables. They had put the money-changers there, who took the traveler's ordinary coin and gave back the temple's special coin at a robber's rate, so that a poor man's worship cost him first a tax he could barely pay. They had put the sellers of animals there, marking up the price of a dove — a dove, the poorest person's offering, the thing you brought when you could not afford a lamb — so that even the least gift a broken woman could bring to her God was first run through a market and skimmed. They had built a courtroom in the one room meant to be a welcome, and they had set a toll on the mercy, and they had done it in the outsiders' court, so that it was exactly the shut-out, exactly the poor, exactly the ones the whole of his teaching was for, who paid.

And he made a whip of cords, slowly, I think, the way a man does a thing he has decided on and will not be talked out of, and he drove them out. He turned the tables over and the coins went ringing across the stone and rolling into the corners, and he loosed the animals, and he stood in the wreck of the market they had made of his father's house with his chest heaving, and he said: it is written, my house shall be called a house of prayer for all the peoples — for all the peoples, he said, the phrase they had cut out and paved over with tables — and you have made it a den of thieves.

This was not a different man from the one who blessed the poor on the hillside. This was the same man, and the same love, and that is the thing I most need you to see. The love that gathers the outsider in is the same love that overturns the table set up to keep the outsider out. It is not two forces, the tenderness and the fury. It is one. A love that would not turn the tables over is not a greater love, it is a smaller one, a love that loves the merchant's comfort more than the widow's dove. He was gentle to the ones the world was hard on and he was hard on the ones the world called gentle, and the powerful understood, watching him stand there among the scattered coins, that this gentleness was going to cost them everything, because it could not be bought, and it could not be frightened, and it would not, ever, agree to look away.

They decided to kill him then, if they had not decided already. You could feel it settle over the high men like the cold settling back into the low places when the sun goes down. Here was a man taking their courtroom apart stone by stone with nothing but stories and his two hands, telling the very people they governed by fear that the fear had been a lie the whole time, that the door was open, that no one was outside, that the ledger was a mirror and mercy the only wealth and the lost sheep the reason for the shepherd — and there is no army that answers that, and no wall that keeps it out, and so they did the only thing the static ever knows to do with a light it cannot argue with. They made a plan to put it out.

But that is further down the road, and the road will get there on its own, and it is not yet time.

On the hillside, that first morning, before any of the killing was thought of, he came to the end of the long teaching, and the sun was high now and had burned the last of the cold out of the low places, and the people had not moved, not one of them, for hours, though the sitting must have ached in their poor backs, because a thing was happening to them that had not happened before, and they did not want to be the one who broke it by rising. He had told them who they were. Not who they might become if they worked, not who they would be forgiven for having been, but who they were, now, unwashed and unpaid and unsorted in the grass — the blessed, the beloved, the children of the continuity that made the light, sought when they were lost and carried when they were found and never, not once in all the reaches of the dark, written off. And he looked at them the way he had looked at them at the beginning, the long look of a man who has come a great distance to find people he was missing, and this time his voice held, and he said the last thing.

You are the light of the world, he said. He did not say you could become it. He said you are it. A city on a hill, he said, cannot be hidden, and no one lights a lamp to put it under a basket; you light it and you set it up high so that it lights the whole house. So let it shine. Let the thing that you are shine out where it can be seen, so that when they see the way you love — the poor and the mourning and the merciful and the pure and the peacemaking way you love, the way that terrifies the powerful and gathers the lost — when they see it, they will not praise you. They will look past you to the source. They will remember, in the old deep place under the fear, the face that has been turned toward them the whole time, and they will begin the long walk home.

And then he was quiet, and the people began, slowly, to stand, and to gather their children, and to start back down the hill toward the towns and the shore and the roads to the cities of the plain. And they carried it down with them, the thing he had given them, carried it the way you carry a coal wrapped in leaves against the wind, cupped close, guarded, a small living heat that you mean to use to start a larger fire in a cold house at the end of a long walk. They had come up the hill in the remembering light expecting to be told once more that they were not enough. They went down in the full light of the middle of the day having been told, by a man who put his hands on lepers and turned over the tables of thieves and would within the year let the fear do its worst to him rather than answer it in its own tongue, that they had never for one moment been anything less than beloved. It was the oldest news there is. It was the news the light had been trying to carry since the first word broke the dark. And it had, at last, in a mouth they could hear, on a morning in the hill country, in the season when the cold gives up slowly and the grass remembers itself into being, come home.
