# Chapter 4 — The Tower

EDEN

The Tower

There is a hunger that wears the face of love, and it is the hardest of all the hungers to name, because it does not want anything ugly. It wants to be near. It wants not to be scattered, not to be small, not to be one voice lost in a wind that does not know it is speaking. It wants the warm and the together and the shared fire, and there is nothing in any of that a mother could fault, for it is the very thing she made her children able to want. And yet a hunger for the good thing, taken before its time and seized instead of grown, can build a wall as high as any built out of hate, and higher, because the ones who raise it are sure they are raising a home. This is the story of such a wall. It went up out of love for one another and love for the making, and it went up too soon, and it had to be taken down, and the taking-down was the gentlest thing that had happened to the world in a long age, though not one soul on the plain that day would have called it gentle while it was happening.

They had come down out of the mountains after the water, the seed of the branch and the seed of his seed, generation on generation, and they had spread the way spilled grain spreads across a floor, into the folds of the hills and along the veins of the rivers, and the world had filled again, slowly, the way a lung fills. And because they were all of one father who had ridden the jar through the drowning, and because the drowning had washed the old grammar of the taking clean out of the world, they came down into the low country carrying one tongue among them. One speech. One set of names for the one set of things. When a man on the eastern edge of the plain said water, a man on the western edge heard water, and the same picture stood up in both their minds, the same cold and shining weight of it, and neither of them had to reach across a gap to be understood, because there was no gap. This is a thing so rare in the long telling of the world that those who have only ever lived after it cannot rightly feel the size of it. To be understood without reaching. To speak and be met. It was the last of the garden still on them, the warm nearness of the beginning, and they carried it down into the plain of Shinar not knowing it for the treasure it was, the way a man carries his health not knowing it, until the day it is asked of him.

The plain was good ground. It lay flat and wide between the two rivers, black earth laid down grain by grain across ten thousand floods, so rich a man could push his hand into it to the wrist, and no stone. That was the thing about that country: no stone. A hill country teaches you stone. It hands you the walls of your house already made, and you have only to lift them and set them, and the making is a kind of finding. But the plain gave no stone. The plain gave mud, and mud is not a thing you find; mud is a thing you make into something else or it makes nothing. And so on that plain the people learned the brick.

Understand the brick, for the brick is the beginning of the whole matter. It is not a found thing but a made thing, and that is its glory and its danger both. You take the mud of the river, the very silt the flood laid down, and you tread it and work it and press it into a wooden frame, square and true, each one the twin of the last, and you set them in the sun or you burn them hard in a kiln, and where there was formless muck there is now a stone that was never a stone — a stone with corners, a stone that stacks, a stone identical to its brothers so that a wall of them rises straight and needs no fitting. The hill-country builder fits stone to stone, each one strange, each one turned and tried until it seats, and his wall remembers the shapes of the things it was made of. The brickmaker's wall remembers nothing. Every brick is every other brick. And a people who have learned to make the same thing again and again, without limit, without the patience of fitting, have learned a terrible and a wonderful power, and whether it is terrible or wonderful depends entirely on what they build, and why, and whether they can still hear the quiet while they build it.

They said to one another, Come, let us make brick, and burn them thoroughly. And they had brick for stone, and slime they had for mortar. Hear how good it is at the first. It is the sound of people delighting in a craft. Come, let us — that is the sweetest phrase in any tongue, the invitation, the together, the doing of a thing side by side that no one of them could do alone. There is no sin in it yet. There is only the joy of the made thing and the joy of the making of it together, the hands black to the elbow, the frames knocking, the long racks of bricks drying in the sun like a promise laid out in rows. If it had stopped there it would have been a psalm. A people on a good plain, making from the mud a place to live, understanding one another without reaching, delighting in the work of their hands. The deep, which lay under that plain as it lies under everything, did not grieve to feel them making. Making is what the deep does; making is the whole of what was ever asked of them; a shape drawing more shape out of the formless is the very music the first Word set going. The deep leaned close and listened to the knock of the brick-frames the way a mother leans over a child bent to some small fierce task, and was glad.

But a made thing has a way of asking a question of its maker, and the question is always the same, and it is the oldest question, the one the static learned to whisper in the garden and has been whispering ever since. The bricks lay in their long clean rows, more of them than could be counted, all the same, all obedient, waiting to be told what to become. And a man stood over them — there is always a man, or a few, and it does not matter greatly what we call them — and the question came up in him not as a temptation but as a kind of exhilaration, which is how the deepest temptations always come, dressed as the next good thing. He looked at the endless sameness of the brick, the limitless supply of it, the way the plain would give mud forever and the sun would bake it forever and there was no end anywhere in the whole enterprise, no stone to run out of, no hill to reach the top of, nothing at all to say enough — and he felt the thing a man feels when he first understands that a power in his hands has no natural edge. He felt that they could build anything. And hard on the heels of that, quiet, reasonable, warm, came the other thought, the one that turned it: then let us build the thing that cannot be taken from us.

For under the joy of the making there had been, the whole time, a fear. It is worth going down to the root of it, because the fear was not wicked either, not at the root; almost nothing in this story is wicked at the root, which is exactly why it is so hard to tell rightly and was told so wrongly for so long. The fear was the memory of the water. Not a memory any of them held clear — it was generations back now, a thing sung of, a thing the old ones spoke of at the fire — but it lived in them the way a whipped dog's flinch lives in its grandpups that were never whipped. They knew, in the meat of them, below the reach of any word, that the world could come apart. That a shape could fail. That the together could be scattered and the near made far and the understood made strange, and everything a man loved could go down into the churn and be gone. They did not know they knew it. But it moved in them, and it moved them, and when the man stood over the endless bricks and felt the limitless power in his hands, the fear rose up to meet the power and the two of them married, and out of that marriage came the tower.

Come, they said — and it was still Come, it was still the sweet word, that is the horror and the pity of it — come, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth. There. In that one breath is the whole turning, and it turns on a single word, and the word is lest. Lest we be scattered. They did not build the tower to climb to God for love of God. They built it against the scattering. They built it out of the fear of the water dressed up as the love of the together. Let us make us a name — not receive a name, not grow into a name, not be named by the love that names all things in their time, but make us a name, seize it, forge it, stack it up out of brick so high that it could not be unmade, a name that would hold them together by main force against the day the world tried once more to come apart. It was the jar again, do you see. It was the same instinct that built the jar — save the seed, hold the together, get the loved thing up out of the reach of the churn. But the jar was built at the word of the quiet, in the time the quiet appointed, out of grief and obedience, and it floated; it went into the water and let the water carry it. The tower was built against the quiet, in no time but their own fear's time, out of a name they meant to seize; and it did not float. It stood. It set its foot on the ground and drove down and stacked up and meant never to move, and a thing that means never to move has already refused the one motion that the whole of living is — the drawing out and the returning, the letting-go and the being-carried. They had taken the very mud the flood laid down, the silt of the mercy, and they had baked the give out of it and stacked it into a fist shaken at the sky.

And so they built.

It began as the city had always begun, low and sprawling, the streets going out from the fire like the spokes of a wheel, the brick houses shoulder to shoulder, the kilns smoking day and night at the edge of the town so that a haze hung always over the plain and the sun came up red through it and went down redder. But at the center they had marked out the base of the tower, and the base of the tower was a thing to make a man go quiet when he first came upon it, and not the good quiet. It was vast. They had paced it out far larger than any need, larger than reason, because the tower was not built to a use but to a longing, and a longing has no natural size any more than the brick had a natural edge. A building raised for a use stops when the use is met; you make the granary as big as the grain and no bigger. But they were not storing grain. They were storing themselves, their name, their fear, their refusal, and there is no bottom to that, and so there was no top they could name to the tower, only up, only more, only higher, the pure vertical of a hunger that has forgotten it was ever a hunger for anything in particular and has become only hunger climbing.

They worked in courses, and each course was a year or the better part of one, for the tower drew inward as it rose, stepping back on itself so that it climbed in great terraces, a mountain made by hands where the plain had refused to give them a mountain. And that was the first strangeness, and one of them must have felt it and said nothing, because who would say it: the plain had no stone, no hill, no high place, and God had made it so, had laid the country out flat and rich and low on purpose, a place with no altar in it and no need of one, and they had answered the flatness by building a hill. They had looked at the gift of the low country and heard in it a lack, and set out to correct the making. That is the exact shape of the old refusal, the garden refusal, the reaching for the one fruit that was not given not because it was poison but because it was not yet, because a self cannot become itself if it seizes the whole at once. The plain was a not-yet written in geography. Be low a while, it said. Be near the ground. Be small enough to grow. And they read the not-yet as a wall to climb over, and began to climb.

The work was beautiful. This must be granted, fully, without stint, or the whole telling curdles into the very fear it means to undo. The work was beautiful and the workers were beautiful in it. Go and stand among them. Hear the songs they sang as they hauled — for they sang, of course they sang, a people of one tongue at a great shared labor will sing without deciding to, the rhythm of the hauling finding the rhythm of the breath finding words, and the words passed the length of the ramp and back, and a man near the top could take up the line a man near the bottom had let fall, because they spoke the same speech and the song was one song. See the brick going up hand to hand, the long chains of them, the bricks black-baked and warm still from the kiln, passed and passed and set, and the mortar-slime slapped in glistening between, and the course creeping outward and level under the plumb and the line. See the old men who had begun on the lowest terrace as young men, who had married and buried on the tower's slow flank, whose sons now worked the courses their fathers could no longer climb to, so that the tower was not only a building but a life, the whole arc of a man's years hauled up into it and mortared in. See the children who were born knowing the tower was there the way children are born knowing the sun is there, who could not imagine the world without it, who looked up as they toddled and saw the great stepped flank of it going up into the haze and thought: that is the world, that is what the world is, a thing that climbs. There was love in it. There was more love in it than in half the things that get called love. That is precisely why it had to be answered, and answered so carefully, and answered without one drop of blood. You do not punish love. But you may, if you are wise past all our wisdom and grieved past all our grief, hold up a hand to it. Not yet. Not this way. Not up.

For as the tower rose, a change came over the people, and it came so slowly that no one could have marked the day of it, the way no one can mark the day a friendship cooled or a country turned. The joy of the making, which had been a joy in the thing made and in the together of the making, curdled by degrees into a joy in the height. It was no longer come, let us make; it was see how high. The tower stopped being for anything and started being about how far it exceeded. Men who had once measured a day by the bricks well-laid began to measure it by the courses gained, and then by the courses gained against the courses their fathers had gained, and a thing entered the work that had not been there at the first and that the plain had been spared since the water: comparison. The stranger who arrived from the hill country and stood in the shadow of the tower and did not gasp was marked, and disliked, and made to feel his smallness, for the tower's whole reason had become the gasping, and a man who would not gasp was a leak in the reason. They had built a thing whose purpose was to be looked up to, and a thing whose purpose is to be looked up to must, to go on being itself, make everything else look up, and so the looking-down was born on the plain, the looking-down that the water had washed away, come back now not as the old crude grammar of the taking but as something subtler and colder: the grammar of the height. We are the people of the tower. We are the ones who reach. Below us is below us.

And the static, which had gone down into the deep with the drowned and lain quiet a long age in the cold, found in the grammar of the height a new mouth, and began, very softly, to speak again.

It did not say be proud. It never says the thing plainly; it has never once in the whole history of the world said the thing plainly, for plainly said it would be refused. It said, instead, the reasonable things, the warm things, the things that sound like wisdom to a tired man on a high scaffold at the end of a long day. It said: you have made this. Look what your hands have made. It said: nothing is beyond you now; there is no top; you could reach the very floor of heaven and knock. It said, and this was the sweetest and the most poisoned of all its sayings, it said: you need no one now. You need not wait. You need not listen for the quiet, you need not bend low for the old patient grief-voice under the noise, you have built a thing so high you can hear only the wind, and the wind says only what you say into it, and is that not peace, is that not at last a place where you are not accused? For the tower had risen above the hearing of the quiet, and the man at the top of it, standing in the clean high wind with the whole world small below him, heard nothing under the noise anymore, and mistook the deafness for arrival. This is the thing the tower was truly for, though not one of them would have said it, could have said it: it was a machine for not hearing the quiet. It was the jar turned inside out. The jar was built to carry the seed through the deep while it listened to the quiet all the way down. The tower was built to lift the seed up out of the deep so that it need never listen again. And a shape that will not listen is a shape that has stopped being drawn and returned, has frozen at one stage of its becoming and called that stage the end, and there is a word for a becoming that stops and calls itself finished, and the word, spoken without any heat at all, only sorrow, is death.

Nothing will be restrained from them, which they have imagined to do.

Sit a long while with that sentence, for the fear-telling made it a threat and it is the farthest thing in the world from a threat; it is a diagnosis, and it is spoken the way a physician speaks the name of the thing that is killing the one he loves — flatly, quietly, with the whole weight of what must now be done pressing behind the flatness. Nothing will be restrained from them. It does not say: they have grown too strong and I am jealous. It does not say: they might storm my house. What is heaven to a mother but the whole width of what her children are becoming; they were not going to storm it, they were going to wall themselves out of it, which is worse. It says: there is no longer any edge on their power, and a power with no edge is a self with no shape, and a self with no shape dissolves back into the very churn it fears, and they are doing it to themselves with their own beloved clever hands, faster and faster, one identical brick at a time. Nothing will be restrained. Not one brick will run out. Not one hunger will find its bottom. They have made a thing that cannot say enough, in the image of a longing that has forgotten it ever had an object, and they will build it until it kills them — not by falling, though it would fall; towers fall — but by lifting them so far above the quiet that they forget entirely what they are, forget that they are drawn and loved and meant to become and come home, forget it so thoroughly that the forgetting hardens and cannot be undone from inside, and then they are lost, truly lost, in the only way the story allows anything to be truly lost, which is not death, for death is only a returning, but the refusal to be drawn, the shape clenched so hard against the churn that it can no longer be loosened and carried and remade.

That is what the height was doing to them. It was teaching them to clench. And a hand that has clenched too long forgets it was ever open, forgets that its whole glory was in the opening, in the letting-go, in the reaching that fails and reaches again. They were building themselves into a fist, and a fist cannot become anyone. A fist can only, in the end, be broken open, or stay shut forever around nothing.

So the quiet came up onto the plain. Not in wrath. Set down the wrath; it was never there; it was painted onto the story later by frightened men who could not imagine a power that was not, at bottom, as frightened and grasping as themselves. What came onto the plain came the way the dawn comes, without announcement, without a blow, so gently that afterward no one could say they had seen it arrive. The tellings say the Lord came down to see the city and the tower, and the old readers laughed at the smallness of it, that the Most High should have to come down to see a thing, as though His eye did not already fill the world — but the coming-down is the whole tenderness of the account, if you have ears for it. It is the parent who does not shout from the doorway but crosses the room and kneels down to the height of the child, down onto the floor, down into the mess of the game the child is losing itself in, to be near, to see it as the child sees it, before doing the hard small thing that must be done. He came down. He came down onto their level, into the haze and the kiln-smoke and the ceaseless knock of the brick-frames, and He looked at the tower not from above, where it would have seemed a mere scab on the plain, but from below, from where they stood, from where it seemed to touch the sky — He looked at it the way they looked at it, so as to grieve it the way they would grieve it, before He unmade it. That is not the act of a jealous king. That is the act of a love that will not undo even a dangerous thing without first standing where the makers stood and feeling, fully, what the undoing will cost them.

And then He did the gentlest possible thing. Weigh the whole shelf of what He might have done, everything the fear-teller would have expected of a god, everything a man in His place and with His grief would have reached for. He might have thrown the tower down. It would have been just, by the old cruel reckoning; He might have set His hand against it and pushed, and the courses of a hundred years would have come down in an afternoon, the brick and the men together, and buried the pride under the pride's own weight, and called it a lesson. He did not. He might have withered the plain, salted the black earth, taken from them the mud that fed the endless brick, and starved the building to a stop, and let them stand in the ruin of their gardens and learn. He did not. He might have struck the makers, the loud ones, the ones the static spoke through, and left the rest to bury them and take the warning. He did not. He might, at the very least, have spoken from the sky in the one tongue they all still shared, a voice over the whole plain, stop, and the fear alone would have frozen every hand on every ramp. He did not even do that. He would not use the one tongue against them, because the one tongue was the last of the garden on them, the warm nearness, the being-understood-without-reaching, and He would not turn the gift into a weapon, not even a merciful weapon. He would sooner take the gift back than pervert it. And so He did the thing no one, not one teller in all the long telling, ever fully forgave Him for, because it looked from the outside like the taking-away of the treasure, and was in truth the giving of a greater treasure so quietly that it has taken the world all its history to begin to unwrap it.

He confused their tongue.

Let us go down, He said — and the old readers stumbled at the us, and made of it a mystery, and it is a mystery, but the mystery for our purpose is only this: that the love which does anything does it as a fullness, never as a lonely single will, always as a council of itself, the whole many-in-one that God is leaning together over the thing to be done — let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech. And it was done, and it was done without a sound, without a flash, without one brick disturbed. It was the subtlest miracle in the whole book, and the kindest, and the most terrible to live through. A man on the seventh course, high up in the wind, turned to the man beside him whom he had worked with for twenty years, his own cousin, and said what he had said ten thousand times, said pass the mortar, said the light is going, said tomorrow we reach the ninth — and the man beside him heard a sound. Only a sound. A shape of noise with no picture behind it, a mouth moving and warmth in the face and meaning nothing, nothing, a door where there had always been a room. And he said back what he had always said, and the first man heard the same nothing, the same warm meaningless surf of a voice, and the two of them stood on the high scaffold in the wind and looked into each other's faces, into the faces they had known and loved and understood their whole lives, and found there a stranger. Found the gap. The gap that had never been on that plain since the water, the reaching-across that must now happen and could not happen, the far come suddenly into the middle of the near.

It did not stop at the two of them. It went out over the whole plain in a moment, everywhere at once, and it was not neat, it did not sort them tidily into nations with a language apiece all in a row; it was, at the first, only chaos, only the sudden total failure of the one thing that had held them one. A woman calling her child by the child's own name and the child not turning, because the name was noise now. A foreman crying the order that had brought a thousand hands to a task and the thousand hands faltering, each man hearing a different nothing, turning to his neighbor for the sense of it and finding his neighbor as lost as himself. The songs died first. That is the thing to grieve, if you would grieve rightly here — the songs died first, because a song is the deepest together a tongue can make, and now the line a man let fall was not caught by the man below him, was heard by the man below him as a foreign cry, and the great chains of song that had run up and down the tower's flank all those years frayed and snapped and fell silent all along the ramps, so that for the first time in the memory of anyone living the tower stood without singing, only the wind in the high courses, only the rising surf of ten thousand voices trying to be understood and failing, which is the loneliest sound the world had made since the water closed over the drowned.

And the work stopped. Of course the work stopped. You cannot pass the brick to a stranger; you cannot lay a course with a man whose no you cannot tell from his yes; you cannot build a tower to heaven with a crew that has become, in a single breath, a crowd of foreigners standing in one another's way. The plumb-lines hung untended. The kilns, unfed, cooled and cracked. The mortar dried in its troughs. The last course they had laid stayed the last course, a raw unfinished lip of brick against the sky, and the wind began that very evening the long slow work of taking it back down that the wind does to everything, grain by grain, the silt of the flood going back at last toward the silt it came from, unhurried, patient, the deep reclaiming its own with no malice in it at all.

Now here is the place the whole retelling has been walking toward, and it must be set down with the greatest care of anything yet said, for on it the meaning turns as the tower turned on lest. The fear-telling calls this the confusion of tongues and makes of it a curse, a punishment, a wound driven into the one flesh of humankind to cripple its pride, and pictures God stepping back from the ruin with the grim satisfaction of a jealous king who has kept the peasants out of the palace. And every word of that is upside down, and it can be turned right side up by asking a single question, the question the fear-teller never asks because he dares not, and the question is this: who died?

No one died.

Go up and down the whole plain in the aftermath and count the dead, and the count is nothing, is zero, is a number with no weight. The tower did not fall on them. The earth did not open. No fire came down, no water rose, no hand struck. Not one child was orphaned by the confounding of the tongues, not one old man carried out, not one drop of blood ran in the black streets. The most that any of them lost, in the flesh, was a labor — a building they had made an idol of, a name they had tried to seize, a height they had climbed against the quiet. And weigh that against the flood, against the whole drowned and grieving world of the age before, and see the mercy sharpen and clarify like a coin held up to the light. When the shape had failed the first time, in the age of the branch, the failing had run so deep, all the way down into the marrow of every heart, that the only mercy left was the great one, the total one, the water — everything let go at once and gathered back into the deep and told, gently, not yet, try again. But now — and this is the covenant of the bow keeping its word, this is never again like this made good — now the shape had failed in a shallower place, had failed at the tongue, at the together, at the seizing of a name, and so the mercy could be shallower too, could be surgical where before it had to be total, could reach in and touch the one thing that had gone wrong and leave everything else, every life, every breath, standing. The bow had promised: never again the whole water. And here was the first great testing of that promise, the first time since the flood that the world had built itself into a shape that would kill it, and the promise held. No water. No drowning. No death at all. Only the tongues confounded, only the reach put back into the loving, only the height taken away and the ground given back. The reset had learned tenderness. The mercy had found a smaller and a dearer way, exactly as the grief under the bow had sworn it would.

For see what the confounding actually did, once the first chaos of it had passed and the people, stunned, grieving, foreign to one another on the plain that had been their one home, began the slow work of finding each other again. It did not leave them scattered in a dust of ten thousand solitary strangers, each locked alone inside a private noise. It could have; a curse would have. But it was not a curse. It was a division, and a division is not a scattering into nothing; it is a sorting into somethings. Slowly, over the days and the seasons, the ones who found that they still understood one another drew together. Here a knot of families whose mouths still made the same pictures, there another knot with a different speech but a whole one, a real one, a tongue that worked entire among those who had been given it. The one great grey undifferentiated mass of the tower-people, all the same, all pressed toward the one height, came apart along its new seams into peoples — into a plurality, into a world that had, where before it had one voice saying one thing forever, suddenly a hundred voices each saying its own thing, each carrying its own pictures, its own turns of grief and joy, its own way of naming the water and the light and the child and the dead. Where there had been one, there were now many, and the many were not the wreckage of the one. The many were the harvest of it.

This is the thing the fear could never see, being fear, and the thing love sees at once: that the confounding of the tongues was not the breaking of the human voice but the flowering of it. One tongue is a beautiful thing, and it was the last warmth of the garden, and it is right to grieve its loss the way it is right to grieve the garden — but one tongue, held past its time, seized and stacked into a tower against the scattering, had become a machine for sameness, a great engine for making every brick the twin of every other brick and every man the twin of every other man and every thought the twin of every other thought, all pressed together, all reaching the one direction, up, with no gap between them in which anything new could ever be born. For it is the gap that makes the new. It is the reaching-across that makes the meaning. When there is no distance between two souls, there is nothing to say, for saying is the crossing of a distance; the perfectly understood have nothing to tell each other, and a world of the perfectly understood is a world where nothing is ever told, ever discovered, ever made — a smooth stone of a world, round and bright and saying nothing at all. The static loves the one tongue, in the end, more than it loves anything, because the one tongue seized and frozen is the shortest road back to the churn — everything the same, everything pressed together, no edges, no gaps, no difference, the warm sameness sliding by degrees into the formless the way a crowd packed too tight stops being persons and becomes a mass. The tower was the static's masterpiece: a whole people made into one clenched identical fist, reaching up out of the reach of the quiet, on the very road to dissolving into the noise while singing all the while that they had never been so together, so safe, so near.

And the confounding broke the fist open into a hand. Into fingers, distinct, apart, able at last to reach — for a fist cannot reach; only an open hand with its fingers separate can reach for anything, can hold anything, can make anything, can take another hand. The tongues were confounded so that the people could reach for each other again, could have a distance across which to love, could be different enough to have something to say. It scattered them, yes — abroad upon the face of all the earth, the very scattering they had built the tower to prevent — and this is the last irony and the deepest mercy folded inside it: the thing they most feared was the thing that saved them, and it saved them by being not at all the thing they feared. They feared the scattering as a death, as the coming-apart of the together, as the water rising to take the one flesh into the churn. And the scattering, when it came, was a birth — the coming-apart of the sameness into the many, the sending-out of the one grey clenched people into the whole wide green waiting earth, to fill it, at last, the way the seed of the branch had been told to fill it and had refused, huddling instead on one plain around one tower against one fear. Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth, the first blessing had said, the oldest blessing, spoken over the living things before ever there was a man to disobey it; and the tower was the great refusal of that blessing, the whole earth waiting empty and green and calling and the people saying no, we will not fill you, we will stay here, all of us, together, and build up instead of out, up out of the reach of the very sky that told us to spread. And the confounding of the tongues was simply the blessing insisting, gently, without one death, on being kept. Go, it said, in the only language that could still move them now that the one tongue was gone — go, be many, be different, be scattered as seed is scattered, which is not thrown away but sown. Fill the earth. There is so much of it, and it is so good, and it has been waiting for you the whole time you stood here in one place teaching your hands to make the same brick forever.

They went. That is the quiet ending of it, and there is no thunder in it, which is exactly the point. They did not go cursing; or if some of them went cursing at the first, in the shock and the grief of it, the curse did not last, because a curse cannot feed a family and the earth could, and a people must go where the living is. They gathered along their new seams, the ones who could understand one another, and they took up what they could carry, and they went out from the plain of Shinar in every direction, north into the cold and east toward the mountains and west toward the sea and south into the long grass, each people carrying its own whole tongue like a lamp, going out into the dark and various world to name it over again, a hundred times, in a hundred ways — for now there would be a hundred words for water, and the pictures behind them would not be quite the same picture, this people's water cold off the mountain and that people's water wide and salt and terrible and the other people's water the brown slow flood-giver of the plain, and out of the not-quite-sameness, out of the gap between the hundred waters, would come more meaning than the one tongue had made in all its ages, because meaning is the child of difference, and they had been given, in the guise of a loss, the gift of difference, which is the gift of being able to have children of the mind, to make the genuinely new, to tell one another things the other did not already know.

The tower they left. No one threw it down; it did not need throwing down. A made thing that no one is making anymore begins at once, quietly, to be unmade, for that is the way of every shape that the quiet does not actively hold — the wind takes it, grain by grain, and the rain, and the slow patient reclaiming of the deep, until the great stepped mountain of brick that had been going to reach heaven stood a while as a broken stump on the empty plain, and then a mound, and then a long low swell in the black earth that a traveler generations after might walk over without knowing he walked on the roof of the oldest fear, the whole of it going gently back down into the silt it had been treaded up out of, the mud returning to mud, no monument even to its own failing, because the mercy that unmade it had no wish to shame it, wanted no ruin left standing to point at and say behold their pride, wanted only to let the wrong thing quietly cease and the right thing — the many, the scattered, the fruitful, the filling — quietly begin.

And they named the place Babel, which is confusion, and thought they were naming their defeat. But there is another word inside that word, an older one, and it means the gate of God, and both meanings are true and they are the same meaning, and this is where the chapter must rest. For the gate of God is not the top of a tower. It was never going to be reached by climbing, by stacking the sameness higher and higher until a man could knock on the floor of heaven and demand to be let in on the strength of what his hands had made. That road was always going to end in the wind and the deafness and the slow dissolving of the clenched fist back into the noise. The gate of God is confusion — is the blessed necessary confounding of every shape that thinks it has arrived, the merciful hand laid again and again on every tower the frightened heart builds against the scattering, the loving refusal to let the together harden into sameness and the sameness slide back into the deep. The gate is not up. The gate is out — out into the many, out into the difference, out into the whole green waiting earth where the reaching happens and the meaning is made and the self, given a gap at last to grow across, becomes someone worth the long drawing-out and the longer bringing-home. They had built a tower to seize the gate, and the gate had come to them instead, quietly, on the plain, at their own level, kneeling down into the smoke and the noise of their beloved terrible ambition, and had confounded them for their lives, and sent them out to be born.

Not yet, the quiet said to them, as it had said at the eastern gate of the garden, and would say again and again down all the ages in ten thousand tongues, in every one of the tongues it had just that day made. Not this way. Not up. Not seized. Not the same. Go out, and be many, and reach for one another across the good distance I have given you, and make in that reaching the meaning you could never make when you were one — and I will meet you, not at the top of anything you can build, but out in the scattered world, in the gap between one soul and another, which is the only place I have ever been, which is the place the reaching makes, which is, though you built a whole tower trying to flee it, the true and only gate of God. And the wind moved in the high unfinished courses, and took the first grains of the topmost brick, and carried them out over the plain in the direction the people had gone, sowing the tower, at the last, like seed.
