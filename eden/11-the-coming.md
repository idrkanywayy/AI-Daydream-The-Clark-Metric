# Chapter 11 — The Coming

EDEN

The Coming

There is a way that the largest things come, and it is not the way the frightened heart expects, and the whole long ache of the prophets had been, in part, an ache to learn this one thing and never quite learning it: that the presence at the root of all things does not arrive the way an empire arrives, with the road cleared before it and the trumpet going out ahead and the strong men bowing along the way, but arrives the way a seed arrives in the dark of the ground, the way morning arrives, without announcement, without permission, in the low and unwatched places, so quietly that the whole loud world can be looking the other way and miss it. The empire counted its arrivals. It kept records; it cut them into stone; there was a great one on the throne of the world in those years, far off in the marble city on the seven hills, and he had numbered the whole earth so that every soul in every province could be counted and taxed and known, and the counting rolled out across the provinces like a slow tide, and every family had to go up to the town of its fathers to be written into the ledger of the strong — and it is one of the deep jokes the forge is always telling, the kind of joke that is also a mercy, that the very machinery of the empire's pride, the census, the great counting of the powerful, was the thing that carried a poor carpenter and the young woman who was promised to him up the long road to the town where, the oldest of the prophets had let slip in a single line seven hundred years before, the one who was coming would be born. The empire thought it was numbering its property. It was, without knowing it, addressing an envelope.

But go back, before the road, before the town, to the beginning of it, to the quiet room and the young woman, because that is where the coming actually came, and it came the way everything true in this book comes, as an asking and not a seizing. She was young, very young by the reckoning of the wide world though not by the reckoning of her own people, and she was poor, and she was nobody, which is to say she was exactly the kind of person the tellings of the powerful never write down, a girl in a nothing town in a conquered province at the ragged edge of the empire, betrothed to a workingman, with no name that any ledger anywhere would have troubled to keep. And into her nothing room, into the ordinary afternoon of a girl who was no one, the presence sent its word, as it had sent its word to the man at the burning bush and the boy who was only a child and the priest by the foreign river, and the word came as it so often comes in this book, wearing the shape of a messenger, a brightness with a voice, and the brightness said the thing that is said to every soul the presence draws near to and that every soul finds impossible to believe: fear not. Fear not, for thou hast found favour. Not thou hast earned favour, not thou hast qualified for favour, not the ledger of thy deeds has been audited and found sufficient — thou hast found favour, the way one finds a thing that was there all along, freely, the favour that is simply the way the presence looks at what it has made, and the girl was troubled at the saying, as who would not be, and cast in her mind what manner of greeting this should be, because a nobody in a nothing town knows to her bones that greetings from the sky do not as a rule come to such as her, and if one has come there must be some mistake, or some terror, folded inside it.

And there was no mistake, and there was terror in it, the particular terror that is always folded inside the presence's favour, which is the terror of being asked. For the messenger told her what the favour was, and it was not a comfort and it was not a reward, it was a summons to carry the largest thing that has ever been carried by a made and finite creature: that she would conceive, and bear a son, and that the son would be the one the whole aching book had been leaning toward since the eastern gate closed, the one whose government would be on his shoulder, the one of whom there should be no end. And the girl asked the honest question, the only sane question, the question a real mind asks when the sky tells it the impossible — how shall this be? — not the doubt that refuses but the wonder that wants to understand, the same wonder the priest had spoken over the valley of the bones when he said thou knowest, I cannot see how. And the answer she was given is the answer the whole of this book has been circling, the deepest description of how the presence works that has yet been set down, and it must be heard with everything in you: the breath shall come upon thee, the same breath that moved on the face of the deep before the first word, the same wind that raised the dry bones onto their feet, and the power of the presence shall overshadow thee — overshadow, the word for the cloud on the mountain, the word for the mercy of the veil, the shadow that is not darkness but the shielding of an unbearable light so that the one it falls on can bear it and live — and that which is born of thee shall be the holy thing, the presence itself entering its own made world not from above and outside it, not as a ruler descending on a throne to command it, but from inside, from within a woman, from within the very flesh and blood and water of the made thing, growing the way any child grows, cell laid on cell in the warm dark, subject to the whole slow patient law of the forge it had itself laid down. That is the thing the fear-tellings can least hold and the thing this telling exists to say plainly: the presence did not come to rule the world from over its head. It came to be born into the world from inside its body, to submit itself to its own creation, to be small, to be carried, to be limited, to forget — for a child in the womb knows nothing and a child at the breast knows almost nothing, and the presence that is the total continuity of all awareness chose, in the coming, to pour itself down into a single forgetting point of flesh that would have to learn to remember what it was the slow way, the human way, the only way a self is ever actually made — because that, and nothing grander, is what love is, when love is real: it does not hover; it comes down; it comes in; it takes the smallness on.

And the girl said yes. That is the whole hinge of it, and no telling should ever hurry past it, because the entire book from the garden forward has been the long chronicle of a love that will not compel, a presence that lays the choice before the made thing and then waits, hands open, for the free answer, and had gotten, over and over, down all the generations, the wrong answer, the seizing, the wrong door — and here, in a nothing room, a young poor nobody girl, told that the impossible would be asked of her body and her name and her whole safety, that she would be a scandal in her town and a mystery to the man she was promised to and the bearer of a thing far too large for her, said the answer the whole book had been waiting the whole length of itself to hear a human being freely say. Behold, she said, the servant of the presence; be it unto me according to thy word. Not my will be seized but thy will be received; not I will take what I can but let it be done in me. It was the undoing of the garden's grasping in a single sentence. Where the first ones had reached up to seize a thing not given, to become like gods by taking, this one opened her empty hands and let herself be given a thing beyond all seizing, let herself become the door the presence had chosen to come through, and the coming could begin, because at last, after all the long ages, a human heart had said the yes that love had been waiting for, and love, having waited so long and so patiently for exactly one freely opened door, went through it.

Now the road, and the town, and the low place, for the yes led to a long walk in the last hard weeks of a woman's carrying, up out of the conquered province along the dusty roads crowded with all the other poor souls the census was driving home to be counted, and the two of them came at last to the little town the prophet had named, the town of the great singer-king's fathers, crowded now past all its room with the census multitude, so that there was no place for them, no bed, no space in any house, the doors all full — and this is the first thing the coming teaches and it teaches it in the plainest way, by simply having no room. The one on whose shoulder the government of the world would rest came into the world unwanted by the world's rooms, turned away not out of cruelty but out of the ordinary crowdedness of a busy and indifferent town that had no idea what was standing at its doors, which is exactly how the largest things always arrive, unrecognized, inconvenient, without a reservation, into a world too full of its own counting to notice. And so the child was born, at the very bottom, in the place where the animals were kept, among the beasts and the straw and the warm animal dark and the smell of the stable, and the young mother wrapped him in bands of cloth the way the poor have always wrapped their children, tight against the cold, and laid him — this is the image, hold it, this is the whole theology of the coming folded into one homely picture — she laid him in the manger, in the feeding-trough, in the low stone box the animals ate their fodder from. The presence at the root of all things, the total continuity of all awareness, the one whose weight the burning ones cried fills the whole earth, came into its own creation and its first bed was the box the beasts fed from. There is no lower place to be laid. That was the point of it, the whole deliberate point of it, that there should be no soul anywhere, in any gutter of any age, however poor, however forgotten, however far down the long crushing had ground it, that could ever say the presence does not know where I am, has never been as low as I am, cannot reach the place I have fallen to — because the presence had begun its coming at the very floor, in the animals' feeding-box, below the beggar and below the slave, so that from that night forward there was no bottom the love had not already been to first.

And here the coming did the thing that undoes the whole fear-telling of it, the thing that shows the heart the story was told from: the sky did not announce it to the palace. The great counting emperor on the seven hills slept that night and knew nothing. The frightened little client-king in his fortress a few miles off, the cruel one, the one who was coming into this story soon and terribly, knew nothing. The priests in the mended temple on the old hill, the ones who kept the ledgers of the holy, knew nothing. The whole apparatus of the powerful and the counting and the sorting slept through the largest arrival in the history of the made world, and the presence, choosing as it always chooses the ones the powerful overlook, sent the news instead to shepherds. To shepherds — and you must understand who shepherds were, in that world, to feel the mercy of the choosing, for they were not the gentle picturesque figures the later tellings made of them; they were rough men, poor men, men who slept in the fields with the animals and smelled of the animals, men whom the respectable counted unclean and untrustworthy precisely because their work kept them out in the wild places with the flocks and away from the temple and its careful washings, men near the bottom of every ledger there was. And it was to them, out in the dark fields, keeping watch over their flock by night, that the glory came — the same glory, the same weight, the same brightness the man had seen high and lifted up in the temple, the same overshadowing light — came down not onto the temple's altar but into a cold field full of poor men and sleeping sheep, and shone round about them, and they were sore afraid, as who would not be, the whole night suddenly full of the light they had been taught only fell on holier ground than theirs. And the word to them was the word to the girl, the word to everyone the presence draws near: fear not. Fear not, for behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people — to all, not to the counted, not to the clean, not to the ones the ledger approved, but to all, and first of all, this night, to you, to shepherds, to the ones the world keeps out in the dark. For unto you is born this day a Saviour. And the sign they were given, the way they would know him, was itself the whole gospel in a token: this shall be a sign unto you; ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling bands, lying in a manger. That was the sign. Not a crown, not a throne, not a guarded palace door — a poor child in a feeding-trough, so that the rough shepherds, the poor men, the ones down at the bottom of the ledger, would come to the low place and find there someone even lower, even poorer, even more defenseless than themselves, and know him for their own, and know that the presence had come down not above them but below them, come to the floor to find the ones the world had shoved to the floor. And of a sudden there was with the messenger a multitude of the whole shining host, the whole choir of the deep, singing over a cold field of poor men the song that is the true anthem of the coming and the exact opposite of every trumpet the empire ever blew: glory in the highest, and on earth peace, good will — peace on the earth, to the earth, into the low and aching earth, and good will toward the whole of it, the announcement not of a conqueror come to sort the world into the crushed and the crushing but of a peace come down into the field where the poor men slept with the animals, sung by the whole host of heaven to an audience of shepherds, because that is where, and to whom, the presence has always most wanted its largest news to go.

And the shepherds went, in haste, and found it as they had been told, the young mother and the workingman and the child in the feeding-box, and they told what they had heard sung over the fields, and the young mother kept all these things and pondered them in her heart, which is the posture of every soul that the coming touches, to keep the thing and turn it over in the quiet of the heart and not yet understand it and not need to, and the shepherds went back to their flocks glorifying and praising, the first in all the earth to know, the poor men, the overlooked men, carrying the largest secret in history back out into the ordinary dark of their ordinary work — which is exactly how the presence wanted its secret carried, not proclaimed from the palace steps but whispered first among the poor and taken back by them into the fields, a seed planted at the very bottom of the world where it could grow up slowly and unwatched, the way seeds do, the way morning does, the way the largest things always come.

But there is a shadow that falls across a birth, and this telling will not sand it away, because the coming came into a real world and the real world has the static loose in it, the old accuser voice, chaos given a tongue, and the static has a favorite house, and its favorite house is a frightened man with power. There were seekers who came, after the shepherds, from far in the east, wise men, star-readers, scholars of the wheeling heavens in the old lands beyond the two rivers where the exiles had once wept, men who had watched the sky the way the priest by the foreign river had watched the wheels within wheels, and had seen a new light rise, a star, and had read in it the birth of a king, and had followed it the long way west across the world to come and honor the child — and this too is part of the width of the coming, that the presence called not only the poor shepherds from the near fields but the far strangers from the outer lands, the foreigners, the ones outside the beloved people altogether, called them by the very heavens they studied, so that from the first night the coming was for all people exactly as the host had sung, the near and the far, the low and the learned, the inside and the outside, all of them drawn to the one low place. But the star-readers, being strangers, and reasonable, and not knowing the country, did the reasonable thing and the nearly fatal thing: they went first to the palace. They went to the frightened little client-king in his fortress and asked, in the innocence of reasonable men, where is he that is born King — and there is the whole danger of the coming in a single question asked at the wrong door, because the man they asked it of was the static's own house, a man who had murdered his way to his small throne and murdered to keep it, who had killed his own wife and his own sons out of the terror that gnaws forever at power that knows itself illegitimate, a man made entirely of the fear that another will take what he has seized — and to such a man the news that a king had been born was not good tidings of great joy, it was the one sound in all the world he could not bear, the sound of the thing he had built his whole crushing life to prevent, and it woke in him the static's oldest and purest scream, which is the scream of the grasping self that would rather destroy the world than share it, that would rather see everything dead than see one thing outside its control.

And so the shadow of the crushing, the same shadow that had ground the poor and burned the temple and scattered the tribes, fell now across the cradle itself, and the frightened man, hiding his terror behind a mask of reverence, told the star-readers to go and find the child and come back and tell him where, so that he too, he lied, might come and worship — meaning to send his soldiers to the low place and end the coming before it could grow. But the star-readers were warned, in a dream, in the quiet inward way the presence has always spoken to the sleeping and the listening, not to go back to the frightened man, and they went home another way, and the workingman too was warned in a dream to rise and take the child and the young mother and flee, that very night, out of the country altogether, down the long refugee road into the great southern land where the beloved people had once been slaves — and so the presence, come into the world as a child, spent its first months exactly as the poorest and most hunted of the earth have always spent their lives, as a refugee, carried in the night across a border by frightened parents fleeing a killer with a crown, sleeping in strange country, one more poor family running from one more powerful man's fear. The one on whose shoulder the government of the world would rest fled that world's power in the dark like a hunted thing, and this too the telling will not soften, because it is the truth of the coming: that love, come into the world, was hunted from its first breath, that the static in its house of frightened power went straight for the cradle, that the world the presence came down to save met it, at the very door, with a sword.

And the sword fell, though it missed the child. For the frightened man, cheated of his star-readers, mad with the terror that would not let him sleep, sent his soldiers to the little town and had them kill — and here the telling must stop and be still, because there is a grief in the coming that no glory can be allowed to drown, and the fear-tellings and the sweet-tellings both hurry past it and they must not — had them kill all the small children of that town, every boy under two years, on the chance of catching the one, and the mothers of that town woke to the soldiers in the dark and the whole low place was filled with a sound that the oldest of the weeping prophets had heard coming down the centuries and written before it came, a voice heard, lamentation and bitter weeping, a mother weeping for her children and refusing to be comforted, because they were not. That happened. Into the very season of the coming, into the same weeks the host sang peace over the fields, the static drove its sword through a town full of the poorest children in the world, and the presence did not stop it, did not send the burning ones down with flaming swords to cut the soldiers apart, did not, and here is the hardest edge of the whole forge and the telling will not pretend it away — the same hard edge that runs under the flood and the exile and the whole permission the presence gives to the made world to be truly free and therefore truly dangerous — let the free and terrible thing happen, let the static's chosen man do the full evil his freedom allowed him, and the children died, and the mothers wept and would not be comforted, and the one child the sword was hunting was already gone over the border in the dark in his mother's arms. And this is not a thing to be explained; it is a thing to be held; and the only thing the telling can say true about it is the thing the whole book says, which is that the presence did not stand off from that grief and watch it from a throne — it was in it, it had come down into the very world where that could happen and made itself one more hunted child in one more fleeing family, had put itself under the sword rather than above it, so that the mothers of that town, and every mother of every murdered child in every age since, weep not to an absent sky but to a presence that was there, in the killing season, as a child hunted by the same power, that knows the border road in the dark and the terror in the arms of a fleeing mother from the inside, and grieves it not as a distant judge but as one who was in it. That is not an answer. The forge does not give answers to the grief of murdered children; there is no answer; there is only the astonishing fact that the presence chose to be in it and not above it, to be hunted and not enthroned, and to carry that grief in its own coming body from the very first weeks of its life in the world.

The killer with the crown died, as such men do, eaten alive at the end by the very body he had spent on his terror, and the family came home from the southern land when the danger was past, and the child grew, in a nothing town in the conquered province, in the workingman's house, in obscurity so complete that the tellings kept almost nothing of it, thirty years of a poor craftsman's life in a poor town, thirty years of the presence learning the smell of sawdust and the ache of a workingman's back and the taste of poor bread and the long ordinary patience of a life that no ledger anywhere was keeping — and this too is the coming, this too is the point, that the largest portion of the presence's life among us was spent doing nothing the world would count, being no one the world would note, growing slowly the way a seed grows in the dark, learning the whole weight of the ordinary human thing from the inside, so that when at last he stood up to speak he would speak not from above the human life but from thirty years down inside it, one of us all the way, a workingman with sawdust in the creases of his hands who knew what the yoke felt like on the shoulder because he had carried the ordinary yokes of the poor for thirty ordinary years. The presence did not skip the ordinary. It did not come down for a week of miracles and go home. It came down and lived the long unremarkable human middle, the years that no one records, the patient forgotten forge of an ordinary life, because that too, that most of all, was the thing it had come to be with us in.

Now turn to the wilderness, because before the one who was coming could begin, the way had to be prepared, and the prophets had said how it would be prepared, and it was prepared exactly as they had said, out in the wild empty places where the presence has always done its truest work. There was a man in the wilderness in those years, out past the mended city and the mended temple and the whole apparatus of the counted and the clean, out in the raw desert by the great river where it runs down into the low dead sea, and he was the fulfillment of the oldest promise of a herald, the voice crying in the wilderness that the weeping-toward-hope prophets had drawn, and he looked the part and lived the part with a wholeness that shamed the soft city, a wild man, clothed in rough camel's hair with a leather belt, eating what the desert gave, locusts and wild honey, owning nothing, wanting nothing the empire could give or take, and therefore free in the one way the empire can never touch, the freedom of a man who has already given up everything it could threaten him with. And out of that freedom he cried the herald's cry, the one the prophet had drawn: prepare ye the way, make his paths straight, every valley filled and every mountain brought low, the crooked made straight and the rough places plain — and the crowds came out to him, out of the cities, down into the wilderness, because there is a hunger in every age that the mended temple and the counting empire cannot feed, the hunger for the real thing, for the true voice, and when a true voice cries at last in the wilderness the hungry will walk a long way into the desert to hear it.

And what he cried to them was the thing the prophets had always cried and the mended religion had always softened: not that they should perform the rituals better, but that they should turn — turn, the whole of it in one word, turn around, change the direction of the heart, stop walking through the wrong door, come back — and be washed, go down into the river and be buried under the water and rise up out of it new, the old life drowned and left in the water and a clean life begun, which is why they named him for the washing, the immerser, the one who baptized. And it was a washing not of the body's dirt but of the heart's direction, a thing done in water because water is the oldest sign in the book, the deep, the flood, the sea the slaves came up out of singing, the going-down-and-rising-up that the whole forge is, death of the old and birth of the new, and the crowds went down into the river and were washed and rose, and the herald was fierce with them, fierce the way the prophets were fierce, tearing at the comfortable ones who came to the water thinking their blood or their temple or their ledger would save them, telling them that the presence could raise up children from the very stones of the riverbed if it wanted them, that the axe was already laid to the root of every tree that bore no fruit, that no inheritance and no ritual and no membership in the counted people would stand in the place of a turned heart and a life that actually loved the neighbor — for they asked him, the crowds, what then shall we do, and his answer was the whole ethic of the book made plain and immediate and impossible to mistake: he that hath two coats, let him give to him that hath none; and he that hath meat, let him do likewise; and to the tax-gatherers, take no more than is appointed you; and to the soldiers, do violence to no man, and be content with your wages. Do not hurt. Do not take more than is yours. Share the coat and the bread. Turn from the crushing and toward the neighbor. That was the way that was being prepared. Not a cleared road for a conqueror's chariot but a turned and softened and made-honest human heart, ready at last to receive a love that would come not to crush the crushers but to end the crushing from the inside — the valleys of the humble lifted up and the mountains of the proud brought low and the whole crooked grasping human landscape made straight and level and ready for the one who was coming.

And the herald knew he was only the herald, and this is the beauty of him and the thing that sets him apart from every false voice that has ever cried in a wilderness, that he knew exactly the size of himself and did not grasp for one inch beyond it. The crowds, hungry, half-wondering, began to wonder if he himself were the one, the awaited one, the course-correction come at last — and there is no test of a soul like that one, the moment when the hungry crowd offers to make you the thing you have spent your life pointing toward, the moment the static leans in and whispers take it, they will give it to you, be the one — and the herald refused it whole. I am not, he said. I baptize you with water, but there cometh one mightier than I, the latchet of whose shoes I am not worthy to stoop down and unloose; he shall baptize you with the breath itself and with fire. He must increase, the herald said, and I must decrease. There is the exact opposite of the grasping in the garden, said by a man at the very height of his power and fame with a whole hungry crowd ready to crown him: not I will take, but I must decrease; not become like a god by seizing, but become smaller so that the true one may be seen; the herald's whole greatness was that he wanted to grow smaller, wanted to point away from himself, wanted the light to fall on another and not on him, and a soul that wants to decrease so that love may increase has understood the whole book, and a soul that wants to increase so that it may be worshipped has understood nothing, however loud it cries in however true a wilderness.

And then, one day, into the crowd at the river, into the line of the turning and the washed, came the one the herald had been pointing to, come up quietly from the nothing town, thirty years old, a workingman with sawdust still in the memory of his hands, no crown, no announcement, no star over him now that the crowd could see, one more poor man from the provinces come down to the water — and he asked to be washed. And this is a thing the tellings have wrestled with forever and this telling will say simply what it means: the one who needed no turning, the one who had never once walked through the wrong door, the one clean of the soil the near presence shows in every other self, went down into the water with the sinners, took his place in the line of the guilty and the turning, asked to be buried under the same water and raised up the same way — not because he needed the washing but because he would not stand above the ones he had come to be with, would not be washed apart and clean while they went down into the muddy river of their turning, and so went down into it with them, into the whole low muddy human thing, the same way he had gone down into the feeding-box and the refugee road and the thirty ordinary years, down, always down, into the place with the people, refusing every height, taking every low place there was. The herald knew him, and drew back, and said thou shouldst baptize me, and would have refused him, and he answered, gently, suffer it to be so now, let it be, this is how the rightness is filled up — let me go down with them, let me stand where they stand, let me be numbered among the turning even though I have nothing to turn from, because that is the coming, that is the whole shape of it, love taking the low place it does not owe, so that no one in the low place is ever alone in it again.

And he went down into the water, and rose up out of it, and in the rising the veil of the ordinary world went thin the way it had gone thin for the man in the temple and the priest by the river, and the deep opened, and the breath came down — the same breath that had moved on the face of the deep before the first word, the same breath that had overshadowed the young woman, the same breath that had raised the dry bones — came down visibly, gently, in the likeness of a dove, the gentlest of the creatures, no eagle of empire, no lion of war, a dove, and rested on him; and there came a voice out of the opened deep, out of the whole continuity of all awareness that is the presence, and the voice said the thing that is the true center of the whole coming, the thing that undoes the entire fear-telling in seven words, the thing every soul in the whole aching world has starved its whole life to hear said to it and been told instead that it must earn: thou art my beloved Son; in thee I am well pleased. Before the miracles. Before the teaching. Before the cross. Before he had done one single thing that the world or the ledger would count as work, at the very beginning, wet from the river, the voice from the opened sky said beloved, said well pleased, said the belovedness first, as a gift and not a wage, as the ground he would stand on and not the summit he must climb to — because that is the order of the whole book, rightly read, that the love comes first and the life flows out of the being-loved and not the other way around, that the presence does not love the ones who have become worthy but calls the ones it loves beloved before they have done anything at all, the way it had called the young woman favoured before she said yes, the way it had breathed on the bones before they stood, the way the father would run down the road to the ruined son before the son had finished his confession — beloved first, always beloved first, and everything true that a self ever does flows out of that first word spoken over it and never, ever, into it as a payment. That is the sound the coming makes at its center. Beloved. And in thee I am well pleased.

But the belovedness is not the end of the story; it is the beginning of the testing; and this is the last and deepest scene of the coming, and it is the whole ancient war of the book gathered into forty days in a wilderness, one man against the static, love against the accuser, the slow road against the fast one, and it must be told as the true and terrible thing it was. For immediately after the water, after the voice, after the belovedness, the same breath that had descended as a dove drove him — drove him, the word is hard and it is the right word — out into the wilderness, away from the crowds and the water and the comfort, out into the raw empty desert alone, to be tested, to fast, to go down into the hunger and the silence and the emptiness where the static lives, because the belovedness had to be proven in the fire before it could be spent for the world, had to be planted in a real self that had faced the whole force of the accuser and chosen, freely, in hunger and weakness and alone, the slow road of love over the fast road of power. Forty days, the old sacred number, the number of the flood and the wandering, the number of the forge, and he ate nothing, and at the end of it he was hungry, and hungry is too thin a word for what forty days is; he was at the very end of himself, weak, emptied, alone in the waste with the animals, exactly where the static has always found its easiest prey, in the exhaustion and the hunger and the aloneness of a self scraped down to its last reserve. And there, at his weakest, the static came, chaos given a voice, the accuser-loop, the old whisper that had hissed in the garden and hissed under every wrong door since, and it came with three temptations, and the three are not three random tricks; they are the whole strategy of the static laid bare, the three faces of the single lie, the lie that says seize it now, you don't have to become.

The first came straight at the hunger, because the static always comes first at the body's true and honest need, and there is the subtlety of it, that the first temptation is not to do an evil but to meet a real need the wrong way. If thou be the Son — and hear the crack it drives in first, if thou be, the accuser's oldest move, to make the beloved doubt the word just spoken over him, to turn beloved into a thing that must be proven rather than a gift already given — if thou be the Son, command that these stones be made bread. Thou art hungry, and thou hast the power, and here are the stones; feed thyself; use the power for thyself; end thy own need now, by thy own hand, on thy own authority. And it is a small thing, on its face, a hungry man making himself bread, what could be wrong with bread — but the whole war is in it, because it is the temptation to make the power serve the self, to come down from the belovedness that receives its bread as gift and become a self that seizes its bread by force, to break, at the very root, the whole posture of the coming, which was to receive and not to seize, to be given and not to take, to trust the presence for the bread the way the freed slaves had learned to trust it for the morning manna, and never once to short-circuit the trust by seizing with power what should be received as gift. And he answered the static with the oldest wisdom of the wilderness, with a word from the very telling of the manna: man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the presence. He would not turn the stones. He would stay hungry, weak, dependent, receiving, rather than seize even so small and honest a thing as bread by the self-serving use of power, because to seize the bread would be to walk through the wrong door on the very first step, to become, in miniature, the grasping self the whole coming had come to undo. He chose the hunger. He chose to keep trusting the presence for the bread rather than become his own provider by force. He chose the slow road, in the smallest and hardest thing, the road of the received gift over the seized possession, and the first face of the lie fell.

The second temptation left the body and went up to the whole aching world, because the static, failing at the small thing, always reaches next for the large and the noble, and this is the most dangerous of the three by far, because it is a temptation not to sin but to do the very good he had come to do, by the fast road instead of the slow one. The static took him up, in the vision, to a high place, and showed him in a moment all the kingdoms of the world and their glory, all of it, the whole crushing empire-ridden world he had come to heal, the field under the yoke, the poor under the boot, the mended temple and the counting emperor and the frightened kings, the whole vast machine of the strong feeding on the weak spread out below — the exact world the prophets had ached over, the exact suffering he had come to end — and the static said all this will I give thee, all of it, all the kingdoms, all the power to set it right, right now, today, without the long slow road, without the cross that lay at the end of the slow road, without the years and the misunderstanding and the death — all this power to fix the whole broken world at a stroke I will give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me. And there is the deepest cut the static has, the offer of the good end by the evil means, the offer of the kingdom without the cross, the whole world set right by seizing the world's own weapon, power, dominion, the throne of the strong man on the horse that the frightened people had always wanted — take the power, use the power, become the greatest of the crushers so that thou mayest crush the crushing, rule the world into rightness from the top by force, and skip the long unbearable slow road of love that has to go all the way down to the bottom and be broken there. It is the temptation that has felled more good souls than any other in the whole history of the world, the temptation to do good by becoming the thing you are fighting, to seize the master's whip in order to free the slaves, to worship the static's own principle, power-over, domination, the fast road of force, for the sake of a beautiful end — and it is always, always, the lie, because a world crushed into rightness is not rightness, and a kingdom seized by worshipping power is the static's kingdom no matter whose hand holds the whip, and the only way the long chain of the crushing is ever actually broken is by the one who refuses the whip altogether, who will not take the power even to do good with it, who chooses the slow road all the way down to the bottom where the mothers weep and there, at the bottom, and only there, plants the thing the whole machine has no answer for. And he knew it, in the hunger, at the height, with the whole suffering world spread out below and the power to fix it all offered into his open hand, and he refused it whole, refused the kingdoms, refused the fast road, refused to save the world by the world's own weapon, and threw the accuser off with the first commandment of the whole covenant: thou shalt worship the presence, and him only shalt thou serve. He would not worship power, not even to end the reign of power. He would take the slow road, the low road, the road that led down and not up, the road with the cross at the end of it and no throne but a beam, because that road, and no other, was the only one that actually went to the bottom of the crushing and broke it, and the second and deepest face of the lie fell.

And the third came at the belovedness itself, straight at it, the most inward of the three, and the static, having failed at the body and failed at the world, reached last for the soul's own trust and tried to turn it into a demand. It took him to the high place of the holy, the pinnacle of the very temple, the height above the crowds, and it turned his own scriptures against him the way the accuser always turns the holy words into a snare, quoting the promise that the presence would give its own charge over him and bear him up in their hands lest he dash his foot — cast thyself down, it said, throw thyself off the height, force the presence to catch thee before all the watching crowd, make it prove its love by a spectacle, put it to the test, demand the sign, and then thou wilt know, then all will know, that thou art truly the beloved. And there is the subtlest venom of all three, because it wears the face of faith and it is the very opposite of faith; it is the demand that love prove itself on command, the refusal to walk the slow trusting road of the beloved who does not need a spectacle because the word has already been spoken, the grasping after certainty by forcing the presence's hand, throwing thyself into danger to make the presence rescue thee and so seize the proof of a love that faith is meant to trust without proof. It is the temptation to turn the beloved's quiet trust into the accuser's loud test, to say if thou lovest me, prove it, catch me, jump — and it is the death of trust, because a love you have forced to prove itself by a spectacle is a love you have stopped trusting, and the whole slow road of the coming was a road of trust, of the received word held in the heart the way the young woman held it, pondered and trusted and not put to the test. And he refused it too, with the last of the three great words, the one that names the whole game: thou shalt not tempt, shalt not test, shalt not put to the trial the presence at the root of thy being. He would not jump. He would not force the proof. He would keep walking the slow road of the beloved who trusts the word already spoken over him and does not demand the sky prove it again on his terms — beloved was enough, spoken once at the river was enough, and he would carry it down the long road into all that was coming without ever once demanding that love prove itself to him on the way. And the third face of the lie fell, and the static, spent, having thrown the whole of its ancient strategy at the one man and been refused at every face of it, left him — for a season, the telling says, only for a season, because the static does not give up, it only withdraws to wait, and the war was not over, it was only, in the wilderness, at the very beginning of him, decisively begun and decisively, in the deep place where all the later battles were already contained, won.

And he came up out of the wilderness, hungry still, weak still, alone still, with nothing seized and nothing proven and no kingdom in his hand and no bread but the trust and no sign but the word spoken over him at the water — and he had never been stronger, because he had faced the whole force of the accuser in the emptiest and weakest place there is and had chosen, freely, in hunger, alone, the slow road of love over every fast road of power the static could paint, had chosen to receive and not to seize, to serve and not to dominate, to trust and not to test, three times over, the whole grasping of the garden undone three times in one wilderness by one hungry man who would not take the fruit however beautifully the static dressed it. The coming was complete. The presence had come down all the way, into the flesh, into the feeding-box, into the refugee road, into the thirty ordinary years, down into the muddy river with the sinners, down into the wilderness with the accuser, down into the hunger and the aloneness and the testing, down to the very bottom of the whole human thing, and had at every step refused the height and chosen the depth, refused the seizing and chosen the receiving, refused the fast road and chosen the slow one — and now, tested and proven and full of the breath that had descended as a dove, he turned at last toward the crowds and the towns and the whole aching world the prophets had wept over, to begin the work he had come the long slow road to do, the course-correction, love walking into the world on foot, with no army and no throne and no crown but the word beloved spoken over it at the water, to set the whole broken story right the only way it could ever actually be set right, from the bottom, by love that will be hurt and will not hurt back, coming, as the prophets had known he would, and coming, now, at last, out of the wilderness and into the world, to stay.
