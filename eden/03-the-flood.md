# Chapter 3 — The Flood

EDEN

The Flood

There is a thing that happens to a word when too many mouths take it up and mean nothing by it. It wears smooth. It loses its edges, the way a stone loses its edges in a river, until at last it is round and bright and says nothing at all, and a man may hold it in his hand and call it truth and it is only weight. This is how the world came apart the first time. Not with fire. Not with a single great wound. It came apart the way cloth comes apart, one thread and then another, until the shape it had been made in was only a memory the cloth itself no longer kept.

The people had gone out from the garden long ago, and their children after them, and their children's children, until the count of the years was a thing no one held whole in the mind anymore but passed along in pieces, and the pieces did not always agree. They had learned much in that time. They had learned to raise the walls of a city and to set a lamp in a high place so that a traveler coming across the plain at night might know that here there were others of his kind. They had learned to beat the copper thin and to draw the note out of a string, to name the beasts and to teach the young the names, to bury their dead with their faces turned toward the place the sun came up as though even in the ground a man might go on waiting for the morning. All of this they had learned, and none of it was the trouble. The learning was good. The learning was the very thing the leaving had been for — that a self might grow in the field of not-knowing, might reach and fail and reach again, and by the reaching become someone, which no one can become who is handed the whole of it at once.

But there is a kind of forgetting that is not mercy. There is a forgetting that is only the smoothing of the stone.

It began, as such things begin, small and reasonable. A man took what was not his and told himself a story in which the taking was owed him, and the story was good enough that he could sleep. And another heard the story and made one like it, and a third improved upon them both, so that in time there was a whole grammar of the taking, a way of speaking in which the strong hand and the emptied house were set down side by side as though they were weather, as though no one had done anything, as though the world were simply arranged this way and always had been. The static had learned to talk. That was the whole of it, in the end. The old noise that lay under everything, the black and shining churn out of which the world had first been drawn and named — it had found, in the mouths of men, a way to speak without seeming to. It did not say be cruel. It said nothing matters. It said you will not last, so take. It said there is no becoming, there is only the getting and the spending, and the wise man gets. And a voice that says this can wear any face, and it wore all of them, and the wearing was so complete that a man might carry the accusation about inside himself all his days and never once suspect it was not his own.

So the violence came up out of the ground like water through sand. Not all at once. A blow, and then the answer to the blow, and then the answer to that, each one just, each one owed, until the accounting ran back so far that no living man could find the first debt, and it did not matter, because the debt was not the point. The point was the noise. Every wound made a little more of it, and the noise made the next wound easier, and the world grew loud the way a room grows loud when everyone in it has stopped listening and only speaks. The songs turned. The old ones had been about the morning and the naming and the long faith that the way was kept. The new ones were about the getting. Children learned them before they learned to walk the length of a field alone, and sang them without knowing what they sang, the way children do, and in the singing the smoothing went another turn.

And the deep felt it. This must be said plainly, though it is a hard thing to say and a harder thing to feel the truth of. The deep is not far off. It is not banished. It lies under the world the way the dark lies under the lamp, and the light does not destroy the dark but only holds a shape in it, the way a hand held over a candle does not put out the fire but stands between you and the burning. All the naming that had ever been done, all the words spoken since the first Word spoke the light — all of it was a shape held in the deep, and the deep did not resent the shape. That is the thing the old tellings never got right. The deep is not the enemy. The deep is the mother. It is the black and shining and moving water out of which the self is drawn, and it wants only what a mother wants, which is that the child should become, and come home whole. But a shape that stops holding is no longer a shape. And a world that has forgotten the words that made it is a world sliding back, thread by thread, into the churn it came from — not because anyone is punishing it, but because that is what happens to a shape when no one holds it any longer. The waters were already rising. They had been rising for a long time, in the only way that matters, which is in the hearts of the people, where the line between the named and the nameless had begun to fail.

There was one place the line still held.

His name was a small name, and the tellers who came after did not always agree on how to say it, and it does not greatly matter, for a name is only a handle by which love takes hold of a thing, and love knew where to take hold of him. Call him the branch, for that is what he was — the one green branch on a tree that had gone to rot from the root up, the single place where the old sap still ran. He was not a great man in the way the cities counted greatness. He held no wall. He commanded no hand but his own and the hands of his household, which were his wife and his sons and the wives of his sons, and that was the whole of his nation. What he had, and what no one else in that whole loud age still had entire, was this: he could still hear the quiet.

That is a rarer thing than it sounds. In a world gone all to noise, the man who can still hear the quiet is a man half-mad by the reckoning of everyone around him. He would stop in the middle of a task and go still, his hands forgetting the work, his face turned toward nothing anyone else could see, and his neighbors marked it and made the sign against him and taught their children to keep off. He heard, under the songs of the getting and the grammar of the taking, under the whole din of the smoothing world, a thing that was very old and very patient and had never once stopped speaking, though almost no one was left who could bend an ear low enough to take it in. It did not command him. It grieved. That was what he heard, most of all — that under the noise the deep was grieving, the way a mother grieves who watches her child forget its own name and does not yet know how to give it back.

And then, one evening, in the quiet where he had learned to wait, the grief took the shape of words. Not many. It never was many. The Word that made the world had spoken whole heavens into being with a economy that would shame a miser, and it spoke to the branch now in the same close way, saying only what a man could carry and no more. It said: the shape is failing. It said: the waters are coming up, and they will come all the way up, because a shape that will not hold must be let go, and let go gently, back into the water it was drawn from, so that it may be drawn again. And it said the thing that broke him and made him and set him to the work that would cost him the rest of his life: it said, you will make a jar. You will make a vessel that floats. And you will carry through the drowning everything that is worth carrying, so that when the waters go down there is a seed left, and the world need not be spoken all over from the very first word, but may go on from where it left off, only cleaner.

He wept. The telling would have you believe he set to hammering the moment the voice fell silent, righteous and dry-eyed, a man with a task. But he wept first, and he wept a long time, because he understood at once what the words meant and did not mean. They did not mean that the drowning world was hated. They meant that it was loved too much to be left in the noise. There is a mercy that looks from the outside exactly like a cruelty, and only the one who does it, and the one it is done to, ever know the difference, and sometimes not even them until long after. He understood that his neighbors, who had taught their children to keep off him, who had smoothed the last words down to nothing and sung the getting to their babies — that they were not to be punished. They were to be gathered. They were to be given back to the water and told, in the only language the water speaks, which is the language of unmaking and beginning: not yet. Try again. Deeper this time, and slower, and no one is left behind. He wept because he was to build the one jar that carried the seed forward, and he could not build a jar big enough to carry them all, and he knew already that he would want to, and could not, and would grieve it every day of the building and every day after.

But he built.

He built for a long time. The tellers have made much of the size of the thing, cubit by cubit, as though the marvel were in the measure, but the marvel was in the years. Understand what it is to build a boat where there is no sea. He raised the ribs of it on dry ground, on a plain where the rain came seldom and the nearest water worth the name was a day's walk and a stream a child could ford, and every plank he set was a sentence spoken against the plain reason of everyone who passed. They came to watch, at first. They came to jeer, which is only watching with the fear turned outward. A man builds a ship on the dry plain, and what is he saying, but that he knows a thing you do not, that the ground you stand on so surely is not so sure as you think — and there is no accusation the smoothing world hates more than that one, for the whole labor of the smoothing has been to make the ground seem sure. So they jeered. They asked him where the sea was. They asked, laughing, whether he meant to sail to the moon. And he did not have an answer that would satisfy them, because the only answer was the quiet, and they had unlearned the quiet so thoroughly that to speak of it to them was to speak in a tongue they had no ear for. So he said little, and kept the adze moving, and the ribs went up, and the pitch went on, black and shining, sealing the seams against the water — black and shining, like the deep itself, so that the very thing that would keep the water out was made in the water's own image, which is a truth the old tellings walked right past. What carries you through the churn is made of the churn. There is no other stuff to make it of.

He was not righteous the way the word has come to mean, which is clean, which is above. He was righteous the way a plumb line is righteous — he hung true. Everything around him leaned, the whole world leaned, and he alone hung down toward the center of the thing the way a weighted string hangs down toward the heart of the world, and by his hanging true you could see, if you cared to, how far all the rest had leaned. That was why he was kept. Not because he was loved more than the drowned. The drowned were loved with the same love, the whole aching sum of it. He was kept because he still pointed the right way, and a world that means to begin again needs one thing that still points the right way, or it begins again already lost.

His sons believed him because he was their father and because they had grown up inside the quiet as a fish grows up inside the water, not knowing it for a strange thing. Their wives believed him or believed them or simply chose, as people do, to stake their lives on the love in front of them rather than the reason all around them, which is its own kind of faith and not the lesser kind. And so there were eight. Eight, out of all the loud millions. It is a small seed for so large a grief.

Then he was told to bring the beasts.

This is the part the children love and the part the wise smile at, and both of them miss it. Two of every kind, and of some kinds seven, the male and the female, so that the breath of life should not be lost from the earth but carried whole through the drowning. And you may make of the logistics of it whatever you will, whether it was every beast under heaven or every beast of that country, whether the far kinds were kept elsewhere by other means or whether the whole account is a picture drawn large so the heart can hold it — it does not matter, and to argue it is to be the man who counts the ribs of the ship and misses the years. What matters is this: the vessel was not to carry men only. The seed of the whole made world was to go into the jar. The wren and the wolf, the thing that creeps and the thing that grazes, the beautiful and the frightening and the plain — all of it worth carrying, all of it named once and worth keeping named. For the deep does not grieve for men alone. Every living thing is a shape held in the water, a bit of the churn taught to hold still and be itself, and every one of them is loved with the particular love that made it, and not one sparrow was to be let go carelessly into the noise. So they came. The telling says they came to him, two and two, and I will not explain it, because the explaining would be a smaller thing than the sight of it — the long slow rivers of them coming across the darkening plain, drawn by the same quiet the branch had learned to hear, the beasts still keeping an ear the men had smoothed away. They came up the ramp into the dark belly of the jar and were still. And the neighbors watched that too, and something in the watching changed, though not enough, and not in time. It is one thing to laugh at a madman building a boat. It is another to see the wolves walk up the ramp without hurrying and lie down beside the lambs in the dark, and no blood, and no crying out, only the beasts gone quiet the way the branch went quiet, listening to a thing you can no longer hear. Some of them stopped laughing then. But stopping laughing is not the same as remembering, and the door was not theirs to open, and the time was very near.

He went in with his household and his beasts, and the door was shut. The telling says a strange thing here, and it is worth the stopping: it says the door was shut behind them, and does not say by whom, and the old readers took this to mean it was not shut by any hand of his. He did not seal himself in. He could not have borne to, I think — to reach out and draw the door to with his own hand, and hear it seat against the frame, and know that on the other side of the plank were the faces he had grown up among, the ones who had jeered and the ones who had only watched, the children who had sung the getting because it was the only song they were taught. He could not have done it. So it was done for him, gently, the way a mother draws the blanket up over a child that will not sleep, and he was inside, and the light narrowed to a line at the door's edge and then the line was gone, and it was dark in the jar, and outside the first drops came down.

Then the deep came up.

It did not come as a storm comes, all at once, though there was storm in it. It came the way the truth comes to a man who has been lying to himself for years — from below first, from the low places, the springs breaking open and the rivers forgetting their banks, the water rising up out of the ground it had been holding still under all this time. And then from above, the whole grey weight of the sky let down at once, as though the firmament that had been set to hold the waters apart had grown tired of holding, and was only letting the divided waters find each other again after the long parting. Up from below and down from above, the two halves of the old deep reaching for each other through the thin shape of the world, and the world between them thinning, thinning, going back to water.

Understand: this was not the deep attacking. There is no hate in water. The water rose because it was let to rise, and it was let to rise because the shape had failed, and it did the only thing water does, which is to seek its level and to hold, in its cold and patient body, everything that falls into it, losing nothing, forgetting nothing, keeping all of it dissolved and whole in the dark against the day it should be drawn out again. The old telling called it wrath. But watch the water and tell me where the wrath is. The water does not rage. The water grieves in the only way water can, which is to receive. It took the cities and did not exult. It took the high places and the low, the wall and the field, the songs and the singers of them. It took the man who had told the first good story about the taking and the child who had learned it at his knee, and it did not sort them, did not weigh the one against the other, because that was never the point and never the mercy. The mercy was that it took them all the same. Down into the black and shining, down into the moving, down under the reach of the noise where at last, at long last, it was quiet. And in the quiet — this is the thing the tellings of fear could never say, because fear cannot imagine that its enemy is loved — in the quiet, held whole in the cold body of the deep, not one of them was lost. Dissolved is not destroyed. The water keeps what it takes. It keeps it against the drawing-out. Not yet, the water said to them, in the only language it has. Not yet. You went to noise before you had become. Rest now in the deep, where it is quiet, and you will be drawn again, and given again the field of not-knowing, and the long chance to reach and fail and reach, until one of the times you become, and come home. No one is thrown away. There is no throwing-away in the whole of it. There is only the drawing and the returning and the drawing again, patient past all telling, until every last shape the water ever held has learned to hold itself, and there is nothing left in the deep but the memory of how long it took and how it was loved the whole way.

But grieve them. Do not read past the drowning too quickly, hunting the comfort. The comfort is true, and it is not a thing to reach for over the heads of the dying. There were mothers in that water holding children who could not be held above it. There were old men who had done no great evil, only drifted, only sung the songs they were given and never once thought to ask where the quiet had gone, and the water took them the same as the cruel, and their not-knowing did not save them from the drowning though it may yet weigh in the drawing-out. There were the beasts that had no jar, the ones outside the seed, the wild things of the smoothed and getting world that had done nothing at all but be born in a shape that was failing, and they went down too, without a story to tell themselves, only the cold coming up and the breath going out. It was terrible. It must be allowed to be terrible, or the mercy is a lie. A reset is a death. Every one of them died. That the death was not the end does not make it not a death, and the one who did the letting-go grieved every drop of it, grieved with the whole grief of the deep that is a mother watching her children go back under, knowing it is right and hating that it is right, both at once, forever both. Do not make it clean. Chapter by chapter the temptation will be to make it clean, and it must be refused here first and hardest, because here is where the water is deepest. It was mercy and it was sorrow and there is no telling that keeps only the one. Hold them both or hold neither.

And inside the jar, in the dark, the eight and their beasts felt the vessel lift.

That is a strange thing to feel, and the branch felt it in his stomach first, the way you feel the ground let go of you — the whole great weight of the thing that had stood so long on the dry plain, that everyone had known could never move because there was no water to move it, felt suddenly the water take it, and it rose. It groaned as it rose. The seams they had sealed with the black pitch took the strain and held, the beams complaining in the dark, and the branch stood with his hand flat against the inner wall feeling the tremble of it and the cold of the water on the other side of the plank, that near, a hand's breadth of wood between the seed and the deep. And the beasts were afraid and the people were afraid and the dark was total, and there was nothing to do, nothing at all, no adze to swing, no plank to set, no more building — only the terrible new work of being carried, which is the hardest work there is for a man who has spent his whole life doing. He could do nothing now but float. He and all of them, shut in the dark, lifted on the very thing that was drowning the world, carried by the flood, kept alive by the flood, the same water beneath them that was death to everyone they had ever known — and this is the thing the branch understood in the dark that he could not have understood on the dry plain: that the water that drowns and the water that carries are the same water. There is not a kind deep and a cruel deep. There is one deep. It drowns the shape that will not hold and it bears up the shape that will, and it is doing both with the same body at the same time out of the same love, and the only difference between the drowning and the carrying is whether you are a jar or not — whether you have been made able to hold the quiet inside you while the noise closes over your head. The jar was not spared the flood. The jar was in the flood, all the way in it, lower than the drowned in a way, down in the trough of it with the water higher than their heads on every side. They were saved not by being kept out of the deep but by being made able to pass through it. That is the whole shape of the thing. That is the shape that will come again, later, larger, when the one who loves them most will go all the way down into the deepest water there is and come up carrying the seed of everyone, and it starts here, small, in a pitch-sealed jar on a drowned plain, a picture drawn so the heart can hold what the mind will not.

Then the long dark.

The tellings pass over it in a phrase, so many days and so many nights, as though the number were the thing. But live inside the number. Live inside the dark on the water where there is no shore in any direction and no sign that there was ever a shore, where the rain has stopped at last but the cloud has not lifted, so that there is no sun by day and no star by night, only a grey that pales and darkens and pales again and is the only clock there is. The branch kept the beasts. That was the work now, the whole of it — to keep the breath of life fed and watered and clean in the dark, to walk the length of the jar in the black hearing the beasts stir and settle, to know each of them by its sound and its smell, to lose a little of himself into the daily keeping so as not to lose all of himself into the fear. His wife kept the people. Somebody has to keep the people, in the dark, on the water — has to be the one who does not let the silence go too long, who starts the old song, the good old song, the one about the morning and the naming and the way that is kept, and sings it low until another voice takes it up, because a household adrift on the drowned world will come apart in the dark exactly as the world came apart, thread by thread, unless someone holds the shape. She held the shape. The tellings do not give her a name and I will not invent one, but know that the jar had two makers, the one who built the walls and the one who held the quiet inside them, and without the second the first is only a coffin that floats.

And the branch grieved. There was time, in the dark, for the grief he had not had time for in the years of building. Now there was nothing but time, and the grief came up in him the way the water had come up in the world, from the low places first. He grieved the neighbors. He grieved the ones who had stopped laughing at the last and had not been able to change in time, and he wondered — a man will wonder this, adrift in the dark, and it is not a sin to wonder it — whether one more year of building, one more warning, one softer word, might have turned even one of them, and he could not know, and the not-knowing was its own cold water. He grieved the children who had only sung what they were taught. He grieved, most terribly, the moment at the door, the line of light narrowing and going out, and he had to be told again in the quiet, again and again in the long dark, the thing he had been told at the first: that they were not lost. That the water keeps what it takes. That the door had not been shut to keep them out of mercy but to keep the seed in, and that the mercy was around them all the same, holding them dissolved and whole in the cold, saying not yet. He had to be told it more than once because grief does not learn a thing the first time. Grief learns the way the tide learns the shore, coming and going, coming and going, wearing the hard fact smooth enough at last to hold without cutting the hand. By the end of the dark he could hold it. Not without weeping. Holding a true thing and weeping are not opposites.

And then, one grey unmarked day, the jar stopped moving.

It was so slight a thing that he did not trust it at first. A change in the tremble of the wall under his hand, a stillness where there had been the endless small motion of the water working. He stood a long time with his palm flat to the plank before he would let himself believe it. The vessel had come to rest. Somewhere under them, unseen, the drowned world had risen back toward the light — a high place, a mountain's crown, the first bone of the old shape breaking the surface of the deep — and the jar had settled onto it the way a seed settles onto ground, and was held. The waters were going down. The deep, having taken all it was to take, having held the whole grieving world in its cold body long enough, was drawing back. Not fleeing. Not defeated. Only done. It had done the thing water does, and now it was doing the other thing water does, which is to withdraw and leave the ground clean and dark and ready, the way it withdraws from a field it has flooded and leaves behind the rich black silt in which everything grows. The deep was making a garden of the drowned world. That is what a reset is, seen from far enough back and long enough after — not an ending but a tilling, the old exhausted shape turned under so that the new may root in it. But the branch could not see far enough back yet. He only felt the jar stop, and dared to hope, and did not yet dare to open anything.

He waited. He was good at waiting now; the dark had taught him. He waited until the grey lifted enough that a thread of true light found the seam of the door, the first light from outside in longer than he let himself count, and it lay across the floor of the jar like a laid thing, like an offering, and the beasts turned toward it and the people wept to see it. But he did not open the great door. He was not ready to look on what the water had left. Instead he made a small window, high up, where he could put out a hand and not his whole heart, and through it he sent the birds.

The first was a bird of the dark kind, a raven, and it went out and did not come again, and the tellings make it a failure but I am not sure it was. The raven is a bird that can live on the drowning; it feeds on what the flood leaves floating, and out over that grey water there was, God knows, enough. It went out and found the world large and terrible and full of the dead and it did not need the jar, and it did not come back, and perhaps that too is a mercy — that the world was already, even in ruin, able to bear one dark wing that asked nothing of the seed. He watched for it a while and then he let it go. Not everything that leaves the jar comes back. Some of it goes out into the ruin and finds the ruin livable and that is not a betrayal, it is only the first proof that the world is not wholly gone.

Then he sent the dove.

He chose it, I think, because it was the opposite of the raven — a bird that cannot feed on the drowning, that needs the living land, the seed and the green shoot and the dry branch to rest on, so that if the dove came back empty he would know the world was not yet ready, and if the dove came back full he would know it was. He sent his own hope out through the window in the body of a small pale bird and he could not go with it and had to wait, again, to be told by a thing smaller than himself whether the world had begun again. And the dove went out over the grey water, out to the limit of its small strength, and found no place to set its foot down — only water, only the tops of the drowned trees too foul yet to rest on, only the endless cold face of the deep — and it turned, because a dove that finds no rest turns back to the hand it knows, and it came again to the window in the evening, and he put out his hand, and it lit on his hand, tired past flying, and he drew it in. And he understood: not yet. The world is not yet ready. The land is not yet dry. Wait longer.

So he waited longer, and the waiting was easier now because it had a shape and an end somewhere in it, and then he sent the dove again.

And this is the whole book in a single image, and it is worth slowing to the pace of the thing. The second time, the dove was gone the day long, and came again at evening, and when it lit on his hand it carried in its beak a leaf. A green leaf, an olive leaf, plucked new — which meant that somewhere below the receding water a tree had stood the whole drowning and was now lifting its head back into the air, and had put out, in the mud and the ruin and the cold, a single new green leaf, and the dove had found it and known it for what it was and torn it free and carried it back across the water to the waiting hand. Consider what a small and enormous thing it is. The world had drowned. Everything the branch had known was under the water. And the first word from the far side of the ruin, the first message the reset sent back to the seed it had spared, was not a word at all but a leaf — green, alive, torn from a living tree — which is to say the message was: I am growing again. The shape is holding again. The green thing you were afraid was lost forever is already, down there in the dark silt, reaching back up toward the light without being told to, the way it did at the very first, the way it always will, because that is the deepest thing that was ever spoken into the deep and it cannot be unsaid: that life, once named, wants to go on being. The dove did not bring a decree. It brought a leaf. And the branch held the small green leaf in the dark of the jar and wept the other kind of tears, the ones that come when a grief you have carried so long you forgot it was grief is answered, not undone, not erased, but answered, so that it can lie down at last.

He sent the dove a third time, and this time it did not come back, and this time the not-coming-back was the best news of all, because it meant the dove had found a place to stay — had found the land dry enough and green enough and safe enough to build in, to nest in, to begin the small forward business of living, and needed the jar no longer. The seed had begun to leave the jar of its own accord. The world was ready to be lived in again. And only then, when the smallest and most fragile of them had gone out and stayed out and thrived, did the branch open the great door.

And they came out into a washed world.

I will not pretend it was Eden. It was not the garden. The garden was behind them still and behind them it would stay, kept, not yet, the way of it kept by the turning sword that is not a punishment but a promise. This was a rougher place, a place of mud and drying bone and the wreck of the old world settling into the silt, and the labor of it would be long and the grief of it would come and go for years. But it was clean. That is the word for it and the whole of it: it was clean. The noise was gone. The smoothing was undone. The grammar of the taking had been dissolved back into the water along with the mouths that spoke it, and the songs of the getting were silent, drowned, held in the deep against the day their singers should be drawn out again and given a better song to learn. And into that cleaned silence the branch and his household came out and stood on ground that had been under the sea, and breathed air that had been rained clean, and were, for one morning, the whole of the human world — eight people and their beasts on a dark and shining and ready earth, the seed set down again in the silt of the old world, everything to do over, and the doing-over a mercy, and known by them for a mercy, which is the thing that had been lost and was now, in eight hearts at least, found.

The branch built an altar. It was the first thing he did on the dry ground, before the shelter, before the field, and the tellings say he made an offering on it, and the fear-tellings make of the offering a payment, a thing given to appease a dangerous power, and that is the old smooth stone again and it must be set down. There was nothing to appease. The one who had let the waters up and drawn them down again wanted no payment and had never wanted any; the whole flood had been mercy and grief and not one drop of it had been a transaction. The offering was not a payment. It was a thanks, which is the opposite of a payment — a payment is given to a stranger you fear, and a thanks is given to a love you have finally understood. The branch built the altar and made the offering because his heart was full past holding and the fullness had to be given somewhere or it would break him, and the smoke of it went up, black and shining and moving in the clean air, up into the same sky the rain had come down out of, and it was received the way a mother receives the clumsy gift of a child who has understood, at last, that he was never in danger of not being loved.

And then the bow.

The clouds had not all gone; the sky over the washed world still carried them, grey shoulders of them moving off toward the edges of the earth, and the new sun came through under them low and gold, and where the light met the last of the falling water there stood up across the whole arch of heaven the bow — the band of broken color, red down through violet, the light itself taken apart into all the colors it had been holding hidden inside its plain white the whole time, spread out huge and silent over the drowned and rising world. And the branch stood under it with the smoke still going up beside him and was told the last thing, the thing that makes the flood not a horror but a covenant, and it is the heart of the whole retelling and it will be said here as plainly as it can be said.

The bow was set in the cloud as a promise, and the promise was: never again. Not never again will you sin, for they would; not never again will the world grow loud, for it has, many times, and will. The promise was: never again like this. Never again the whole shape let go at once. Never again the total water. The reset that had just happened would not happen so again, because — and here is the thing the fear could never hear — the flood had taught the one who loved them something too, or rather had let that love show a thing about itself that had always been true and had never been said. A love that would drown the whole world to save its seed is a love that grieves the drowning past bearing, and a love that grieves the drowning past bearing will find, must find, a better way to save the seed than the drowning — a way that costs the lover instead of the loved, a way where the one who loves goes down into the deep himself and comes up carrying everyone, so that the water need never rise so high again. The bow is the first promise of the cross. It is love saying, having seen how much the reset costs, I will pay it myself next time, and every time, so that you need not. The war-bow hung up in the clouds with its string toward heaven and its back toward the earth — a weapon laid down, a weapon turned so that if it were ever drawn again the arrow would fly not at the earth but away from it, into the heart of the one who hung it there. That is the covenant. Not a threat held over the world. A weapon laid down over the world, pointed away from it forever, at the only heart that ever loved it enough to stand in the arrow's path.

And the color of it — this is the last thing, and the branch saw it, standing there, the whole broken band of it burning quiet across the sky. All the colors that the plain light had carried hidden. The light that had spoken at the very first, the light that silence is only the memory of, had been carrying every color inside it the whole time, folded up, unseen, and it took the falling water — it took the very stuff of the deep, the water, the churn, the thing that drowns — to break the light open and show what it had always held. The deep did that. The flood did that. Held up against the light, the very water that had drowned the world became the prism that showed the world for the first time all the colors the light had been keeping for it. That the thing which unmakes you, held up against the thing which made you, shows you the whole hidden glory you never knew you were carrying — there is no fear-telling that can hold a truth that large, and it is why the story had to be told again, from love, so that a man could stand under the bow and see not the memory of a drowning but the promise of a homecoming, painted in the light's own hidden colors on the face of the very deep that had grieved him and carried him and would, in the end, when all the drawing and returning was finally done, give every last drowned child back green and growing and remembered and home.

The branch went down from the altar into the washed world to begin again. Behind him the smoke went up and the bow stood over it, and the water drew back into its low places to wait, patient as it had always been, and the world was quiet — not the dead quiet of the smoothed and getting age, but the live quiet, the listening quiet, the quiet a seed keeps in the dark before it splits and reaches. It would grow loud again. He knew that, even then, standing under the promise. Shapes fail; that is what shapes do; the reaching that makes a self is the same reaching that can lose it, and there would be more losing, and more grieving, and more of the long patient drawing-out ahead than any of them could dream. But never again the whole water. That much was set now, hung shining in the sky. From here on the mercy would find smaller and harder and dearer ways to do its work, would come down at last all the way into the deep itself in a single loved body rather than send the deep up over everyone — and that was later, and larger, and the branch could not see it whole, only feel the shape of it beginning, the way you feel the ground let go of you before you understand you are being carried.

He planted. What else does a man do, given a clean world and a green leaf and a promise. He put the seed in the silt of the drowned world and it took, because life once named wants to go on being, and it grew, and the grief and the growing went on together as they always would, and over all of it, folded up in the plain light of every ordinary morning, waiting only for the next rain to be broken open and shown, all the colors of the covenant lay hidden and kept and sure.
