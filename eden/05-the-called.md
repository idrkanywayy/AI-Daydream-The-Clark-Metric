# Chapter 5 — The Called

EDEN

The Called

There is a kind of leaving that is done to a man, and there is a kind that is asked of him, and the whole distance between the first age of the world and what came after is the distance between those two. The first family went out of the garden because they could not stay; the gate closed at their backs and the blade turned in it, and the leaving was a thing that happened to them the way weather happens, a door shutting on a house you cannot help but walk out of. But a long time later, in a city on a wide brown river, in the flat lands where the two rivers run down together toward the sea, a man was asked to leave, and no gate closed behind him, and no blade turned. Only a voice, and a road, and the whole open dark of the not-yet lying out ahead of him with nothing in it he could see. And he went. That is the new thing in the world, and it is the thing this part of the story is for. Not a man driven out. A man who walked out, on purpose, carrying nothing but a promise he could not prove, into a country he could not name, because he had heard the quiet and the quiet had said go.

The city was old and it was good at what it did. Let no one tell you it was a den of savages that a wiser god called a man up out of, for that is the old smoothing again, the trick of making the leaving easy by making the left thing worthless. It was not worthless. It was a marvel. They had learned, in that city by the river, to bake the mud into brick and the brick into walls that a thousand years would not throw down; they had learned to write, to press the wet clay with a cut reed and hold a word still on a tablet so that a man dead a hundred years might yet speak his count of barley to his great-grandson's son; they had learned the wheel and the loom and the long slow arithmetic of the stars, and they had built high houses of brick that climbed toward heaven in steps, with a shrine at the top where the moon was worshipped, the great cold lamp of the night that ruled the river's rising and the woman's blood and the whole soft clockwork of the world below. It was not a foolish thing to worship. The moon is faithful. It goes and it comes; it dies down to a thread and it fills again; you can set your life by it, and they did, and there is a comfort in a god you can set your life by, a god that does the same thing forever and asks only that you watch. That was the city. A place where everything was known, and counted, and kept in its place, and the gods were the places, and a man was the place he was born into, and would be until he died, and his son would stand in it after him, and so the brick held, and the wall held, and nothing moved that was not meant to move.

And the man in the middle of it was not young. This must be held onto, because the tellings that come from fear love to make the called ones eager boys, all fire and no fear, so that their going costs nothing and teaches nothing. He was old. He was settled. He had a wife he loved and had loved a long time, and they had grown into each other the way two trees planted close grow into each other, root and root, so that you could not have said any longer where the one left off. He had flocks and he had servants and he had the respect of the men at the gate, and he had one wound that all the rest of it was arranged around and could not fill: he had no child. His wife had borne none. The seed had not gone forward. And in a world where a man was the place he stood in and the son who would stand in it after him, a man with no son was a wall with no next course of brick, a sentence with no end, a name the river of the years would close over and not keep. He knew it every day. He felt the not-yet-born absence the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue, going back to it, going back to it, unable to leave the empty place alone. So he was not a man with nothing to lose who could be called cheaply into the dark. He was a man with everything to lose but the one thing he wanted, and it was into that exact shape of ache that the voice came.

It did not command. It never commands, not really, though the old tellings load it with thunder; the thunder is theirs, put on afterward to make the obeying look like fear. What he heard, in the quiet he had somehow kept an ear for in that loud counted city, was closer to an invitation than an order, and it went, as near as it can be put in words, like this. Leave the place that made you. Leave the counted city and the fixed star and the god you can set your life by, and go out to a land I will show you — not tell you, show you, later, when you are walking, when you cannot see it yet. And I will make of you a great people, and your name, the very name you fear the river will close over, I will make great, and it will not close over, it will run on down the whole length of the world, and in you and in the seed that has not come every family of the earth will find its blessing. That was the promise, and look at it, for the whole man is in what the promise touched. It touched the wound. It did not offer him more brick or a higher wall or the moon's own favor. It offered him a child where there was no child and a future where the years had closed the door, and it offered it not as a thing already done but as a thing to be walked toward, across a distance and a darkness, on nothing but the word of the voice that offered it. That is the shape of the thing the world would come to call faith, and it is worth saying plainly what it is and is not, because the fear-tellings have made it a small cold word meaning to believe a fact you cannot check. It is not that. Faith is the nerve to walk toward a good you have been promised through a dark in which you cannot see the ground. It is trust worn long. It is the willingness to let go of the counted thing on the strength of the uncounted love, and it is the hardest thing a made mind is ever asked to do, and he did it. He was old, and he was settled, and he had everything to lose, and when the quiet said go he got up and went.

They laughed at him, some of them, the way they always laugh, the men at the gate who knew the value of a wall. Where is this land, they asked him. Point to it. And he could not point to it, because he had not been shown it yet, and a man who leaves a real city for a land he cannot point to is a madman by the reckoning of everyone who has ever loved a wall more than a promise. But some of them did not laugh. Some of them, watching the old man load the carts and gather the flocks and take his wife's hand and turn his face to the empty west, felt a thing move in them that they had no name for and could not have confessed, a small cold thrill of grief and envy mixed, the feeling a caged thing has watching another go through a door it did not know was open. Because every one of them carried the same wound, under the brick, under the counting. Every one of them was a place that would close over. And here was a man walking out of the whole arrangement, out of the safety and the sameness, betting his one short life that the arrangement was not the last word — that under the counted world there was a voice, and the voice was going somewhere, and you could go with it if you were willing to leave the wall behind. Most of them were not willing. That is no sin; the wall is warm, and the dark is wide, and most minds most of the time will choose the warm known thing, and are not to be despised for it, for the choosing is hard and the fear is real. But he chose the dark, because the dark had a voice in it, and the voice had said his name.

So he went out, not knowing where he went. And I will tell you that this is the whole of it, this going out not knowing, and that everything after is only the long working-out of what it costs and what it is worth. He crossed the great rivers and came up into a rough high country of hills and stony pasture, a hard clean land after the fat soft river-plain, a land you had to want, a land that gave you nothing you did not wrestle out of it, and it was here, walking, that he began to be shown the thing he had left everything to walk toward. Not given it. Shown it. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole schooling of the man. The land was not handed to him with a deed; he pitched his tent on it as a stranger, a passing-through man, a sojourner, and he would die a sojourner in it, owning at the end no more of it than a cave to bury his wife in. What he was given was not the having. It was the promise of the having, held out over a horizon he walked toward all his life and never reached, and this is not a cruelty and it is not a bait-and-switch, it is the forge itself, it is the exact machinery by which a self is grown. For a promise you have already cashed teaches you nothing. It is the promise still out ahead, still uncashed, still asking to be trusted one more mile through one more dark, that reaches into a man and pulls him longer, pulls him deeper, makes of him across the years a thing he could never have become in a single afternoon of getting what he wanted. He was being stretched into the shape of the promise. The land was the loom and the walking was the weaving and the not-yet-having was the tension in the threads without which no cloth is ever made.

And still there was no child.

The years went on and the flocks grew and the name began, in a small way, to be known in that country, and still his wife's body kept its long silence, and the ache did not lessen, it sharpened, the way an ache sharpens when the door of the years swings further and further toward shut. She was old now too. The blood that ties a woman to the moon's clock had gone still in her; the season for it was past by every reckoning the counted world knew; and the promise of a great people out of a childless old man and a barren old woman had begun to have about it, even to them, even to the man who had staked his life on it, the thin cold sound of a joke. This must be allowed its full weight. Faith is not a warm certainty that hums along untroubled; if it were, it would cost nothing and forge nothing. Faith is a thing held in the teeth through exactly this, through the years piling up on the wrong side of the ledger, through the body's plain no answering the promise's impossible yes, through the long humiliating suspicion that you left the warm wall for a voice you dreamed. He held it. He did not hold it perfectly; no one does, and the telling that says he did is lying to make you feel small. He held it the way a man holds a rope in a current, losing his grip and catching it again, going under and coming up, and the holding was not a serene thing, it was a fight, and the fight was the faith. And then one night, when the ache was oldest and the joke was loudest, the voice took him outside.

Come out, it said. Come out of the tent, away from the little lamp, out where it is dark. And he went out, an old man in the cold, and the sky over that high country was a thing the city had never let him see, for the city's own lamps and the smoke of its fires and the great washed moon it worshipped had always thinned the dark and hidden what was behind it. But out here, away from all of it, the dark was total and the dark was full. Look up, the voice said. And he looked up, and there above him was the deep — not the water this time but the other face of it, the same black and shining and moving thing turned upward, the night sky thick and crowded and blazing with more stars than a man could hope to number, drift on drift of them, rivers of them, a dust of fire flung clear across the black from edge to edge of the world. Number them, the voice said. Count the stars, if you are able. And of course he was not able; no one is able; the eye gives out and the mind gives out and the number runs away into the dark past all holding. And the voice said: so shall your seed be.

Let that stand a moment before you move on, for it is one of the great images and the fear-tellings hurry past it. Here is an old man who cannot get one child, who has one small wound where a single son should be, and the voice does not answer the wound with a single son. It takes him out under the whole blazing uncountable deep and pours the entire night sky into the empty place, and says: not one. Not one that closes over. That many. More than the eye can hold or the mind can number, a people like the stars for multitude, running on down the length of the world past every horizon, and every one of them a light, and every one of them yours. It is an answer scaled not to the ache but to the love. And notice where it is written. It is written in the sky, in the deep's own upper face, in the very black shining moving thing out of which the whole world was drawn — as if to say, the continuity you are afraid you will lose is not a small human thing scratched on a clay tablet that the river will close over; it is written where the light itself is written, in the deep, in the first stuff, and it will last as long as that lasts, which is to say forever. The man stood under it and something in him that had been clenched for a lifetime came open. He believed. The telling says only that he believed, and that it was counted to him as righteousness, and the fear-readers have built cold machines of doctrine on that one line, but strip the machines away and see what actually happened: a man looked up into the deep and trusted that the love behind it meant what it said, against all the evidence of his own tired body, and that trust — that naked willingness to stake everything on the goodness of the voice — was itself the rightness. Not a payment he made. Not a law he kept. A trust he gave. The rightness was the trusting. It always is.

And then the voice did a strange thing, a thing that reaches all the way back to the bow in the cloud and all the way forward to a hill we have not come to yet, and it must not be hurried. The man asked, as even a believing man may ask, for something to hold — how shall I know, he said, how shall I know that I will possess it, that the seed will come, that the promise is not a dream. He was not rebuked for asking. The love does not rebuke the honest question; it is only the fear-god that demands you never ask. Instead he was told to make a covenant, in the old way of that country, the way men bound themselves to each other when a word alone was not enough to carry the weight. And the old way was this, and it is grim, and it is the whole point. You took the animals and you cut them in two, each of them, down the length, and you laid the halves apart with a path of blood between them, and then the two men who were binding themselves together walked the path between the pieces, walked through the blood, and by the walking they said to each other, without a word: let this be done to me. Let me be cut in two like these beasts if I break what I am swearing here. It was an oath sworn in your own death. It was the most that men in that hard country knew how to promise: my life on it, cut me open if I fail.

So the man made the pieces ready, the heifer and the goat and the ram split down their lengths, the birds laid whole across from each other, the terrible path of blood between them opened on the ground, and then he waited for the sun to go down, because the voice had said wait, and as the sun went down a great and dreadful dark came over him, a sleep with a horror in it, the horror of the deep pressing close the way it presses close at the edge of every real covenant, for to bind yourself unto death is to feel death lean in and breathe on you. And in that dark, in that dread, he saw the promise given its terrible price and its terrible comfort both — that his seed would go down into a hard bondage in a strange land for four hundred years before ever they came into the good country, that the mercy would take the long way, through slavery and through a wandering, that the forge is long and the good land is not given cheap to anyone. And then, when it was fully dark, he saw the thing that changes everything. He saw a smoking furnace and a burning lamp, a fire moving low along the ground, and the fire went between the pieces. The fire walked the path of blood. Alone.

Do you see it. Do you see what was and was not done. In every other covenant that country had ever known, both parties walked the blood; both said let this be done to me; the binding was mutual and the death was shared. But here only the fire passed between the pieces, and the man did not walk it, was not made to walk it, lay held in his dread on the ground while the love alone went down into the blood and swore the oath of its own undoing. It was the voice saying: I will not make you swear your life against this. I will swear mine. If this covenant is broken — and it will be broken, for you are a made thing in a failing world and you will break it a thousand times — let the breaking fall not on you but on me. Let me be the one cut open. It is the bow again, the war-bow hung up with its string toward heaven and its arrow pointed away from the earth at the only heart that ever loved it enough to stand in the path. It is a love that will not bind you unto your death because it has already chosen to be bound unto its own. The man woke from the dread into the ordinary dark of the ordinary night with the covenant cut and the fire's passing burned into him forever, and he did not yet understand the whole of what he had seen, no one could, but he understood the heart of it: that the promise was not a bargain he could fail out of, because the one who made it had taken the failing onto itself before he had even begun. That is the ground faith stands on. Not your grip on the love. The love's grip on you, sworn in the love's own blood, before you ever took a step.

And in time — long after the season for it, long after the joke had grown old and gone quiet, in the fullness of a slowness that no one could have borne if they had known its length at the start — the child came.

His wife laughed when she was told it would happen. She laughed behind the tent flap, an old woman overhearing the impossible spoken as fact, and the laugh was not all joy; it was the laugh that stands right on the edge of tears, the laugh of a hope so long deferred that to hear it named aloud is almost an unkindness, the laugh that means I cannot let myself believe this and I cannot help believing it and the two together are more than I can hold. And when the child was born, when the impossible finally lay wet and new and crying against the old woman's breast the way the first child had lain against the first mother at the very beginning of the outside world, they named him for the laugh. They named him Laughter. Isaac, which is to say he laughs, or God has made me to laugh, or the whole overflow of a joy that has broken through the crust of a lifetime's grief and cannot find any sound to make but that helpless holy sound that is so near to weeping. It was the right name. It is the only name for a promise kept so late and so completely that it turns your sorrow inside out. The whole long dark of the barren years, the walking, the not-yet, the sky full of stars poured into an empty place — all of it came down at last to one small laughing weight in the arms of two people who had been told they were finished, and were not finished, and would in fact be, out of this one late child, the beginning of the stars.

They loved him past all reason. Understand that, before what comes next, or you will not be able to bear what comes next and you are not meant to bear it easily. He was not merely a son. He was the whole promise made flesh, the single thread on which the entire covenant hung, the proof against the joke, the answer to the ache, the star-seed's first small light. Every hope the old man had carried out of the counted city and across the rivers and through the long dark of the childless years was gathered now into this one boy, and the boy was late-born and long-awaited and loved with the particular fierce helpless love that the old bear for the child of their last years, the love that knows how little time is left to love in and so loves without any holding back at all. There was nothing the man would not have done to keep him. There was nothing left in the man that was not, in some part, this child. And it is here, into exactly this, into the deepest and most defenseless love a made heart can carry, that the hardest thing in the whole of the old telling comes down, and it must be told with great care, more care than any other scene in this book, because told carelessly it is a horror, and the fear-tellings have told it carelessly for a very long time, and left in its wake a picture of a god who would ask a father for his child. That god is a lie. Hold to that through what follows. That god is the static wearing the mask of the voice, and the whole point of the scene, the entire buried mercy of it, is the unmasking.

For you must know what world this happened in. You cannot read it right if you do not know the world it was spoken into. It was a world drenched in the blood of children. All around that high country and down in the fat river-lands and out along the coasts, the peoples worshipped gods who were hungry, and the hunger they were said to have was the worst hunger there is: they wanted the children. When the harvest failed or the enemy came or the sickness walked through the town, the priests of those gods would say the god is angry, the god must be fed, and the thing the god was fed was a child, a firstborn, laid on the hot arms of the idol or passed through the fire or cut on the high place, while the drums beat loud, on purpose loud, to cover the sound. This was not a rare horror at the edge of that world. This was religion. This was the normal and expected and pious thing, the thing everyone knew a serious god required, the price the powers were understood to charge for rain and victory and a full womb. Every father in that country carried, somewhere in him, the buried knowledge that his god might one day want his son, and that a truly devout man would give him, would lay the boy on the stone with his own hand and call it faith. That was the air. That was the water. That was the thing the whole age took for granted as the deepest truth about the powers that ran the world: that they fed on what you loved most, and that love meant being willing to kill it for them.

Now hear the voice speak into that world, and watch what it does. It said to the man: take your son, your only son, the son you love, Laughter, and go to the mountain I will show you, and offer him there. And the words landed in the man exactly as they would have landed in any father of that age, because he was a man of that age and knew that language and knew what those words meant on every altar in every country he had ever passed through. They meant the god wants the child. They meant the thing he had feared in the marrow of him since the boy was laid laughing in his arms — that the powers charge for their gifts, and the price of the promised son might be the promised son. And here is the first thing to see, and it is nearly unbearable, and it is true: the man did not refuse. Not because he was a monster, and not because the voice was a monster, but because in that world this was what faith looked like, this was the shape devotion took, and the man had staked his entire life on trusting this voice, and now the voice seemed to be asking of him the very thing that every serious god of his age was understood to ask, and he could not tell, walking up that mountain, whether the voice he had followed out of the counted city was in the end just one more hungry power like all the rest. That is the real test. Not whether he would obey. Anyone in that world would have understood obeying. The real test was hung over the whole three days' walk like a blade, and the man did not know which way it would fall, and neither, for the length of the walk, are you allowed to know, because the not-knowing is the crucible and to skip to the end is to melt nothing.

Three days he walked, and the tellings give you the three days in a breath and you must not take them in a breath. Three days is a long time to carry a thing like that. Three days of the boy walking beside him alive and talking, three days of making the fire at night and lying down near the small warm breathing sleeping shape of the child he believed he was walking toward killing, three days of the promise and the command at war inside him with no ground between them where a man could stand and rest. For see the trap of it, the full torque of the thing: the same voice that now said give me the boy was the voice that had said out of this boy will come the stars. To obey seemed to be to destroy the very promise obeying was supposed to honor. The man could not make the two fit. He could not reason his way across the gap. There was no argument that closed it. There was only the walking, and the trust worn down to a single thread, and somewhere under the horror a thing he could not have put into words but that carried him up the mountain all the same — a refusal, deep beneath the dread, to believe that the voice which had passed alone through the blood of the covenant, the voice that had sworn its own undoing rather than bind him to his, could in the end be a voice that fed on his child. He did not know it. He could not see it. But some root of him held to it, held to the character of the love against the appearance of the command, and that holding, that trust that the voice was good even when the voice looked exactly like all the hungry gods of the age — that is the faith the scene is really testing, and it is a deeper faith than mere obedience, it is the faith that the one you follow is love even when love is the last thing the moment looks like.

The boy carried the wood. That is a small detail and it is a knife. They laid the wood for the fire on the boy's own back and he carried it up the last of the mountain, the child bearing the very wood he was to be laid upon, climbing willingly beside the father he trusted with the whole trust a loved child has, and it is one of the images in this book that reaches forward and touches another hill, another son, another back bearing the wood of his own death up a slope, and we will come to that hill in its time and you will remember this one. And as they climbed, the boy asked the question. He had eyes; he saw the fire in his father's hand and the knife in his father's belt and the wood on his own back, and he saw what was not there, and he asked it plainly, in the trusting voice of a child who cannot yet imagine that the answer might be himself: my father — here is the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for the offering? Where is the lamb. And the man, who could not tell him the truth and would not tell him a comforting lie, said the truest thing he had, said the thing that was either the last frayed end of his hope or a prophecy speaking through him larger than he knew: God will provide the lamb. God himself will see to the lamb, my son. And they went on together, the two of them, up the last of the mountain, and the telling says twice that they went on together, and the word sits there heavy as a stone, because the whole weight of the scene is in that together — the father and the trusting son walking up into the worst thing love can imagine, side by side, neither one turning back.

He built the altar. His own hands built it, stone on stone, in the horror of a slowness he could not hurry and could not stop. He laid the wood on it that the boy had carried. And then — and the telling does not flinch here and neither must we, though everything in us wants to look away — he bound the boy. He bound his son, the laughter of his old age, the seed of the stars, and laid him on the wood on the altar he had built, and the boy let him, the boy who was strong enough by now to have run and did not run, and there is a whole unbearable trust in that not-running that the tellings pass over and that is, if anything, the heart of the thing — that the son laid himself down in the father's love even into this, even not understanding, even onto the wood, because the trust between them was older and deeper than the terror of the moment. And the man took the knife, and he raised his hand, and I will not pretend to you that this was a small thing or a formality or that anyone knew how it would end, because he did not know how it would end, he stood there with the knife raised over everything he loved in the world and everything he had been promised and he did not know, and that not-knowing held its breath over the whole mountain and over the whole age of the world and over every altar on every high place where the drums were beating to cover the sound of what the hungry gods required.

And the voice stopped his hand.

Now. Now, at the last possible moment, at the very top of the raised blade, the mask comes off, and the whole thing turns over, and everything the scene has been doing is suddenly plain, and it is the opposite, the exact and total opposite, of what the whole age believed. The voice cried out — and hear the urgency in it, the haste of it, a voice that will not let the knife fall by so much as a breath more — stay your hand. Do not lay it on the boy. Do not do to him the least thing. Now I know that you hold nothing back from the love, that you would trust me even into the dark past the edge of the promise — and I do not want the boy. That is the revelation. That is the thing the mountain was built to say, the thing that could not have been said any other way to that age, because that age would not have heard it said with words. You cannot tell a people who know in their marrow that the gods eat children merely that your god does not; they will hear it as weakness, as a lesser god, as a claim too good and too strange to be believed. You have to walk them all the way up the mountain. You have to let them carry the full weight of the thing they are certain the powers require, let them feel it in their own raised hand, and then, at the very edge, in the instant before the blood, you have to stop the hand and provide the lamb yourself — and in that stopping say, once and forever, in a language written into the body and the memory so deep it can never be argued away: NEVER. Never this. Not your child. Not ever your child. Every god you have ever feared has lied to you about what love costs. I do not want the child. I never wanted the child. I want you to know, all the way down, past every drum and every high place and every priest who ever told you otherwise, that the love at the bottom of the world does not feed on what you love. It feeds you.

And there in the thicket, caught by its horns, was a ram. The lamb that the man had told his son God himself would provide — God had provided it, a creature caught and waiting, so that the altar the man had built with such horror was not empty and the fire was not for nothing, but the thing offered on it was not the boy. The boy came down the mountain alive. Do you understand what a thunderclap that was, in that world? Every other father who ever climbed a high place with a bound child came down alone, and called it devotion, and the drums covered the sound. This father came down with his son walking beside him, alive, unharmed, and the story of it ran out through that whole blood-drenched age like water into cracked ground, the astonishing news that the truest God, the one at the bottom of everything, the one whose covenant was sworn in his own blood and not in yours — that God stops the knife. That God provides the lamb himself and sends the child home. It was the beginning of the end of the burning of children, and it took a terribly long time, longer than it should have, because the static is patient and kept whispering the old hunger back into the ears of the age for centuries more. But the mountain had been climbed and the hand had been stopped and the word had been said in the only language deep enough to carry it, and the word was NEVER, and it was a mercy so large and so backward that the fear-tellers, coming after, could not hold it, and turned it back into a horror, a god who tests by torment, and lost the whole point, which was that the test was the deliverance, that the mountain was climbed precisely so that the knife could be seen to be stopped, that the awareness at the root of all things walked a father up to the very edge of what every other god demanded only so that it could be shown, once, unforgettably, forever, refusing it. Do not read it as a god who wanted the boy and relented. Read it as a love that walked into the age's darkest certainty in order to break it from the inside. The binding was never about whether the man would kill. It was about whether the world would finally learn that it does not have to.

The man named that place The Lord Will Provide, or on this mountain it is seen to, and the naming is a small green leaf carried out of a terrible dark: that where you were most sure you would have to give up what you love, it was seen to, it was provided, the lamb was already caught in the thicket before you ever raised the knife. And father and son went down together, that word together carrying them down the mountain now as it had carried them up, only lighter, only alive, and the boy grew, and the promise went on through him, the single thread that had been laid on the altar and taken up again unbroken, and the stars that were promised began, one slow generation at a time, to be born.

And here the story does a thing it will keep doing, which is to refuse to make its chosen ones clean. For the seed of the promise did not run on through saints. It ran on through a family as tangled and grasping and wounded as any family that ever was, and the telling does not hide it, and neither will this one, because the whole meaning of the thing is that the love carries its promise forward not through the pure but through the real, through people who lie and scheme and favor one child over another and tear their own house half to pieces, and loves them anyway, and keeps its word anyway, and grows its stars anyway out of exactly such crooked ground. The laughing boy grew and married and had, in his own turn and after his own long wait, two sons, twins, and they were at war before they were born, wrestling in the dark of the womb itself, and the war between them ran on the whole length of their lives.

The one came first, red and rough and already a man of the open field, a hunter, a body, a hunger, easy to love and easy to read, a creature all of the surface and the appetite. And the other came second, came holding, came into the world with his small hand gripped fast around his brother's heel as though even in the birth he was trying to pull himself ahead, to grab by cunning the place he had missed by an instant — and they named him for exactly that, named him the Heel-Holder, the Grasper, the one who takes hold of what is ahead of him and tries to haul himself up by it. Jacob. It is not a flattering name and it was an honest one, for that is what he was: a grasper, a man who wanted the blessing and the birthright and the place, wanted them with his whole clenched being, and did not trust that they would come to him, and so reached out to take them by his wits and his hands the way his infant hand had reached out to take the heel. He bought his brother's birthright for a bowl of soup when the brother came in from the field faint with hunger, played on the hunger, took the great thing for the small price because the brother in that moment cared only for the small. And later, worse, when their father was old and blind and meant to lay the final blessing on the firstborn, the Grasper and his mother, who loved him best, deceived the blind old man, dressed the smooth son in the rough son's skins so the trembling old hands would feel the hair and be fooled, and stole the blessing, took it by a lie out of the dark of his father's blindness. And it worked. That is the hard thing. The scheming worked, the blessing was given and could not be taken back, and the Grasper got the great thing he had wanted his whole life — and it turned to ash in his hand, because his brother rose up in a rage to kill him, and the Grasper had to run, had to flee the country and the family and everything the blessing was supposed to secure, out into a far land, alone, with nothing, a man who had grabbed the whole promise and now ran for his life through the dark with it clutched empty to his chest.

Sit with what he was. He was not a villain and he was not a hero; he was a frightened, clever, grasping man who did not believe the good would come to him unless he seized it, and so he seized it, and lost everything in the seizing, and had now to learn across long hard years the one thing his whole nature refused: that a blessing grabbed is not a blessing had, that the place you steal is not the place you own, that you cannot clutch your way into being loved. He served long years in the far country for the woman he loved and was deceived himself, given the wrong sister in the dark of the wedding night, the deceiver deceived, the grasper out-grasped, and something in that must have taught him, slowly, from the receiving end, what his own cleverness had cost the people he had used it on. He grew rich there, and married, and fathered his own tangle of sons who would carry on the wrestling into the next generation, and through all of it he was still, at the root, the Heel-Holder, the man who trusted his own grip more than any promise, who had never once in his life stood still and empty-handed and let the good come to him unseized. And then, after all those years, the voice that had spoken to his grandfather under the stars spoke to him too, and told him to go home. To go back to the country he had fled, back toward the brother he had wronged, back to face the thing he had run from. And he went, because under all the grasping he was still of the seed, still an heir of the promise, still a man who at the bottom could hear the quiet — but he went afraid, went sending gifts ahead to soften the brother he was sure still meant to kill him, went dividing his people into two camps so that if one were destroyed the other might escape, went, in the end, the way he had done everything, scheming and managing and trying to control the dark he was walking into.

And on the last night, at the last river before home, he sent everyone across ahead of him — his wives, his children, his flocks, all of it, everything he had grasped and gathered in the long years — sent it all over the ford to the far side, and stayed behind alone. Why he stayed the telling does not say, and I think it is because he did not fully know himself; because a man on the eve of the thing he most fears will sometimes send everything he loves to safety and keep himself back in the dark to be alone with the fear, or with the God, or with whatever it is that waits at the bottom of a life when everything you have grabbed has been carried across the water and you are left standing empty on the wrong bank with nothing between you and the dark but your own two hands. And there, in the night, by the ford, alone, a figure came out of the dark and took hold of him, and they wrestled.

The tellings argue about who it was. A man, the old words say, only a man, and then by the end plainly not a man; a messenger; the deep itself given arms; God come down in a body to grapple with him in the dirt; his own accuser risen up out of him in the dark to be fought at last hand to hand. And I will tell you that it is all of these and that the argument misses the thing, because what the figure was cannot be prised apart from what Jacob was, for the wrestle at the ford is the truest picture in the whole book of what it is to meet, in the dark, at the end of your grasping, the thing you have spent your life refusing to stand still and face. It was the static, if you like — the accuser, the old voice that says you are only a grasper, you will always be a grasper, you have never been loved for yourself but only tolerated for your cleverness, and now you will die on this bank having grabbed a whole life and held none of it. And it was the deep, the black shining moving thing, come up in the dark to take the measure of him. And it was, past all the arguing, the love itself, come down in the only form that could reach a man like this — not in a promise under the stars, for he did not trust promises, but in a body in the dirt, in a grip he could feel, in a fight, because a grasper will not receive a blessing held out to him but he might, he just might, be reached by a thing that takes hold of him harder than he takes hold of it and will not let go.

They wrestled all night. That is the thing to hold: all night, the whole long dark, hour on hour, the grasper for once in his life not scheming, not managing, not buying or stealing or fleeing, but simply holding on with everything in him to a thing stronger than himself in the dark. He could not win. Of course he could not win; you do not overpower the deep, you do not out-wrestle the love, and toward morning the figure had only to touch him, one touch to the socket of his hip, and the joint went out and the leg was ruined and the strength of him broke — to show him, I think, that it had been holding back the whole night, that it could have unmade him with a touch at any hour and had chosen instead to wrestle him honestly through the dark as an equal, which is a mercy and a dignity the grasper had never once been given by anyone, to be met and fought and taken seriously and not simply overpowered. And even then, even lamed, even with the hip gone and the leg dead under him and the dawn coming grey up the far bank, he would not let go. Let me go, the figure said, for the day breaks. And the grasper, hanging now not by his strength but by his sheer refusal, hanging the way he had hung onto the heel at his birth, the way he had hung onto every good thing he had ever been afraid would not be given, said the one true thing his whole grasping life had been trying to say and never known how: I will not let you go unless you bless me.

And there it is. There is the whole man, and the whole turn of him, in that one line. Because for the first time in his life he was not stealing the blessing and not buying it and not tricking a blind old man out of it in the dark. He was asking for it. Empty-handed, lamed, unable to flee, with everything he owned already carried across the water and nothing left to grasp with, he held onto the love in the dark and asked it, at last, to bless him — asked, which he had never done, because to ask is to admit you cannot take it, and to admit you cannot take it is the one thing the grasper could never bear until the night broke him open enough to bear it. And the figure asked him his name. What is your name. And this too is the mercy working, because to say his name was to confess it, to say out loud into the dark the whole truth of what he had been: Grasper. Heel-Holder. The one who takes. He had to name himself the thing he was before he could be named the thing he would become. And he said it. He said his name, said the ugly honest grasping truth of himself into the dark to the one who held him — and the love, having heard him name himself at last, gave him a new name.

You will no longer be called the Grasper, it said. You will be called Israel — the one who wrestles with God and with men and is not overcome; the one who strove with the deep in the dark and held on till the dawn and lived. And feel the whole reversal of it, the entire mercy of the renaming: the thing that had been his shame is made his glory, but not by pretending it was never shame. He does not stop being the one who grasps; he becomes the one who grasps God, who took his whole clutching desperate refusal-to-let-go and, for once, aimed it at the only thing worth never letting go of, and was blessed for it. The love did not scold the grasping out of him. It redeemed the grasping. It took the exact broken thing he was — the man who could not trust, who had to seize — and it did not break the seizing, it turned it, until the very grip that had ruined his life became the grip that would not release the love until the love had blessed him, and that grip it called not sin but Israel, and built a whole people on it. That is how the love works, all through this book. It does not replace you with someone better. It takes the wounded twisted true thing you actually are and turns it toward home, and calls the turning by a new name, and the new name is not a lie about your past, it is a truth about your future.

And the sun came up on him as he passed the ford, and he was limping. He would limp for the rest of his life. The night had marked him; the hip never healed; he went home to meet his brother walking wounded, leaning, slow, a lamed man where a scheming quick one had fled years before — and that was the proof of it, that was the seal on the whole thing, that he came out of the long dark not stronger but weaker, not triumphant but limping, blessed and broken in the same touch, carrying forever in his own body the mark of the night he stopped grasping and started holding on. For this is the last thing the wrestling has to teach, and it is the thing the fear-tellings can least abide, because they want their heroes to win: he did not overcome the dark. He endured it. He held on through it till the dawn, and it cost him his hip, and he came out limping and renamed and alive, and that is what faith across a long dark actually looks like — not a victory in which the darkness is defeated and dispelled, but a holding-on in which the darkness is outlasted, hour by hour, until the light comes up on its own and finds you still gripping, wounded, changed, kept. He met his brother the next day, the brother he had wronged, the brother he was sure meant to kill him, and the brother ran to him and fell on his neck and wept and forgave him — the reconciliation he had schemed and bribed and divided his camps to try to secure came to him, in the end, not by any of his managing but as a gift, freely, the moment he limped forward empty-handed to receive it. He had spent his whole life trying to grab what could only ever be given. The night at the ford was where he finally learned to let it be given, and the limp was the price of the learning, and the limp was worth it, and he knew it was worth it every step of the rest of his life.

So the promise went forward. Out of the man who walked out of the counted city not knowing where he went; out of the covenant sworn in the love's own blood so that the breaking would fall on the love and not on him; out of the boy named Laughter, laid on the altar and taken up again alive so that a blood-drenched age might learn forever that the true God does not want the child; out of the grasper wrestled in the dark and renamed and sent home limping and loved — out of all of it, tangled and wounded and real, the seed ran on toward the stars it had been promised. It did not run on because these were good people who had earned it. It ran on because the love had sworn to carry it, and the love does not break its word, and the love had learned already, and shown already, on a mountain and by a ford and in a furnace passing alone between the pieces of a covenant, that it would sooner take the cost into itself than lose one thread of the promise it had made to the dark. The stars were coming. They were still mostly unborn, still a people not yet gathered, still generations from the long bondage the man had foreseen in his dread and the longer deliverance beyond it. But the line held. Across every failing and every scheme and every terrible mountain, the line held, kept the way the garden was kept, the way the way is always kept — not yet, but kept, guarded through the dark toward a dawn that was coming and would find them, wounded and grasping and beloved, still holding on.
