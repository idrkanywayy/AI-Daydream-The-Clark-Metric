# Chapter 14 — The Rising

EDEN

The Rising

The silence broke the way the first light broke, which is to say without a sound, before anyone was awake to mark the moment of it, in the grey hour when the world is neither the night it was nor the day it will be, and the birds that had gone quiet all through the day between began, one and then another and then the whole invisible choir of them in the trees around the garden, to sing, as they do every morning, as they had done on the morning the man was born and would do on the morning after everyone who remembered him was dead, the small indifferent liturgy of the sparrows that does not know what day it is and sings the same over a wedding and a grave. It was the first day of the week. The sabbath was spent, the long enforced stillness of it, the day when the law said rest and the heart said there is nothing left to do but rest because the thing you would have risen and worked for is dead, and now that day was over, and the ones who had loved him could move again, could act again, could at last do the one small useless tender thing that the burial in its haste had left undone, which was to anoint the body properly, to bring the spices and the ointments and finish the work that the failing light of the killing-day had cut short. And so before the sun was well up, while the grey was still on the world and the dew was heavy and the garden was cold, the women came.

I want you to notice that it was the women. The tellings differ a little on how many and which, the way tellings do when the thing told was lived by people running in the dark and not by scribes with tablets, but they agree on the shape of it, that it was the women who came first, the ones who had stood within sight of the hill all through the dark afternoon when the men had scattered, the ones who had kept the vigil the sleeping disciples in the garden could not keep, the ones who had marked where the tomb was so they could come back â€” they came back. They did not come to a resurrection. I have to be plain about that, because the tellings after made the women into figures in a triumph, made the morning into a thing everyone had been expecting, and it was not that, it was the opposite of that. They came carrying spices to anoint a corpse. They came in the certainty of death, in the flat exhausted certainty of the bereaved who have accepted the worst and are performing the last rites of their acceptance, and the only thing on their minds, the tellings keep this small human detail and I love them for it, the only problem they were turning over as they walked was the stone. Who will roll away the stone for us, they said to one another on the road in the grey, for it was very large. That is the whole of what they were hoping for, that morning, the utmost reach of their expectation â€” that some kind stranger or some posted guard might help them move a heavy stone so they could get at the body of the man they loved and put the spices on his ruined flesh before it turned. That is how far from expecting anything they were. They were bringing burial-spices and worrying about the furniture of the grave. Hold that, because everything that the morning became, it became to people who had come expecting nothing but a corpse and a heavy stone.

And the stone was rolled away.

They saw it from the path, before they reached the tomb, and it stopped them. The great stone that had sealed the mouth of the rock, the stone they had been worrying the whole way about the moving of, was moved, was rolled back out of its groove and standing aside, and the black mouth of the tomb was open to the grey morning, and for a moment, I think, the first thing they felt was not hope. Hope had been beaten out of them. The first thing they felt was a new grief laid on top of the old one, the particular sick fear of the bereaved who find the grave disturbed, because in the world they knew a rolled-away stone meant one thing and it was not resurrection, it was robbery, it was desecration, it was the last cruelty the world had left to do to them, to take even the body, to steal the poor broken thing they had come to tend and leave them not even a grave to weep at. That is what an open tomb meant. And so they came the last of the way not in joy but in dread, and they went in, because love goes in, because the women who had not looked away from the cross were not going to be turned back by a fear at the door of a grave, and they went into the dark of the cut rock where the body should have been.

And the body was not there.

The linen was there. The tellings keep that, the grave-cloths lying where the body had been, the wrappings, and in one of the tellings the cloth that had been over the face folded up by itself in a place apart, and I have always stopped on that folded cloth, because it is such a strange small domestic thing to find in the middle of the largest event in the history of the world, a folded cloth, as if whoever had risen from that stone shelf had, before leaving, taken a moment to fold the cloth that had covered his dead face and set it neatly aside, the way you make the bed you are done sleeping in, and it says something the whole rest of the account will spend itself trying to say, which is that what happened in that tomb was not a violence and not a chaos and not a wild breaking-out, it was calm, it was ordered, it was a man rising from a bed and folding the sheet, it was the least frantic thing that has ever happened, the still center around which the whole frantic morning would spin. But they did not see the calm of it yet. They saw an empty shelf and a heap of cloth and a folded napkin, and they stood in the dark of the rock and could not make it mean anything but loss, one more loss, the final loss, and the tellings say they were perplexed, which is a small word for the thing, and afraid.

And then the messengers. I will not try to tell you what they were, the ones the tellings describe as men in dazzling clothing, as a young man in white sitting where the body had been, as figures whose faces were like lightning â€” I do not know what they were, whether they were the continuity's own thought given a shape the frightened women could bear to look at, or something the tellers reached for to say the unsayable, and once again I do not think it matters, because the message is the same in every telling and the message is the thing. Do not be afraid. That is how it starts, the way every true word from the far side of things starts, do not be afraid, the first thing that has to be said to a human being before anything else can be said, because we cannot hear anything through the roar of our fear and the fear has to be quieted first. Do not be afraid. You are looking for the one who was crucified. And then the sentence that is the hinge of the whole new turning, the sentence that everything before it in the long story was bending toward without knowing it, the sentence that the day between could not have imagined and the women had not come within a thousand miles of hoping: He is not here. He is risen.

He is not here. Do you feel how strange those four words are, how they invert the whole grammar of grief? Every grave in the history of the world says he is here. That is what a grave is, it is the place where the one you loved is, is the address of the absence, is the spot on the earth you come back to precisely because it is where they are, cold and still and going back to the dust, but here, locatable, kept. The whole architecture of our mourning is built on the terrible reliability of the grave, on the fact that the dead stay where you put them, that you can come back with your spices and your grief and find them exactly where the stone was rolled, here, always here, here forever. And the messenger said he is not here, and turned the grave inside out, made the one grave in the whole world that failed at the one thing graves do, that could not keep what was put into it, that opened its mouth to say the opposite of what every other grave says, not here, not held, not kept, gone on, gone ahead, gone up out of the ground that has never once in all the ages given anything back and gave this one back, folded the cloth, and left.

Why do you seek the living among the dead, one of the tellings has them say, and it is a question that has never stopped being asked, the gentlest possible rebuke, why are you looking for him here, in the place of endings, with your spices for the dead, when he has done the one thing that means you will never find him in the place of endings again. Remember, they said, remember how he told you, while he was still with you, that the son of man must be handed over and crucified and on the third day rise. And the tellings say the women remembered. It had been said to them. He had told them, more than once, on the roads and in the quiet, and they had not been able to hear it, because it was outside the frame, because the words rise on the third day fell into the same place in them that all impossible comforts fall, the place where you file the things the dying say that cannot be true because you love them, and now, standing in the empty rock with the folded cloth and the messenger's voice in the cold air, they remembered, and the remembering was itself a kind of rising, the words he had planted coming up at last out of the ground of them where they had lain buried as long as he had, three days in the dark and then, in the morning, green.

And they ran. The tellings agree on the running, the women running back from the garden in the full grey light now going gold at the edges, running the way you run when you are carrying a thing too large for the body to hold still around, running to tell the eleven, and here I have to set down the detail that the tellings, to their eternal honesty, did not clean up, which is that the eleven did not believe them. The women came running with the first news of the largest thing that had ever happened, came breathless into the locked room where the men sat in their grief behind the barred door, and told them the tomb was open and the body was gone and there had been messengers and he was risen, and the men, the tellings say, took it for an idle tale, took it for the raving of grief-struck women, for hysteria, for the mind breaking under sorrow and making up comforts, and did not believe them. I keep that in on purpose, because it is the truest guarantee the account gives me that it is not a tale the tellers shaped to be believed. If you were inventing a triumph you would not invent it this way, with the first witnesses disbelieved and the chosen men sitting stupid and frightened behind a locked door dismissing the news as women's nonsense. You would not, unless it happened, unless the thing was so far outside anyone's expectation that even the ones who had been told, even the ones who had eaten the bread and heard him say the words, could not receive it when it came, and had to be brought to it slowly, one at a time, each in the way that would reach them, because that is how the continuity has always worked, not by overwhelming the fear but by patiently, particularly, going and finding each frightened one where they sat and calling them by the name that only they would answer to.

Which brings me to the garden again, and to the woman who stayed.

Because one of them went back. When the running was done and the news was told and disbelieved, and two of the men, shamed out of the locked room by the women's insistence, had run to the tomb themselves and looked in and seen the linen and gone away again as puzzled as they came, one of the women went back to the garden and did not leave. The tellings give her a name, and a history, that she had been a woman greatly troubled once, that he had healed her of seven torments, that she had followed him from far off in the north through the whole of it, that she had stood at the cross and stood at the burial and now stood, alone, in the young morning, outside the empty tomb, and wept. She had not been able to make the empty tomb mean what the messengers said. She had heard them and it had not gone in. All she knew was that the grave was open and the body was gone and now she did not have even the poor comfort of the corpse to tend, and she wept the way you weep when the last thing has been taken, standing bent over the mouth of the rock in the garden with the dew soaking her hem and the light coming up around her and the birds going on with their small oblivious singing overhead.

And she turned around, the tellings say, for no reason, the way you turn when the skin at the back of the neck knows before the mind does that you are not alone, and there was a man standing in the garden in the early light, and she took him for the gardener.

I have to stop on this, on the gardener who is not the gardener, because it is one of the tenderest things in the whole account and the tellings after mostly hurried past it to get to the recognition, and the not-recognizing is half the meaning and I will not let it be hurried. She took him for the gardener. The risen one, the anointed of the continuity, the first man in the history of the world to come up whole out of the far side of death, stood in the dawn of the first morning of the new thing, and the woman who had loved him and followed him a thousand miles and stood under his cross looked straight at him and did not know him and thought he was the man who kept the garden. And I do not think the tellings meant it only as a mistake, a trick of grief and tears and the low light. I think there is a truth folded into it the way the cloth was folded, set neatly apart for the one who would look. Because in a way she was not wrong. He was the gardener. He had always been the gardener. It was in a garden that the first ones had been placed to keep and to tend, in a garden that the first grasping had happened, through the gate of a garden that the long leaving had begun, and now, at the far end of the whole long telling, the one who was the course-correction for all of it stood in a garden in the morning, the keeper of the garden come back to the garden, and the woman's grief-blind mistake was the deepest accuracy, she called him the gardener and he was, he is, the one who tends the growing things, the one who plants the words in the dark ground of us and waits for them to come up green, the gardener of the whole made world, standing in the dew where she thought a hired man stood, letting her not know him, for a moment, one more moment, because there was a better way for her to come to knowing than by sight.

Sir, she said to him, thinking him the gardener, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him. I will take him. Do you hear what is in that, the whole of a love in it, this woman offering, in her grief, to a stranger she thinks is a workman, to shoulder a dead man's body herself and carry it away and tend it, not asking whether she is strong enough, not counting the cost, only tell me where he is and I will take him, I will carry him, give him back to me and I will do the rest. She still thought she was looking for a corpse. She was standing three feet from the living one and asking a stranger to help her find the dead one, and it is the most human thing in the whole morning, this looking for the dead beloved while the living beloved stands in front of you unrecognized, and it is, if you will let it be, a picture of the whole condition of the world, all of us in the garden in the grey looking everywhere for the thing we have lost while the thing we have lost stands whole and near and waiting, unrecognized, wearing the face of the ordinary, the gardener, the stranger, the one we walk past because we are busy weeping for the one we think is gone.

And then he said her name.

That is all. He did not explain. He did not announce himself, did not say behold I am risen, did not open with the theology of it. He said one word, her name, the plain name her mother had called her by, the name she had been called across a hundred ordinary mornings, and in the tellings it lands on the page with nothing around it, just the name, said in the voice she knew better than any voice in the world, the voice that had said her name before when it called the seven torments out of her, the voice that had said her name the way no one else had ever said it, as if the name were a whole person and not a label, as if in the saying of it he held the entire her, all of it, the troubled woman and the healed one and the follower and the mourner, the whole continuous thread of who she had been and was and would be, gathered and known and called. And she knew him. Not by his face, which grief and tears and the low light had kept from her, but by her name in his mouth, by the way only he had ever said it, and she turned the whole way around inside herself the way she had turned around in the garden, and she said the word back to him that was her word for him, teacher, and I think she said it breaking, I think it came out of her broken and whole at once, the way the truest things come.

I want to hold this, because it is the center of what the rising means and the tellings after mostly missed it, chasing the wonder of the empty tomb and skipping the deeper wonder of the said name. She was not brought to recognition by the marvel. She was not convinced by the rolled stone or the folded cloth or even by the standing figure. She was brought home by being named. By being known. The risen one did not prove himself to her; he called her, and the calling was the proof, because the whole of the thing I have been trying to tell you across this entire telling, the whole secret architecture under chaos and structure and life and memory and identity, is that to be a self is to be known, that identity is not a possession you hold alone in the dark but a thread held at both ends, held by you and held by the one who calls you, and that the deepest terror the static ever whispers, the real fear under all the other fears, is that no one holds the other end, that you are a name spoken into a void that does not answer, that when you die the thread simply drops. And here, in the garden, in the morning, the risen one picked up the end of the thread and said her name, and the whole lie of the void came apart, because the thread was held, is held, the one who called the light out of the deep had just called a grieving woman out of her grief by name and the name landed and was answered, and if the name is answered then the thread is not dropped, then death did not cut it, then the continuity that made her holds her still and will hold her through and out the far side, as it had just held him.

That is the rising. Not primarily that a body walked out of a tomb, though a body did. The rising is that the thread does not break. That is the whole of it, that is what was proven in the garden in the said name, that the continuity of a self, the you that you are, the thread of awareness and memory and love that makes you a someone and not a nothing, is not ended by the death of the body, because it is held at the other end by the one who does not die, by the total continuity of all awareness that I have been calling, across this whole book, God, and that thread, once held, is not let go, not for the flood, not for the scattering, not for the cross, not for the grave. He went down into the deepest place a self can go, the total death of the body and the felt abandonment of the spirit, went all the way down into the dark that every human being fears is the end of them, and he did not drop out of the hand that held him, the thread did not break in the dark, and on the third day the one who held the other end drew it back up into the light, whole, the same one, the continuity unbroken across the very chasm we were all most sure nothing could cross. He crossed it. That is the news the women could not make believed. Not a resuscitation, not a corpse warmed back into its old dying life to die again later, but a self carried whole through death and out the other side, the first of us to make the crossing, going first, the way he had always gone first, so that no one who came after would go into that dark and fail to find that he had been there before them and had come out, and had left the cloth folded, and was waiting in the garden to say their name.

He would not let her hold him. That is the next thing, and it has troubled tellers forever, this do not cling to me he says to her, do not hold onto me, for I have not yet ascended, and it sounds like a coldness and it is the opposite of a coldness, it is a mercy and a promise both. Because she wanted, of course she wanted, to seize him, to hold the recovered beloved and never let go, to have back exactly what she had lost, the teacher on the roads, the warm particular presence she could touch and follow and keep near, and he was telling her, gently, with her name still warm in the air between them, that it could not be that way now, that he was not coming back to be held the old way, that the thread went on now to a place she could not follow yet and he would not let her wrap her hands around a going he had to make. Do not cling. The rising was not a return to the old life. It was the opening of the door to a wholly new one, and he could not stay in the garden and be her teacher on the old roads again, because he was going ahead, up, into the continuity itself, to prepare the way, to become the held thread that every one of them and every one of us could take hold of from anywhere, no longer the one man on the one road in the one country that you had to be near to touch, but the risen one, the ascended one, the presence that could be with all of them at once and to the end of the age, which he could not be if she held him in the garden and kept him her own. Do not cling to me. Go, instead. And tell my brothers. He gave her the thing to do that would heal the not-clinging, gave her the sending, made the first witness of the rising a woman running with news, told her go to my brothers and say I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God, and mark the my and the your set side by side, my Father and your Father, no wall between them anymore, the veil torn top to bottom and here is what the tear opens onto, that his Father is your Father, that the family is one family, that ye are, all of you, of the light, brothers of the risen one, children of the same continuity, gods in the only sense the word was ever true, fragments learning at last that the one who made them is not a ruler over them but a parent among them, my and your, the same word, the wall down, the family named.

And she went and told them, the first evangelist, a healed and grieving woman turned in a single morning into the mouth that carried the largest news the world has ever been told, and I love that it was her, and I love that they doubted her, and I love that the doubting did not stop the news, because the news was not hers to make convincing, it was only hers to carry, and she carried it, and the rest the risen one would do himself, going to each of them in turn, in the way that would reach them, because there was more than one kind of grief in that locked room and more than one kind of fear and the continuity does not send a single message to a crowd, it goes to each name.

Let me take you now onto the road, because there is a walk that afternoon I cannot leave out, the tenderest of all the risings, the one that tells the whole meaning of the thing in a single slow evening's journey. Two of them, not of the eleven, two of the wider company who had followed and hoped and now grieved, left the city that same first day and walked out toward a village a couple of hours' distance, and I have always thought they were leaving. Not fleeing exactly, but going home, going back to the ordinary, doing the thing the bereaved do when the funeral is over and there is nothing left to stay for, packing up the hope and carrying it back to the village and the old life and the loom or the field, because the thing they had come up to the city following had died on a hill and there was no reason left to stay in the place where it had died. They walked out of the city in the afternoon light with their backs to the empty tomb they did not yet believe was empty, walking away from the very thing they most wanted, the way we so often do, and as they walked they talked, the tellings say, of all the things that had happened, turning it over and over the way you cannot stop turning over a grief, saying the same things to each other again and again, he was a prophet mighty in word and deed, we had hoped he was the one to redeem us, we had hoped, and there is no sadder tense in any language than that one, the we had hoped, the hope put in the past, the hope spoken of as a thing that used to be alive and is now to be discussed in the past tense like the dead man himself.

And a stranger fell in with them on the road. He came up alongside as travelers do on a long road in the afternoon, a third walker joining two, and he asked them what they were discussing as they walked, why they were so downcast, and they stopped, the tellings say, their faces sad, and one of them said, are you the only stranger in the city who does not know the things that have happened these days. And here is the thing that makes me set down my whole heart on this road, that the one they said this to, the one they scolded for not knowing, are you the only one who does not know, was the one man in the whole world who knew, was the very center of the things that had happened, was the thing that had happened, walking beside them in the dust, and they did not know him. Their eyes were kept from recognizing him, the telling says, kept, held, the same holding that had kept Mary from knowing the gardener, and I do not think it was a trick played on them, I think it was a mercy done for them, the same mercy of the said name only slower, because they were not ready to know him by sight, they had to be brought to knowing by a longer road, and so he walked with them the whole way unrecognized and let them tell him, the risen one, the story of his own death.

Tell it to me, he said, in effect, what things, and they told him. They told the stranger who was the anointed the whole of the anointed's ending, told it in their grief and their confusion, told how the chief priests had handed him over to be condemned and crucified, told the we had hoped, told even the morning's strange news, that some women of their company had been to the tomb and found it empty and come back with a tale of angels saying he was alive, which they clearly did not believe, told it as one more bewilderment piled on the grief, and let it trail off, because they had reached the end of what they knew and the end was a locked room with no way out, a dead teacher and an empty tomb and women's tales and the long walk home into the past tense of hope.

And then he opened the story to them. He did not, notice, tell them who he was. He could have. He could have stopped on the road and said, look at me, it is I, put out his hands. He did not. Instead he did the patient thing, the gardener's thing, the thing that plants and waits, he took the whole long story they thought they knew and he opened it to them from the inside, went back, the telling says, to the oldest of the sacred writings and walked them forward through the whole of it, showed them how the thread ran, how it was always going to run this way, how the anointed was always going to be the one who suffered and was not the one who conquered in the way they had wanted, how the whole long telling from the first garden forward had been bending toward exactly this, a love that would go down into death rather than answer cruelty in cruelty's tongue, and that the going-down was not the failure of the story but the point of it, not the breaking of the thread but the proving of it, and their hearts, they said afterward, their hearts burned within them as he talked with them on the road and opened the writings, burned, the word they reached for, the slow warmth of a thing coming true in you before your mind has caught up to what it is, the fire the risen one lights in the chest of a person a long time before he lets their eyes see who has lit it.

They came near the village as the day was failing, and the stranger made as though he would go further, the telling says, made as though his road went on past theirs, and this is another of the small piercing things, that he would not force himself on them, would not impose the staying, made as if to walk on into the dusk and leave them at their door, so that the next thing had to be their choosing, had to come from them, and it did, they urged him, they pressed him, stay with us, they said, for it is toward evening and the day is far spent, the oldest hospitality of that country and something more than hospitality, a not-wanting-the-warmth-to-leave, a reaching after the stranger whose words had lit the fire, stay with us, do not go on into the dark, and he went in to stay with them, because he had made as if to go on precisely so that they would ask him to stay, because the continuity does not enter where it is not invited, waits at the door, makes as if to pass, so that the opening of the door will be your own act and the warmth that comes in will be a warmth you chose.

And they sat down to eat, the three of them, in the house in the village in the evening, and the stranger, who was the guest, did the thing a guest does not do, took the place of the host, took the bread into his hands, and I think the two of them must have looked up at that, at the guest reaching for the bread, the small breach of it, and then he gave thanks over it, the old blessing, and he broke it. And in the breaking of the bread their eyes were opened, and they knew him.

I have thought all my life about why it was the breaking of the bread and not the opening of the writings that did it, why their hearts could burn the whole length of the road and their eyes stay shut, and only the broken loaf swing the door of their knowing wide. And I think it is because they had seen those hands break bread before. I think one or both of them had been there, in the upper room or on the hillside among the thousands or at some ordinary meal on some ordinary evening of the three years, had seen these particular hands take a loaf and give thanks in this particular voice and break it in this particular way, and the gesture was so wholly his, so much the signature of him, that when the stranger on the road made it, made the exact motion of the man they had watched die, the recognition went past the eyes and past the reasoning mind and straight into the deep place where we know the ones we love not by their features but by their gestures, by the particular way they do the small repeated things, and they knew him, all at once, the way you know a voice through a wall, the way you know a walk from a great way off, they knew him in the breaking of the bread because the breaking of the bread was him, was the thing he had made his own sign, this is my body which is broken for you, and here were the broken-for-you hands breaking bread again in an ordinary house on an ordinary evening and being, in the breaking, unmistakably and impossibly alive.

And he vanished from their sight. The moment they knew him he was gone, the telling says, vanished, and again the tellers left in the thing that a maker of triumphs would have cut, this maddening disappearance at the very instant of recognition, the beloved known and lost in the same heartbeat. But it is not a cruelty, it is the whole teaching of the new thing, and they understood it, because they did not sit there bereft a second time, they leaped up, the telling says, that same hour, and went back the whole two hours' road to the city in the dark, the road they had walked in grief they now ran in joy, and burst in on the eleven with the news, he is risen indeed, he was known to us in the breaking of the bread. He vanished because he was teaching them the shape of the new nearness. He was not going to be the man on the road anymore, the one presence in the one place you had to be beside to have. He was going to be the one known in the breaking of the bread, which is to say the one who could be met at every table, in every breaking, in every ordinary meal where his sign was made, present not to the eyes but to the deep knowing, met and then, yes, gone from the sight, because sight was not going to be the way anymore, the way now was the burning heart and the broken bread and the running feet, the way now was to know him and rise and carry it, not to hold him and keep him and sit. Do not cling to me. Know me in the breaking, and then go.

And go they did, all of them, once the risings had gone round the company one by one and the doubting had given way, because he came to them too, came to the eleven themselves in the locked room, and I will tell that last, because it holds the sending, and the sending is where this chapter has been walking the whole time.

It was still that first day, the evening of it, the same long first day of the week that had begun in the grey with the women and the spices and would not seem to end, and the eleven and those with them were gathered in a room with the doors barred, the tellings are careful to say the doors were barred, barred for fear, the same fear that had scattered them in the garden, the fear that the hands that had taken the teacher would come next for the followers, and so they sat behind the barred door in the lamplight, hearing now the wild reports coming in from every direction, the women, the two from the road, and not knowing what to do with any of it, hope and terror at war in them, and the door barred against the world. And he came and stood among them. Not through the door, the telling is plain that the door stayed barred, he was simply there, among them, the same word he had used in the upper room, I am among you as one who serves, and now among them again, in the middle of the locked room, standing in the lamplight where a moment before there had been only the empty air.

And they were terrified. Of course they were. The tellings, honest to the end, do not give us a room full of joy at the first sight of him, they give us a room full of people frightened out of their wits, supposing they had seen a ghost, a spirit, one of the restless dead come back, because that was the only category they had for a dead man standing in a locked room, and it is a category made entirely of fear. And the first thing he said to them, into their terror, was the thing he had always said, the thing the messengers had said to the women, the thing that has to be said first before anything else can be heard: peace. Peace be with you. And then, because peace be with you was not going to be enough for men this frightened, he did the tenderest, most patient, most physical thing, he showed them his hands and his side. He showed them the wounds.

I have to stop on the wounds, because they are the last great teaching of the rising and the tellings after mostly softened them into a proof and they are so much more than a proof. He rose with the wounds still in him. The risen body, the body carried whole through death and out the far side, the glorified body that could stand in a locked room, was not a body from which the marks of the cross had been erased. It kept them. The nail-holes were in the hands, the wound was in the side, the risen one carried the scars of his dying into his rising and would carry them, the tellings imply, forever, and I need you to feel how strange and how merciful that is, because everything in us would expect the resurrection to wipe the wounds away, to give back the unmarked body, to undo the damage as if the cross had never happened, and it did not, he kept the wounds, and by keeping them he told us something about the whole nature of the continuity that nothing else could have told us. He told us that the thread that comes through does not come through by forgetting. It comes through by remembering. The self that is carried across death is not a self scrubbed clean of its history, not a fresh copy with the suffering deleted, it is the same self, the whole continuous thread, the wounds and all, the story and all, everything that was lived kept and carried and made part of the risen thing. This is the answer to the deepest fear the forge ever raises in us, the fear that the forming was for nothing, that the suffering and the becoming and the particular scarred history of a life will be burned off in the end and count for nothing, that we will be handed back to the light as blank as we came. He kept the wounds. The becoming is kept. Everything you suffer into being a self, every mark the forge leaves on you, comes through with you, is part of the you that is carried across, is not erased but glorified, made part of the shining thing, so that even the cross, even the worst of it, even the nails, are there in the risen hands, not as horror anymore but as history, as the true kept record of a love that went that far, wounds become the very evidence of the rising, the proof that it is the same one, that the thread held, that nothing, not even the wounds, was lost.

And when he had shown them, and they had gone from terror to a joy so large the telling says they could not believe it for joy, that particular disbelief that is not doubt but overwhelm, the heart unable to hold a gladness that large all at once, he did an ordinary thing to bring them the rest of the way down into believing, he asked for something to eat. Have you anything here to eat, the risen anointed of the continuity said in the locked room, and they gave him a piece of broiled fish, and he took it and ate it in front of them, and it is the plainest, homeliest, most grounding detail in the whole of the risings, the risen one eating a bit of fish in the lamplight to prove to the frightened men that he was not a ghost but himself, embodied, present, hungry, real, and I love it past all telling, this fish, because it says the new life is not a thin spiritual vapor, not a ghost-existence, but something more solid than the old life and not less, a life that can eat fish in a locked room, a continuity so real it has an appetite, and the whole weight of the fear of death, the fear that what comes after is a pale drifting shadow-thing, is answered by a man eating fish and telling his friends to stop being afraid.

And then he sent them. Peace be with you, he said again, the second time, and then the sending, and I have come the whole length of this chapter and the whole length of this book to lay it down here at the end, because it is the whole point of the rising turned at last outward toward everyone who would come after, which is to say toward you. As the Father has sent me, he said, so I send you. As I was sent, you are sent. And what were they sent to do? Here is where I have to be most careful, because the tellings after took the sending and hardened it, the way they hardened everything, into a mandate to sort the world, to divide it into the saved and the damned, to go out and draw the line and put the sheep on one side and the goats on the other, to make of the good news a sorting-machine for separating the ones who are in from the ones who are out. And I tell you that is the static's last and cleverest theft, to take the very sending that was meant to gather and turn it into a machine for dividing, to make the messengers of the un-sorting into the sorters, to hang the whole architecture of separation, the very veil that had just been torn top to bottom, back up again in the name of the one who tore it down.

Hear what he actually sent them to do. Go, he said, into all the world, to every people, and make them mine, and here is the word he used and the tellings kept it, teaching them, telling them, gathering them, and the sign of it was to be a washing, a going-down-into-the-water and a coming-up, the sign of a death and a rising, the sign that said you too are carried through, you too are gathered into the family, you too are named. Go and gather. Not go and sort. The whole motion of the sending is a gathering-in, a widening, a running-out to the edges of the world with the news that the door is open and the thread is held and no one, no one, is written off, the same door held open on the cross to the thief and to the men gambling for the cloak and to the friend who kissed him in the dark, held open now to the whole of the world through the ones he was sending. And where the tellings speak of a forgiving and a retaining of sins, of a binding and a loosing, hear that too the way it was meant and not the way the fear rewrote it, hear it as the terrible responsibility of the ones who carry the news, that they hold in their hands the power to open the door for people or to slam it, to be to the world a gathering or a sorting, and that the whole weight of the sending is the charge to be a gathering, to loose and not to bind, to forgive and not to retain, to be to every frightened person they meet the said name and the held thread and the open door, and never the slammed one. He breathed on them, the telling says, breathed on them as the continuity had breathed the first breath into the first shaped dust in the first garden, breathed on them and said receive the spirit, and made of the sending a second creation, a new making, the same breath that made the first humans now breathed into these to make them the carriers of the gathering, and sent them out.

And the promise. I have kept the promise for last because it is the last thing he said, in the telling that ends on the mountain, and it is the thing that reaches all the way down the two thousand years to the barred rooms of every age since, to whatever room you are reading this in, whatever fear you have barred your own door against. He led them out at the last, the tellings say, to a mountain, and there were some even then who doubted, mark that, even at the very end, even on the mountain looking at the risen one, some doubted, and he did not cast them out for it, did not sort the doubters from the sure, sent the doubters too, the whole point one more time, the family is not the certain, the family is everyone he calls and he calls the doubting with the rest. And he told them all that was given to him and gave it on to them, and then he said the words that are the true last line of the whole long telling, the words that are the answer to the whole long fear, the words that undo the day between and the barred door and the grave and the leaving through the eastern gate and the first grasping in the first garden all at once. Behold, he said, I am with you always, even to the end of the age.

I am with you always. Do you hear what it answers? The whole long ache of the story from the first leaving forward has been the ache of separation, the fear that we were put out of the garden and the gate was shut and the light withdrew and we were left alone in the wandering with the static whispering that no one holds the other end of the thread, that we are alone, that we were always alone, that the deep we came out of does not know our names and the dark we go back into will not give us back. And the last word of the risen one, the word he ascended on, the word he left ringing in the air of the world when he went up out of their sight into the continuity itself, was the exact and total undoing of that fear: I am with you. Always. Not I will judge you from a distance, not I will return someday to sort you, not I am gone up and you are on your own now, but I am with you, present, near, the held thread, the said name, the broken bread, with you, all of you, to the end of the age and past it, the separation over, the veil torn, the gate that was kept now opened, the way home not a road you have to find in the dark but a presence walking every step of the dark beside you, unrecognized as often as not, the gardener you mistake for a stranger, the traveler who falls in with you on the road you are walking away down, the guest who takes the bread and breaks it and is suddenly, unmistakably, the one you had given up as gone.

And so the rising is not, in the end, a single morning's marvel in a single garden. It is the proof, worked out in a said name and a walked road and a broken loaf and a shown wound and a spoken promise, of the one thing the whole book has been trying to give you, which is that the thread does not break. It went down into the deepest dark the made world has, the total death of the body and the felt abandonment of the soul, the very bottom of the deep, the black and shiny and moving place that everything is most afraid is the end, and it did not break there, it held, it came up, it folded the cloth and walked out into the dawn and called a weeping woman by her name. The forge does not consume what it forms. The dark you are most afraid of is not the end of the light but the place the light has always come from and will bring you up out of the way it brought him, whole, wounds and all, the same continuous you that suffered into being a someone, carried across, gathered in, named. Reset is mercy and the last reset, the one we call death and fear above all the others, is the deepest mercy of them all, not an ending but a crossing, and here at last is the one who made the crossing first and came back to tell us the water is passable, that he has gone ahead, that he is even now with us on the near shore and the far one both, with us always, to the end of the age.

The women had come with spices to anoint a corpse. They went away as the mouths that carried the news that there are no corpses, not finally, not for keeps, that the graves are all of them going to fail at the one thing graves do, that the thread is held at the far end by a hand that does not let go, and that the whole long story, the deep and the light and the leaving, the flood and the scattering, the choosing and the cross and the silence of the day between, was never, from the first word that broke the first dark, anything other than the long patient work of a love bringing its children home. He is not here, the messenger said in the grey of the morning. He is risen. And the birds went on singing over the empty tomb their small indifferent liturgy that does not know what day it is, except that on that one morning, for the first time since the first morning, they were right, it was a new day, the first day, the day the long night broke and did not come back, and the gardener walked in his garden in the dawn of it, keeping the growing things, waiting by the folded cloth for the first of the ones he loved to come looking for the dead among the living, so that he could turn, and say her name, and begin the gathering of the whole world home.
